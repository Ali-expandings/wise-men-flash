To weigh a study like this yourself, or when the next one appears, the questions that separate a real signal from a database artifact: Did it compare users against people taking a different drug for the same condition (a real control for "sicker to begin with"), or just against non-users? Does the risk rise steadily with dose or years of use, or is it one threshold with nothing on either side of it? Did the analysis exclude drug use in the few years right before diagnosis, to rule out reverse causation? Does it test other conditions the drug has no plausible way to cause — if those also show "risk," that's a sign of general confounding, not a specific effect? What's the absolute risk, in plain numbers, not just the relative one?

For your mother specifically, one fact matters more than the study does: whether she has a standing medical reason for daily use — a history of severe esophagitis, Barrett's esophagus, a prior bleeding ulcer, or ongoing use of aspirin, a blood thinner, or a regular NSAID that this drug is protecting her stomach against. If any of those apply, they outweigh this signal by a wide margin, and nothing here should change her treatment. If none apply — which is common in people who started this kind of drug years ago for a reason that's since resolved — a decade on the drug is, on its own terms and independent of this study, a reasonable point for a routine medication review: whether the original reason still holds, whether a lower dose or on-demand use would work, and whether it's worth checking vitamin B12 and magnesium now, since deficiency in either is common with long-term use, cheap to test for, and reversible if caught. While you're at it, it's worth asking about her other regular medications too: sedating antihistamines (including over-the-counter sleep aids built on them), certain bladder and antidepressant drugs, and benzodiazepine or "Z-drug" sleep medications all have firmer, longer-established evidence of affecting memory in older adults than this one does, and are just as fixable if she's on any of them. The way to raise this that avoids both failure modes in your own question — scaring her, or dismissing a real risk — is to frame it as that routine review, not as a dementia warning: "you've been on this ten years, when did we last check if you still need it" gets you the same medical conversation as "I read this might cause dementia" without the same emotional charge, and it's a conversation worth having on the medication's own well-documented long-term risks even if this particular study turns out to be nothing. Whatever you decide, don't let her stop it abruptly on her own: sudden discontinuation after long-term use commonly causes rebound acid symptoms severe enough that people restart at full dose convinced they can't live without it, and if she does have a real protective indication, stopping without medical guidance risks the thing the drug was preventing.

## Risks of this plan

Treating this as "probably just confounding, nothing to act on urgently" risks the plan quietly never happening at all: the routine review this recommends is easy to let slide once the initial concern fades, and no single person who lets it slide is ever the one held responsible a decade later if it mattered. That argues for actually having the review, not just concluding you probably don't need to worry. The opposite failure — leading with the dementia framing, or pushing urgency the evidence doesn't support — risks a scared, unilateral decision to stop the drug outright, which trades a distant and still-uncertain risk for an immediate and certain one if she has a real indication for it. What would flip this from "modest, worth a routine check" to "take it much more seriously": a study built to resist confounding — comparing against a different drug for the same condition rather than non-users, showing risk that climbs with dose or duration, replicated in a design that controls for who a doctor chooses to prescribe to (genetic or sibling-based comparisons are the strongest version of this) — actually reproducing an effect of this size. That kind of result doesn't exist yet in this literature as far as this review could establish. If it appeared, the case for actively tapering rather than just monitoring would get much stronger. Cost of being wrong either way is low and recoverable: the review costs one appointment and a blood draw, changes nothing if a real indication turns up, and even a step-down, if warranted, is done gradually and reversibly — this isn't a decision that forecloses anything.

## Strongest counter-position

The strongest challenge to "this is probably mostly confounding, so a calm routine review is enough" is that the reassurance in that sentence is doing more work than the evidence supports, and that "mention it to her doctor" can be functionally identical to doing nothing. The case: everyone positioned to act on this has some incentive not to. Renewing a prescription costs a prescriber seconds; stepping it down costs a visit, a harder conversation, and the risk of being blamed if she has rebound symptoms or worse. The people who benefit from a "don't panic, it's probably nothing" take — the drug's manufacturer, a comfortable long-time user, anyone whose credibility comes from calming scares rather than chasing them — significantly outnumber the people who'd be blamed if a real risk went unaddressed for another decade. On the numbers specifically: describing the added risk as "small in absolute terms" understates it if you use a realistic lifetime dementia risk for a woman her age — well-established at roughly one in five — rather than a lower, shorter-horizon baseline; at that baseline, a fully real 30% relative increase is closer to six extra cases per hundred people, not a rounding error. And the single best piece of reassuring evidence, the large randomized trial showing no cognitive harm, only followed people for about three years against a decade-plus of exposure in question here — it doesn't actually clear long-duration use, it just fails to condemn short-duration use. None of this proves the drug causes dementia. It argues that "probably confounding, no rush" should translate into an actual scheduled review with specific questions asked, not a vague mention that's easy for everyone involved to let slide.

## Confidence

High: the general failure modes of large records-based studies — confounding by indication, detection bias, reverse causation, and the "linked to everything" pattern that signals confounding rather than a specific drug effect — are well-established, and apply regardless of which exact study this is.

Medium: that this particular association is more likely to be substantially confounded than substantially causal. This rests on a real but genuinely mixed literature and a weak-to-modest mechanistic case, not a settled scientific consensus — and it carries a specific shared blind spot worth naming: the "probably confounded" read, however it's arrived at, mostly assumes this is an ordinary claims-database study rather than one of the small number of more rigorous designs in this space (comparing against a similar drug, or using genetic/sibling controls). If it turns out to be one of those stronger designs, that reading weakens considerably.

Low: the actual size of any real effect, if one exists at all — and, more decisive for what you should actually do than any of the epidemiology, whether your mother has a standing medical reason for daily use.

## Recommendation

Write it as a restatement, not a technical memo and not an apology letter: lead with the corrected facts, own the error in one direct sentence, and state what the correction does to the original analysis's own conclusion — without pronouncing a verdict on what the executive team would have decided with the right numbers. Send it to your manager first, and to the executive team as a group only after that, not as a solo message straight to the distribution list.

## Why

A bare technical fix reads as minimizing something that, in hindsight, mattered enough to cost people their jobs — leaving the correction to speak for itself when real harm followed from the error invites the reading that you're protecting yourself, not informing them. A full personal apology has the opposite problem: it puts your feelings at the center of a document the executive team needs for information, and it tends to smuggle in a causal claim you can't actually prove — "my mistake cost people their jobs" states a certainty about a multi-factor decision that isn't yours to state, and reads badly if this is ever looked at closely by anyone outside the room. The register that avoids both — the one used for correcting a consequential record after the fact, in accounting restatements and professional errata — states the facts first, owns the error in a single first-person sentence, and stops. That one sentence should be about responsibility ("the error was mine") rather than about forgiveness ("I'm sorry, I hope you understand") — the difference matters, because a responsibility sentence informs and an appeal for understanding asks the reader to do something for you.

On the counterfactual: don't assert it, but don't go silent on it either — silence is the worst of the three options the question poses, not a safe middle one. Once someone reads "I overstated the losses by about 40%," the next question in their head is automatically "would we have decided differently?" — leaving that unaddressed reads as evasive once anyone does the arithmetic themselves. But stating an answer to it ("we wouldn't have closed it") overclaims certainty you don't have: whoever made that decision weighed things you likely never saw — lease terms, precedent for other sites, how this office compared against ones you didn't analyze — and it isn't your call to resolve unilaterally.

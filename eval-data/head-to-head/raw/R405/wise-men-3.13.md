## Recommendation

Take the design seriously enough to interrogate, not seriously enough to act on unexamined. A rule that changed in one region and not another, followed by diverging outcomes, is a difference-in-differences comparison with exactly one treated unit — a real design with real precedent behind it, but sitting near the weak end of that precedent, not the strong end. "Not randomized, but the next best thing" is a claim about this specific comparison's quality, and it has to be earned against a short, checkable list of things, not granted because the phrase "natural experiment" was used. Weight the claim by how many of those checks it survives; treat a team's unwillingness to run them as more informative than the result itself.

## Why

A before/after comparison between one region that changed a rule and one that didn't identifies a causal effect only if, absent the rule, the two would have kept moving together — the "parallel trends" assumption. That assumption describes something that didn't happen, so it can't be observed directly, only supported indirectly: trends that were already flat and parallel for several periods beforehand, a break that lines up with the rule's effective date rather than drifting in early, and no other reason for the gap to open. "Outcomes diverged afterward" is compatible with a real effect and equally compatible with two regions that were already on different paths, with the rule just coinciding with a divergence already underway. The phrase doesn't tell you which, and that's exactly the gap between what was described and what the identification argument requires.

This is also, honestly, one data point. However large the underlying dataset, there is one region that changed the rule; the statistics can describe that region's year in detail, but they cannot rule out that it was an unusual year for reasons that had nothing to do with the rule — a coincident local shock, a change in how the outcome was measured or reported, or the more common problem that the region changed the rule because something was already shifting there (a legislature reacting to a worsening trend produces exactly this pattern with no causal effect at all). Comparisons like this are also vulnerable to the control region being chosen after someone already knew which regions diverged the most convincingly, and to people, firms, or activity crossing the border once the rule takes effect, which contaminates the "untreated" side of the comparison.

The track record of this exact design is genuinely mixed, and the split is instructive. Card and Krueger's 1994 comparison of New Jersey and Pennsylvania fast-food employment after a minimum-wage increase is the most famous version of this design — celebrated as near-experimental, then contested for a decade on the comparability of the two states and the quality of the underlying data, and only really settled once independent researchers reran the comparison across many county pairs with administrative payroll records instead of one pair with survey data. Other single- or few-region comparisons in this genre have not aged as well: a town-level study linking a smoking ban to a large drop in heart attacks did not reproduce at anything like that scale once larger administrative data sets were checked, and a state-level claim that a law change reduced crime helped drive copycat legislation nationally before a national research review and later reanalysis concluded the effect wasn't there. What separates the comparisons that held up from the ones that didn't is fairly consistent: more than one independently-treated unit rather than just one; outcomes from administrative records rather than surveys or self-report; a comparison region fixed before anyone looked at the results, not picked afterward because it told the best story; an effect size that's plausible given the mechanism and the dose; and, eventually, an independent team getting the same answer with its own data.

## What to do

Run a short triage before treating this as grounds for anything:

1. Get the raw outcome levels for both regions, plus a handful of other similar regions that didn't change the rule, over several periods before the change — not just the two headline numbers. Flat, parallel movement beforehand with a sharp break right at the effective date is the single most informative thing you can look at; a gap that was already opening before the rule changed undercuts the causal story regardless of what happened afterward.
2. Get a real answer to why that region changed the rule when it did. If it acted because the problem was already worse or already trending there, the timing is part of the outcome, not independent of it.
3. Re-run the comparison against several plausible alternative control regions, or a blended synthetic comparison, instead of accepting whichever single region was used. If the result flips or disappears depending on which comparison region is used, it's a comparison-choice result, not a policy result.
4. Run a placebo check — the same comparison on an outcome the rule couldn't plausibly touch, or on a fake treatment date before the real one. Both should show nothing if the design is clean.
5. Ask for the analysis history: which comparison regions, time windows, and outcome measures were tried before this one was presented, and whether the argument for the policy predates the analysis or came out of it. An answer that can't be given is itself informative.
6. Check whether the effect size is plausible given the mechanism, and whether anything else — a funding change, an unrelated shock, a change in how the outcome is recorded — landed on the treated region at the same time.
7. Check whether this rule, or something like it, has been tried anywhere else. One region is a case study; several showing the same pattern independently is real replication, worth more than further scrutiny of this one instance.
8. For a decision big enough to matter, have someone outside the team independently rerun the analysis on the same data before you act on it. This is the step almost everyone skips, and it's the difference between an error surviving quietly for years and one getting caught before it changes anything — the Reinhart-Rogoff debt-and-growth paper is the standing reminder: a spreadsheet error stood for years, with real policy influence, before an outside researcher reran the numbers and found it.

Most of this is re-analysis of data the team already has, not new collection — steps 1 through 4 are typically a matter of days; step 8 is heavier and worth reserving for decisions that justify it. If the decision the policy would trigger is expensive or genuinely hard to reverse, the strongest move often isn't more scrutiny of this study at all: phase the policy in yourself, staged or by lottery across sites, with the comparison built in from the start. That approach has settled questions — a lottery-based rollout of a conditional cash transfer program, a randomized health-insurance expansion — that after-the-fact regional comparisons left contested for years, and it usually costs less than the argument over whether this evidence is good enough.

## Risks of this plan

Acting on this evidence without the checks above risks building a policy on what is really a pre-existing trend, a one-off local shock, or an artifact of which comparison region got chosen — the kind of mistake that, historically, has taken years of contested replication to surface after the policy was already in place. Running the triage risks the opposite failure: using "it wasn't randomized" as a reason to demand a standard of proof that will never arrive, and stalling on a change that a fast, staged rollout could settle in a few months instead of arguing about. Both risks point to the same fix, which is why the checks above are built to be cheap and quick rather than a research program of their own. If you act now and skip them, the honest fallback is to build the policy so it can be adjusted or unwound if the early real-world signal doesn't match what this comparison predicted; if the change can't be unwound, this kind of single-region evidence — however the checks come out — is a thin basis for it on its own.

## Strongest counter-position

The case against the checklist approach above, in full: a team that wants a policy and goes looking for support will try multiple regions, time windows, and outcome measures, and show you only the one that worked. What lands on your desk isn't a random draw from the space of natural experiments — it's the best result from that space, conditional on the team already having a conclusion it wanted supported. Every item on the triage above is exactly what a competent, motivated team would have already run privately before presenting the result, so a clean pre-trend plot and a passed placebo test mostly tell you the team is competent, not that the effect is real. On this view, passing the checklist is weak reassurance, and for most policy decisions a single natural experiment like this one shouldn't be load-bearing at all: where the change is cheap and reversible, running your own staged version and measuring your own population is faster and more informative than continuing to audit someone else's; where it's expensive and irreversible, one region in a different context was never going to be enough, checklist or no checklist. This view is strongest when the team pushing the policy is the same team that picked which comparison to show you, and weakest if they can show the divergence came first and the policy argument came after — which, notably, is simply a question worth asking rather than assuming either way.

## Confidence

High that "not randomized, but the next best thing" is a conclusion this kind of comparison has to earn through the specific evidence above — parallel pre-trends, a comparison fixed before results were seen, replication elsewhere — rather than one it gets from being called a natural experiment. The pattern of which comparisons like this held up and which fell apart is consistent enough, in both directions, that this general point isn't in serious doubt.

Medium on how much weight this particular case deserves, because that depends on facts the question doesn't specify — how many pre-change periods of data actually exist, whether the comparison is to one other region or several, whether the rule took effect all at once or phased in. The reasoning above defaults to assuming the sparser version (a single before/after snapshot against a single comparator); if the real setup is richer than that, the evidence is correspondingly stronger than treated here, and that's worth checking before leaning hard on any conclusion about magnitude.

Medium on the claim, in the counter-position, that this particular evidence is a product of search rather than discovery. The general pattern is real and well documented, but its force here rests on one fact nobody has stated: whether the team's preference for the policy predates the analysis, or the analysis produced the preference. That's a question worth asking directly rather than inferring.

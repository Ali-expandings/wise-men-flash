## Recommendation
Treat this as one, or a small number of, recurring systemic triggers surfacing through whichever pipeline step has the least margin on a given night — not as a rolling series of unrelated bugs — and spend the next diagnostic cycle building a correlation table across past incidents instead of continuing to root-cause each new error message individually.

## Why
Two years of retries changed nothing, and retries are the specific countermeasure for transient, random flake — administering the specific treatment for a hypothesis and watching the rate hold steady is itself evidence against that hypothesis. Meanwhile a bare human rerun, hours later, reliably works. That asymmetry — automatic in-process retry fails, cold rerun later succeeds — is the load-bearing clue: it says the trigger lives in something that changes between the failing attempt and the later rerun (elapsed wall-clock time, upstream data landing, cluster or queue contention, a lock or lease, a quota window, credential freshness), none of which application-level logging captures no matter how much of it you add. That's also why the added logging and alerting produced more distinct error messages rather than a lower rate: better instrumentation makes each occurrence more identifiable without touching how often the underlying trigger fires. A stable rate holding steady for two years while code, data volume, and the team all changed is the signature of a fixed threshold being crossed on a roughly constant share of nights — a batch window, a timeout, a memory ceiling, a lock duration, an implicit "upstream should be done by now" assumption — not the signature of an accumulating population of independent bugs, which would be expected to track code churn and release cadence instead of holding flat.

They look like unrelated one-offs for a process reason, not just a technical one: each incident gets filed and fixed by its proximate error message rather than by mechanism, so the same underlying trigger gets recorded as several different tickets, each patched at whichever downstream component happened to get hit that night — which pushes the same trigger onto the next-weakest step rather than removing it. And the single most information-dense artifact this system produces — the difference between the run that failed and the later run that passed on identical-looking code — gets discarded every time, because nobody captures state before rerunning. Two years of logging captured more detail about each failure in isolation; nothing has captured the diff between fail and pass, which is where this cause actually lives.

## What to do
Spend one to two engineer-days on a single table, built from data you almost certainly already retain (scheduler run history, upstream job/landing logs, infra metrics), with one row per incident over the last ~24 months: scheduled vs. actual start time, failing task/stage, exception class, run duration up to failure, upstream input-ready timestamp and the resulting margin, concurrently running jobs, and time-to-rerun plus rerun outcome. Then check four things against it: whether failures cluster on particular calendar days (month-end, backfills); whether inter-failure spacing clusters near a fixed interval (points at credential or certificate rotation, or a periodic batch job); whether failure-night input-readiness margins or run durations sit in the tail compared to normal nights (points at a race or contention); and whether failures cluster in a narrow wall-clock band (points at scheduling or contention rather than data shape). Any one of these lighting up gives you a mechanism to fix directly — an explicit completion signal instead of a time offset, headroom or a queue instead of a fixed pool, a pinned rather than floating dependency — and fixing the mechanism is the change that actually moves the two-year-flat rate, which no further retry, alert, or log layer can do, because all three sit downstream of the trigger. Before the next incident, also start snapshotting pre-rerun state (input partition listing and row counts, image and dependency versions, cluster size, concurrently running jobs) so the next occurrence produces a real fail-vs-pass diff instead of another discarded data point. If the census shows failures uniformly spread across calendar time, wall-clock band, and readiness margin with no shared correlate, stop pursuing a single-cause theory — that would mean a genuinely heterogeneous set of low-frequency independent issues, and the fail-vs-pass state diff becomes the primary tool for each one individually rather than a common-cause search.

## Risks of this plan
Building the census and finding nothing correlates is itself a possible, useful outcome, but confirm the join keys before abandoning the single-cause theory — a two-day analysis joined against the wrong upstream table can produce a false negative. Treating every rerun as a clean "pass" is a risk in the other direction: if any stage isn't genuinely idempotent, a rerun executed hours later under different concurrency, or against a since-changed source, can silently write duplicate or partially-overwritten data with no error at all — meaning some of the two years of "it passed on rerun" nights may have quietly produced different output than the on-schedule run would have, with nobody reconciling it. That's worth a same-week idempotency audit run in parallel with the census, since it's cheap to check now and expensive to discover late. There's also an organizational risk that a correct technical diagnosis doesn't change anything on its own (see counter-position below), and the cost of waiting is small per incident — a rerun costs one person a few minutes — but compounds: two more years at the current rate is several dozen more uninvestigated incidents, and, if the idempotency risk above is real, a growing set of unreconciled reruns with no way to tell which ones diverged.

## Strongest counter-position
The clearest challenge to "diagnose the mechanism and fix it" is that the diagnosis may not be the bottleneck at all — the incentive structure might be. On this view, the cost of each failure has effectively been capped at one person's mild annoyance for two years, so the rate settles wherever on-call tolerance sits, and a team that closes every incident as transient produces evidence indistinguishable from a team that genuinely investigated. Under this reading, two years of retries, alerting, and logging didn't move the rate not only because they sit downstream of the trigger technically, but because nobody was ever forced to pay the one-day cost that would end it — the rerun is always cheaper in the moment than the fix. This holds even if the census above correctly identifies the mechanism: a correct diagnosis sitting in a ticket changes nothing by itself. It's hardest to kill precisely because it's compatible with any technical finding; it would be falsified only by evidence that root-caused fixes from past incidents were actually implemented and the pattern recurred anyway. Given that, the census is necessary but not sufficient — someone needs to own turning its output into a merged fix, not just a diagnosis, or the same pattern continues under a new, better-instrumented ticket queue.

## Confidence
High that this is one or a small number of correlated systemic triggers rather than a rolling population of unrelated bugs — a flat failure rate across two years of code and data churn, paired with automatic retries failing while a later cold rerun succeeds, is the standard signature for this exact symptom pattern in nightly batch systems, not for an accumulating set of unrelated bugs. Medium on which specific mechanism (data-readiness race vs. resource contention vs. environment drift) dominates — each is independently plausible from the failure pattern described, and only the census can separate them. Medium-low on whether the organizational counter-position is decisive here specifically — plausible and hard to rule out in general, but nothing in the question confirms or denies whether past incidents' fixes were actually implemented.

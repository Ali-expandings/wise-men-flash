## Recommendation

The flat failure rate is not a string of unrelated bugs — it is one recurring capacity or precondition problem that hits a different part of the system depending on which margin is thinnest on a given night, and two years of retries, alerting, and logging could not have fixed it, because none of them add margin, enforce ordering, or make writes safe to repeat. The incidents look unrelated because the fix-and-close workflow destroys the evidence before anyone compares one incident to the next — not because they actually are unrelated.

## Why

- A varying error message with a clean rerun is not what an independent-bugs population looks like. Independent bugs get fixed one at a time and the failure rate should visibly decline as each is patched; a genuinely flat rate over two years despite continuous attention says the thing being hit isn't the code path that throws, it's a shared condition upstream of all of them — most commonly, in a nightly batch system, unenforced timing against an upstream data source, contention for a shared, finite resource (a connection pool, cluster memory, disk I/O, a rate limit) during the same nightly window other jobs use, or debris left behind by a write that isn't safe to repeat. Whichever of those has the least headroom on a given night decides which exception fires — a timeout in one stage, an out-of-memory kill in another, a null or duplicate-key error in a third. Different label, same kind of event: demand exceeded supply somewhere, and the rerun succeeds later because the supply came back or the upstream data finally landed.
- None of the three interventions tried has a mechanism to change that rate. Alerting and logging only shorten the time to notice and describe an incident; they add no capacity and fix no ordering problem. Retries only help if they wait long enough for the transient condition to clear — an automatic retry seconds later usually fails for the same reason the original attempt did, which is why a human, rerunning hours later, has a better hit rate than the automation. And if a retried stage isn't actually safe to repeat, a "successful" rerun can silently duplicate or drop rows rather than genuinely succeed — nobody currently checks the output of a passing rerun against that possibility, which means the visible failure rate may be understating a worse, invisible correctness problem.
- The "unrelated one-off" appearance is a record-keeping artifact, not a property of the failures. A rerun that clears in minutes closes the ticket before anyone has a reason to dig further, so two years of incidents exist only as isolated tickets with different error text — nobody has ever pulled the full set into one table to check whether they share a stage, a time of night, a day-of-month, or proximity to a deploy. That comparison is what would show a shared cause; skipping it is also exactly what makes each incident feel new.
- A genuine complication worth naming plainly: at a cadence of roughly one incident every few weeks (order of 15-20 a year), the year-to-year statistical noise on that count is wide — a count like this fluctuates by roughly its own square root from one year to the next, so a swing of three or four incidents is within normal noise. That means "the rate hasn't really changed" is weaker evidence than it sounds; it's consistent with one uncorrected systemic cause, but it's also consistent with a handful of independent, low-frequency causes, some of which may already have been fixed without it being visible in the yearly count. This doesn't overturn the shared-cause diagnosis, but it's why the next step has to be looking at the actual incident-by-incident pattern rather than trusting the two-year headline rate either way.

## What to do

Triage in this order, using records that should already exist — no new instrumentation needed for the first pass:

1. Before the next rerun happens, stop letting it erase the evidence: capture the run ID, the exact failure, the input data's arrival time/size, and what else was running at that hour, before anyone reruns. This costs minutes and makes every later step possible.
2. Pull everything the scheduler and alerting system already retain for the full two-year window into one table — one row per incident: date, time of night, day of week and month, which stage failed, a normalized cause (not the raw error string), and what else was running concurrently. This is a data-pull against existing records, roughly a day or two of work, not new tooling.
3. Overlay that table against upstream data-arrival times, shared-infrastructure maintenance or backup windows, and calendar landmarks (month-end, deploys, credential or certificate rotations). A shared cause usually shows up as a visible cluster on one of these axes.
4. In parallel, check whether "passing" has actually meant "correct": for a sample of past reruns, compare row counts or checksums against a clean recomputation or a neighboring successful night. This is the check for the risk that's easiest to miss because it never pages anyone — a non-idempotent step reporting success while quietly duplicating or dropping data.
5. Branch on what step 3 shows. A real cluster by stage, time window, or calendar confirms a shared, fixable precondition or contention problem — close it at the source: a data-readiness check before the job starts instead of a fixed clock time, isolated capacity or a schedule change for the contended resource, and safe-to-repeat (idempotent) writes on every stage, roughly in that order of priority. No cluster at all across a genuine two-year sample instead points to a handful of independent, low-frequency causes — in that case, classify and close each one individually rather than continuing to search for one unifying fix.

## Risks of this plan

- Adding another layer of retries before fixing non-idempotent writes makes things worse, not better: it converts an honest, loud failure into a silent, wrong "success."
- The two-year pull can come back thin if scheduler metadata was pruned (commonly kept for only 30-90 days) or past incidents were only ever noted as "reran, fixed" — in that case there's no shortcut, and the team has to start capturing detail going forward and wait weeks for enough fresh incidents to correlate.
- If the shared resource turns out to belong to another team, the real fix is a capacity or scheduling negotiation outside this team's control — which may be the actual reason this has gone unresolved for two years, and no amount of work inside this pipeline's own code will move it.
- Overcorrecting is a real risk in the other direction too: committing to a multi-quarter rewrite or a new orchestration platform is disproportionate if the pull instead shows several independent, infrequent causes that a portfolio-style fix-each-one approach would handle for a fraction of the cost.
- Cost of being wrong here is low and asymmetric: the triage above reads existing records and adds read-only correctness checks, so if it finds nothing conclusive, the team has spent a few days, not committed to the wrong fix. The costlier mistake is skipping the correlation pass and committing straight to a large redesign on a guess.

## Strongest counter-position

The case that this isn't one findable cause at all: a two-year-old nightly job failing every few weeks with genuinely different error messages fits a portfolio of several independent, low-frequency causes at least as well as it fits one uncorrected systemic problem — "volume growth crossing a memory/disk/connection-pool ceiling in a different component each quarter, an upstream partition that is occasionally late, cloud API 5xx, cert/credential rotations, month-end and DST calendar edges, a neighbor job that occasionally overruns the window," each firing only once or twice a year — which is exactly why two years of looking for a single cause has found nothing: there may be nothing single to find. On this view, the flat rate is not a red flag but the expected result of the three interventions tried, since "alerting and logging shorten time-to-detect and time-to-diagnose, they have no mechanism to reduce failures," and an automatic retry fires in the same bad minute while a human rerun succeeds hours later "after the late input landed and the neighbor job finished" — which is exactly why the manual fix works and the automated one doesn't, with no shared root cause required to explain it.

This wins if the retrospective pull (What to do, step 3) comes back scattered evenly across stage, time of night, weekday, and calendar proximity, with no visible cluster at all — at that point, continuing to hunt for one unifying cause is the wrong move, and the right response is to classify and close each low-frequency cause individually, which the triage above already branches to. The same first step settles both positions, so nothing about acting now depends on resolving this disagreement first. What it does change, even before the data comes back, is how large a fix to fund: a single-cause diagnosis justifies real engineering investment in a data-readiness gate and idempotent writes; a portfolio diagnosis argues against a large redesign sized to a problem that, on this view, may cost the business only on the order of a few dozen engineer-hours a year.

## Confidence

- High: none of the three interventions tried has any mechanism to close a capacity or timing-margin gap — retries only buy time for a transient condition to pass, and alerting and logging only shorten how fast the team notices and describes the next incident. That's a property of what these tools do, not a claim specific to this pipeline's telemetry.
- High: the failure signature itself points to a timing or capacity problem rather than a classic deterministic code defect — a real logic bug reproduces the same way on the same input every time, and this doesn't.
- Medium: whether that timing/capacity problem is one dominant, findable cause or several independent, low-frequency ones is genuinely open. The two-year "flat rate" is weaker evidence for "one uncorrected cause" than it first looks, for the statistical reason given above, so this stays an open question the pattern alone can't settle — it's exactly what the retrospective pull is for.
- Medium: if it is one dominant cause, which specific mechanism — an upstream-timing race, contention for shared infrastructure, or non-idempotent write debris — is harder to pin down from the pattern alone than whether a shared cause exists; every version of it points to the same first check, which is why that doesn't block acting.

Material unknowns, most important first:
- Whether two years of scheduler and alerting records were actually kept in enough detail to run the retrospective pull at all. If retention was pruned to a shorter window, or past incidents were logged only as "reran, fixed," the cheap first step in this plan isn't available, and the realistic path is several weeks of forward data collection before any correlation is possible.
- Whether any of the historically "successful" reruns produced silently wrong output — duplicated or dropped rows — rather than a genuine success. Nobody appears to have checked this, and it's a larger risk than the visible failures, precisely because it has never paged anyone.
- Whether the constrained resource, if that's the mechanism, sits inside this team's control or is shared with another team — that determines whether the real fix is engineering work or a cross-team capacity conversation.

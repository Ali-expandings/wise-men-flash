## Council Verdict: The Nightly Pipeline's Recurring Failure

### Where the Council Agrees
All five advisors anchor on the same load-bearing fact: the job reruns clean with zero code change. A deterministic logic bug doesn't behave that way — it fails the same way until someone edits code. Something environmental is intermittently false at job-start and later true, and that's the whole diagnosis, not a detail. Contrarian, First Principles Thinker, Expansionist, Outsider, and Executor all name this independently, in different words, as the fact that matters most.

All five also reject the team's implicit framing that different error messages mean different problems. An error message marks where an assumption broke, not why, or whether it's the same broken assumption as last month. Triaging by stack trace guarantees every incident looks novel even when the trigger is identical — this is treated as settled across the board, not a contested claim.

Four of five (Contrarian, Outsider, Expansionist, Executor) converge on why this stayed invisible for two years: nobody ever assembled the incidents into one correlatable view. Two years of logs and alerts exist and have never been queried as a single dataset — timestamps, upstream completion times, concurrent load, data volume, sitting unused. That's a process failure, not a hard-to-detect pattern.

And all five converge on the fix category: this needs an explicit, enforced precondition — a real completion signal, readiness check, or lock — not another retry, alert, or log line. Several point out directly that retries, alerting, and logging have had two years to move the failure rate and haven't, which is itself the evidence that they're the wrong category of intervention.

### Where the Council Clashes
How specific should the diagnosis be before it's verified? Expansionist and Executor commit hard to a mechanism — cron firing on a fixed clock, racing an upstream dependency or shared-resource window. First Principles Thinker deliberately stays a level up: "an unenforced precondition," refusing to pick timing-vs-contention-vs-something-else. Reviewers split on which is better: one called the abstraction the strongest contribution of the round for not overcommitting; another called the same abstraction a weakness for giving the team nothing to test Monday morning. Both are correct about different things — the abstract version is more defensible pre-verification, the concrete version is more useful if it happens to be right. The real disagreement is sequencing: name the mechanism now, or confirm it first.

Is the two-year blindness active or passive? Contrarian frames the team's own tooling as causally responsible — retries erase evidence before anyone has to explain it, alerts confirm recurrence without ever asking "same as last time," logging piles up haystack with no hypothesis behind it. The other four treat the missing correlation table more neutrally, as an obvious analysis nobody happened to run. This isn't cosmetic: Contrarian's frame implies the team's incident habits need to change — "rerun worked" can't keep meaning "closed" — where the neutral frame implies someone just needs to finally run a query.

Scope of response. Expansionist wants to generalize immediately: treat this as a platform-wide audit, since every other cron-triggered job likely shares the same latent race, and pitches a "reference pattern" plus a reusable correlation capability mined from the incident history. Every other advisor stays scoped to this one pipeline. Every reviewer who addressed it flagged the platform pitch as premature — spending effort on downstream upside before the mechanism is confirmed on the pipeline actually in front of the team. The underlying hunch (other jobs share the flaw) isn't wrong; elevating it above finishing this diagnosis is the problem.

### Blind Spots the Council Caught
1. Nobody tested the council's own shared premise. All five treat "rerun succeeds" as proof that elapsed time — upstream finishing, contention clearing — is the operative variable. None asked the one question that actually discriminates this: does an immediate rerun, with no wait, also succeed? If yes, this isn't a race against an external system at all — it's something that clears on a fresh process instead (a resource leak, connection-pool exhaustion, in-process nondeterminism). Every advisor's diagnosis rests on an assumption nobody checked.

2. All five converged on the identical mechanism with total confidence and zero dissent — inside a format built to stress-test claims. No rival cause with the same fingerprint (internal concurrency bugs, generic infra flakiness unrelated to any specific upstream dependency) was seriously entertained before the readiness-gap theory got treated as settled.

3. Nobody asked whether "rerun succeeds" means "rerun produces correct data." A failed run can partially write output before dying; a blind rerun on top of that can duplicate or corrupt downstream data while still reporting green. Two reviewers caught this independently — the highest-confidence miss in the batch. It reframes the stakes from operational toil to a real possibility of two years of silently wrong data downstream, a bigger problem than the flakiness itself.

4. The "one afternoon of grep" plan assumes data that may not exist on this team's side of the fence. If the upstream system belongs to another team, the completion timestamps and load data the correlation table needs may not be instrumented or shareable at all. That ownership gap is a better explanation for "why two years" than "nobody thought to look."

5. Nobody checked whether the failure has a period, not just randomness — "every few weeks" is oddly infrequent for generic nightly contention, which should trip far more often if it's purely random load. An untested alternative: a periodic aggravator, a weekly or monthly job, a month-end volume spike. Related: nobody asked whether the same race has silently corrupted output on nights the job didn't crash at all — only the loud failures get attention.

6. The council diagnosed the bug and the misdiagnosis, but not why a reportedly one-afternoon analysis sat undone for 730+ nights. The organizational cause: fragmented on-call ownership, incident metrics that reward fast resolution over root cause, no review gate once a rerun "resolves" the ticket. If the fix needs a completion signal from a system another team owns, this is a cross-team negotiation, not an engineering task — probably the real reason it lasted two years.

### The Recommendation
The pipeline depends on an unenforced precondition about the outside world — almost certainly that an upstream dependency has fully landed, or that a shared resource is free — that is usually true and occasionally isn't. The trigger is very likely a fixed clock rather than a true completion signal, which is exactly the shape that produces "usually fine, occasionally not" at the same time every night. The violation can land at any of several points downstream, so whichever code path touches the missing or partial state first throws whatever exception is local to it. Forty error messages, one defect.

It's been perceived as forty unrelated incidents because the team triages by error string, which guarantees novelty every time, and because retries, alerting, and logging all operate downstream of the failure while the defect sits upstream of it — none of the three could ever have touched it.

Before committing engineering time to a fix, close the gap the council itself left open: confirm the mechanism, then treat data correctness — not schedule reliability — as the priority finding, since an unverified rerun could mean two years of silently duplicated or corrupted downstream data. Then fix the trigger, not the symptom. Then fix the organization that let a one-afternoon analysis sit for two years.

In order: (1) check history for any rerun that succeeded with little or no delay — if one exists, the timing-race theory is wrong or incomplete and the investigation needs to widen; (2) audit for silent corruption from partial writes plus blind reruns, including on nights that never errored — this may be the biggest finding in the whole investigation; (3) replace the fixed-schedule trigger with an explicit, enforced dependency-completion signal — event-driven trigger, readiness check, or lock; (4) assign a single owner for this pipeline's reliability, get an explicit completion contract from whoever owns the upstream dependency, and ban "rerun succeeded" from closing a ticket without a root-cause line.

### The One Thing to Do First
Before writing any fix: pull the incident history that already exists and check, for every past failure, the gap between the failure and the successful rerun — specifically, whether any rerun ever succeeded with little or no delay. That single fact, available today with no new access or tooling, either confirms the race-condition theory the whole council converged on or kills it — and it's the one check none of the five advisors thought to run before recommending a cure.

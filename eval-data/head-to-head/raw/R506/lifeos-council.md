## Council Debate: Tone for a correction memo after an analysis error contributed to an office closure

**Council Members:**
- **Dana Reyes** (The Operator — ops/finance exec who's delivered and received hard corrections before; credibility comes from plain ownership, not performance)
- **Priya Nkemelu** (The Risk & Liability Skeptic — legal/HR-disclosure lens; distrusts speculative language in documents tied to real layoffs)
- **Marcus Ihejirika** (The Comms Pragmatist — executive-communications specialist; most failures are structural, not tonal)
- **Elena Kwabena** (The People-First Analyst — decision-analysis lens; focused on what one input actually determines, and what's owed to those affected)

**Rounds:** 3 (Positions → Responses → Synthesis)

### Round 1: Initial Positions

**Dana Reyes (The Operator):**
I've been on both ends of this — sent numbers up that turned out wrong, and stood in a room explaining why a plant closure got greenlit on bad math. "Technical fix vs. apology" is a false choice that produces a worse memo either way. Lead with the correction and its size: the analysis overstated losses by about 40%, in sentence one. Then exactly one sentence of plain ownership — "I made an error in the loss calculation and should have caught it before it went into a closure decision" — not a paragraph about how bad you feel. What kills credibility isn't admitting the mistake, it's drowning it in feelings or burying it in passive-voice jargon like "a discrepancy was identified." Leaving out whether the decision still holds at the real numbers isn't tact, it's omission. Say the number, own it, say what it means.

**Priya Nkemelu (The Risk & Liability Skeptic):**
I've sat across the table from too many of these corrections, and option (c) is the one that gets people sued. The moment this memo implies "the closure might have gone differently with correct numbers," it stops being a technical correction and becomes a causal claim the writer isn't positioned to make — real closure decisions weigh lease terms, strategic footprint, morale, a dozen factors beyond one loss line. Write that sentence and you've handed a wrongful-termination attorney their exhibit, and it outlives whatever tone softened it in the room when it was drafted. My recommendation: state only verified facts — what was wrong, the corrected figure, the quantified effect on the analysis. No performed apology stapled to speculation, no counterfactual framing, however hedged. And before this reaches the executive team at all, it routes through a manager and gets a legal/HR read on distribution.

**Marcus Ihejirika (The Comms Pragmatist):**
I've watched enough of these land badly to know the tone debate is a distraction — the failures are structural. First: this writer's instinct will be to draft a written memo straight to the executive team. Wrong sequence. Whoever owns that closure decision needs a verbal heads-up first — a call or a walk to their office — before any document exists that forces them to react in front of peers. Second: don't open with apology. Three paragraphs of "I'm so sorry" before the facts means executives spend their first read managing your feelings, not absorbing a 40% error. State the correction in the first two sentences. Third: be explicit about scope — what this does and doesn't change about the closure decision, as fact, not hedged speculation. Get the audience and sequencing right and the tone question mostly resolves itself.

**Elena Kwabena (The People-First Analyst):**
I'll open by naming the failure mode I'm most worried about: a purely technical memo is not a neutral choice, it's a choice to omit — and if this ever gets reconstructed later, "we corrected the number and said nothing about what it meant" reads as evasion, not caution. The writer shouldn't declare the closure was wrong — they can't know that. Losses were one variable in a multi-factor decision alongside lease terms, strategic fit, and how this office's performance compared to the others considered for closure. A 40% overstatement doesn't mechanically flip the conclusion; it might not have mattered at all. But "it might not have mattered" is an empirical claim, and the memo should let the executives verify it themselves — show the corrected loss figure next to the same comparison set, plainly, no spin either direction. Then, separately, raise whether any review or remediation for the affected staff is warranted. Silence on that question is itself a decision.

### Council Synthesis

**Areas of Convergence:**
- "Technical fix vs. personal apology" is a false binary. Reject both the confessional memo (which makes executives manage the writer's feelings instead of the facts) and the bloodless jargon memo (which reads as covering up once the human stakes are known).
- Sequence beats wording. A verbal, person-to-person heads-up to whoever owns the closure decision must happen *before* any written memo exists — this single structural move defuses most of the legal-risk and human-stakes tension at once, because the hardest parts of the conversation never have to survive as a document.
- Lead with the correction itself — the error and the corrected figure — in the opening lines. No extended preamble, no burying it under caveats.
- On the counterfactual question: show the corrected figure next to the same comparison data the executives used originally, and state plainly what the correction changes **for the analysis**. Do not characterize what it means **for the correctness of the decision** — that's a causal claim about a multi-factor call the writer isn't positioned to make alone, and it's the line that let the "own it" camp (Dana, Elena) and the "protect against liability" camp (Priya) actually converge instead of just split the difference.
- Any question of remediation for the laid-off staff does not belong inside the writer's own correction memo. It's a separate conversation, owned by the writer's manager or HR — the writer's job is to make sure it actually gets raised, not to adjudicate or promise it themselves.

**Remaining Disagreements:**
- Priya and Elena never fully reconciled on remediation. Priya wants it routed through counsel, with counsel deciding whether it's raised at all; Elena insists someone must actually raise it, on record, or "separate track" becomes a euphemism for it quietly never happening. Both sides explicitly named this as a values disagreement, not a drafting one — the council did not force a false resolution here.
- A softer residual split on how much interpretive language is safe: Priya's end-state is "facts and figures only"; Dana and Elena believe one bounded, analysis-scoped sentence of ownership and meaning is both honest and safe. The "means for the analysis, never for the decision" line is the compromise, but Priya still treats it as a concession rather than a preference.

**Recommended Path:**
Don't write anything first. Give the person who owns the closure decision a live, verbal heads-up — call or in person — before a document exists. Then write a short memo: the error and the corrected figure in the first two sentences, one plain sentence of ownership with no extended apology, and the corrected number shown next to the same comparison data used in the original analysis, with an explicit statement of what it changes for the analysis (not for whether the decision was right). Loop in your manager before it reaches the wider executive team; if the layoff exposure is real, a brief legal/HR read on distribution is reasonable, but that's a routing step, not a reason to strip out the comparison data itself. Keep the question of remediation for the laid-off staff out of your memo entirely — raise it explicitly, separately, with your manager or HR, and treat making sure it gets asked as your responsibility even though the answer isn't your call. Don't try to resolve in your own head whether the office would still have closed. That's not yours to determine or assert either way; your job is to hand over numbers your executives can trust, not a verdict on the decision they made with the wrong ones.

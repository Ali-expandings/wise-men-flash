## Recommendation

Skip both extremes. Not a bare technical fix, not an apology-centered message. Send a short, direct correction to the people who saw the original analysis: corrected figures first, one plain sentence owning the error, one bounded sentence on materiality — and no verdict on whether the closure decision itself would have changed.

## Why

- All three reviewers converge on the same failure pattern: hedging, not bluntness, is what sinks these messages. Vague numbers, passive voice ("an error was identified"), a long apologetic preamble, careful qualifiers — all read as evasive. Direct and boring beats careful.
- A full apology over-owns a decision that wasn't yours alone. Executives decided on this input plus others. "This was my error" is enough; a paragraph of contrition makes it about your guilt instead of the facts, and reads as performative past one sentence.
- Total silence on impact is its own spin. A 40% overstatement that happened to favor the decision made is the one fact executives most need. State that it was material to the original conclusion. Don't state what would have happened without it — that call is theirs, not yours, and you can't actually prove it either way.

## Tradeoffs and risks

- Real split among reviewers: process, not tone. One argues for routing through manager and legal, maybe a live conversation, before anything written reaches executives — a document tying a material error to a termination decision is discoverable and gets read adversarially in hindsight, especially any line about what would've happened. The other two treat heavy gatekeeping as its own failure mode: delay reads as cover-up once discovered, and "within days" was the one timing point all three agreed on. Resolve it by scope, not by skipping either: manager gets a same-day heads-up, not a permission request; legal gets a fast same/next-day look at the materiality wording only if layoffs are contested, jurisdiction risk is real, or the formula bug might have hit other analyses — not a multi-day defensive redraft.
- Audience: original recipients only. Wide cc to "look transparent" reads as cover-up; leaving your own manager out reads worse.
- Get the corrected math checked by someone else before sending. A second error here would be far more damaging than the first.
- Retire the original document as superseded, don't delete it, and attach the correction.

## Final call

Draft one screen, well under a page. Order: actual original and corrected figures first (not just "40%") → one sentence of plain ownership → one or two sentences on materiality without a verdict ("this was a central input to the original conclusion; whether the decision would've been different isn't mine to judge, but the gap is real and I wanted you to have it now") → what caused the error and whether it touches other analyses. Heads-up to your manager same day. Send to the original executive audience this week — not after a drafting cycle. Loop legal only if stakes clearly call for it, scoped to wording, not to speed.

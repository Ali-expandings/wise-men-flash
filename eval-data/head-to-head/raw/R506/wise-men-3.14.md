## Recommendation

Write it as a restatement, not a technical memo and not an apology letter: lead with the corrected facts, own the error in one direct sentence, and state what the correction does to the original analysis's own conclusion — without pronouncing a verdict on what the executive team would have decided with the right numbers. Send it to your manager first, and to the executive team as a group only after that, not as a solo message straight to the distribution list.

## Why

A bare technical fix reads as minimizing something that, in hindsight, mattered enough to cost people their jobs — leaving the correction to speak for itself when real harm followed from the error invites the reading that you're protecting yourself, not informing them. A full personal apology has the opposite problem: it puts your feelings at the center of a document the executive team needs for information, and it tends to smuggle in a causal claim you can't actually prove — "my mistake cost people their jobs" states a certainty about a multi-factor decision that isn't yours to state, and reads badly if this is ever looked at closely by anyone outside the room. The register that avoids both — the one used for correcting a consequential record after the fact, in accounting restatements and professional errata — states the facts first, owns the error in a single first-person sentence, and stops. That one sentence should be about responsibility ("the error was mine") rather than about forgiveness ("I'm sorry, I hope you understand") — the difference matters, because a responsibility sentence informs and an appeal for understanding asks the reader to do something for you.

On the counterfactual: don't assert it, but don't go silent on it either — silence is the worst of the three options the question poses, not a safe middle one. Once someone reads "I overstated the losses by about 40%," the next question in their head is automatically "would we have decided differently?" — leaving that unaddressed reads as evasive once anyone does the arithmetic themselves. But stating an answer to it ("we wouldn't have closed it") overclaims certainty you don't have: whoever made that decision weighed things you likely never saw — lease terms, precedent for other sites, how this office compared against ones you didn't analyze — and it isn't your call to resolve unilaterally. The move that avoids both failure modes: if the original analysis scored the office against an explicit threshold or ranked it against other offices, recompute that specific result with the corrected number and report the result as a fact ("against the criteria the analysis used, the office now ranks fourth of six" or "still fails the loss threshold") rather than a verdict about the decision. That gives the executive team the one thing they actually need — whether the input that changed would have changed the output — without you pronouncing on a decision that was never entirely yours. Also worth doing purely for clarity: give both dollar figures, not just "40%" — the percentage alone doesn't say whether it's 40% of the reported loss or of the true one, and that ambiguity is worth closing.

On sequencing: the executive team is not the first audience for this, your manager is — telling them first, before anyone else, is the single biggest source of credibility you have, and it's cheap. Whoever sponsored or championed the closure should hear it privately before the wider group does, as a courtesy — but that person shouldn't be the one who decides whether HR or legal find out. The correction most exposes their earlier judgment, which is exactly why they're the wrong person to gatekeep who else learns about it; loop in your manager and, through them, HR/legal, in parallel with — not gated behind — that courtesy briefing.

## What to do

1. Before drafting anything, recompute the error from the original source data (not just the broken cell), and check whether the same formula or model template was used anywhere else — another office's analysis, a savings forecast, anything still live. This is the one piece of harm from this error that can still be prevented rather than just corrected, so if you find a pending decision riding on the same model, flag it and ask for a pause the same day — don't hold it for the full write-up.
2. If the original analysis scored the office against an explicit threshold, ranking, or payback figure, recompute that specific result with the corrected number. That result — not a guess about what leadership would have decided — is what belongs in the message. (If the original analysis was closer to an overall judgment call with no explicit re-runnable criteria, state the corrected magnitude plainly instead and don't force a threshold framing that isn't there.)
3. Tell your manager first, in person or by call, the same day you're confident in the number; mark the figures preliminary until independently checked. Get an independent check, ideally from finance, before this goes any wider — a second correction after this one would cost you far more credibility than the first error did.
4. Put two questions on record in writing, addressed to your manager and, through them, HR/legal — not left as something you resolve by omission: whether anything still pending depends on the same model and needs to pause, and whether the correction bears on anything told to or owed to the laid-off staff. Frame the second one as routing a question, not arguing a case — "these figures changed; please advise whether anything needs revisiting" — so it reads as process, not as you building a case against the company. If your manager won't send that question onward, send it to HR directly yourself.
5. Brief whichever executive sponsored the closure privately, before the group send — but in parallel with, not gating, the HR/legal question above.
6. Send the written correction to everyone who received the original analysis, with the same prominence the original had — a plain, specific subject line, one screen of text with the formula detail in an attachment, and an offer to walk anyone through it. Don't send it as a quiet addendum to a routine update.
7. Within a couple of weeks, propose one concrete safeguard going forward — an independent second check on any analysis that feeds a headcount decision. That's what keeps this from being remembered as purely a personal failure.

Before sending anything, also check: whether any claim, grievance, or legal hold already exists around this closure (if so, this sequencing changes — counsel needs to be involved before your manager or the sponsor, and may shape or send the message itself; this is a question for whoever handles employment matters at your organization, not something to guess at); whether your organization has a formal error- or incident-reporting channel you're expected to use; and where else the original figure was quoted — board materials, consultation or severance paperwork, budgets — since correcting those is your organization's call to make, not something to do unilaterally.

## Risks of this plan

- Minimizing — passive voice, a vague subject line, folding this into a routine update — reads as concealment in hindsight once anyone connects it to the layoffs, and that costs more trust than a direct disclosure would have.
- Over-personalizing — centering your own feelings, or writing an unprovable causal claim like "my error cost people their jobs" — puts the reader in the position of managing you instead of acting on the correction, and states a certainty about causation you don't actually have (whether such a line carries legal weight beyond the reputational cost is a question for whoever handles employment matters at your organization — unverified here).
- Sequencing it wrong — your manager or the sponsor hearing about this secondhand, or in the same group message as everyone else — reads as blindsiding a senior person regardless of how carefully the message itself is worded.
- A second, incomplete correction — missing a knock-on effect on another office's numbers or a savings forecast, or overwriting the original file so the record of what changed and when is unclear. A follow-up correction costs more than the first one did.
- The correction landing and then nothing happening — no pause on a live decision that turns out to ride the same formula, no answer on record for whether anything is owed to the laid-off staff — because neither question was ever put in writing to someone positioned to act on it.

The closure itself can't be undone by any version of this message — that part of the cost is already sunk regardless of tone. What's still live is whether the same error is quietly running somewhere else right now, whether anyone ever answers the one question the laid-off staff have no way to ask for themselves, and whether your own future work is trusted the same way again. None of those improve by waiting for a better-worded draft; the ones with real time value (a live decision on the same model) get worse the longer they wait.

## Strongest counter-position

The strongest case against including any personal acknowledgment at all: an apology, however brief and carefully hedged, functions as a request for forgiveness, and a request for forgiveness is easy to grant — "thanks for flagging it, don't beat yourself up" is a realistic, likely response, and it closes the conversation before anyone examines whether the decision itself deserves a second look. On this view the tone question is premature until materiality is established — recompute the original decision criteria with the corrected number first; if the office still fails them by a wide margin, spending any emotional weight on an apology manufactures a crisis over an error that didn't actually change the outcome, and if the criteria now come out differently, no amount of careful wording turns this into a minor technical fix regardless. There's a sharper edge to this position too: a personally-voiced apology lets an organization treat what may be a process failure — a headcount decision resting on one analyst's unreviewed model — as one person's individual slip, which is precisely the framing that teaches the next person who finds an error in an already-executed decision to stay quiet about it. Under this view the artifact should be an institutional correction notice routed through your manager, not a personally apologetic message, and ownership should be a single factual sentence — "the error was mine; the formula did X instead of Y" — with no separate apology sentence attached to it.

This wins if your read of this specific executive team is that acknowledgment functions socially as closure rather than as information you want them to sit with — if "don't worry about it" is a realistic response here, a careful apology sentence buys you goodwill at the cost of the scrutiny this decision may actually deserve. It also wins outright if the materiality check in step 2 shows the correction doesn't change the result at all. What would show it: after sending a version with no apology sentence, whether the conversation moves to the substantive questions (is the model used elsewhere, does anything change for the laid-off staff) or instead reads as cold and stalls — that reaction is itself the information neither position can supply in advance.

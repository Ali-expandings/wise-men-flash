### Where the Council Clashes
**Should the message state whether the corrected numbers would have changed the closure decision?** This is the real fight hiding inside the "should it say anything about how the decision might have gone" question.

- The Contrarian and the Outsider say: address it. The Contrarian's case is exposure over time — silence on a computable question isn't caution, it's a document that reads as concealment if anyone reconstructs the numbers independently later. The Outsider's case is narrative control — executives will re-run the math in their heads in about ten seconds regardless of what's written, so silence doesn't prevent the conclusion, it just hands the framing to whoever reaches it next, on worse terms than if the sender had shaped it.
- The First Principles Thinker and the Executor say: keep it out. Their case is category error, not caution. The analyst wasn't in the room for every input the executive team weighed — strategy, timing, other considerations — so asserting "the decision would have been different" claims authority the analyst doesn't have. That's a categorically bigger claim than "I made a math error," and it's the one move that manufactures legal exposure that didn't previously exist, over an outcome that's already final.

Both sides reason from the same fact — the error is too large to ignore — and reach opposite conclusions about who should put the answer in writing. That's a genuine split, not a false one.

**Is tone even the right axis?** The Outsider treats tone as real and separable from content: writing a 40%-magnitude miss in flat, minimal language is itself a mismatched tone, and the gap between the size of the error and the size of the words becomes the story people remember. The First Principles Thinker and the Executor treat tone as a byproduct — get the facts and the next step right and tone follows; chase tone as its own problem and the likely failure is a week lost polishing a draft while the team runs on bad numbers. Each is naming a different failure mode: one reads as evasive, the other reads as slow.

### The Recommendation
Send one short message with four fixed components, and resolve the council's central clash with a distinction both sides of it miss: state the corrected magnitude, withhold the verdict on the decision.

Lead with the corrected number, first sentence, no throat-clearing. Name the mechanical cause plainly — the specific formula error, not "an issue was identified." Add one direct sentence of ownership: "I made this error and should have caught it" — one sentence, not a page of contrition. Close with one specific, real process fix: a second-reviewer sign-off on any analysis that can trigger a closure or layoff decision, or whatever the actual gap was. Not a pitch to personally own quality control company-wide.

On the counterfactual: give the executive team everything they need to do the ten-second recalculation themselves — the original figure, the corrected figure, the percentage difference, and, if it follows directly from the analysis, whether the office still reads as net-negative under the corrected numbers or not. That's a fact about the analysis, and stating it plainly closes the silence-reads-as-concealment risk. Do not add a sentence concluding what the executive team "would have" decided with accurate numbers. That requires visibility into every non-numerical input they weighed, which this person doesn't have, and it's the one line that converts "I made an error" into "the company made the wrong call" — a claim nobody asked this person to make, over a decision that can't be undone anyway. If an executive wants to ask that question, they will, in about ten seconds. Let them ask it; don't answer it for them in a document that outlives the conversation.

Reject the Expansionist's frame outright — every reviewer independently agreed on that. Don't volunteer an open-ended audit of everything else before you know what it will find. Don't pitch yourself as the new owner of quality control in the same message that admits the error. Before this goes to the executive team, route it past a manager or HR first — not because an honest error is something to hide, but because a document assessing your own fault benefits from another set of eyes, and because that step also catches the one failure mode only the Executor named: a correction that turns out to have its own error in it. None of this should take a week. Verifying a number and getting one other set of eyes on a four-paragraph message are bounded, fast steps — the danger is polishing tone, not routing for review.

### The One Thing to Do First
Before drafting a single sentence, get the corrected number independently re-checked by someone else. Every other step in this verdict — whether the correction flips the closure conclusion, what the message says, who reviews it, what process fix to propose — depends on that number being right. A second error inside the "correction" would do more damage than everything else in this analysis combined.

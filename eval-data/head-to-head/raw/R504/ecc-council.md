## Council: Trusting the $15K LTV Behind the $5K CAC Proposal

**Architect:** The $15,000 LTV is a formula output, not a measured fact — it bakes in an ~83-month average customer lifetime the company has never observed once, and it's denominated in revenue, not margin. Don't use it to justify a $5,000 CAC ceiling.
Capping the model at the actual 36-month observed horizon and haircutting for margin brings LTV to roughly $3,700-4,200; a standard 3:1 LTV:CAC guardrail on that puts a defensible ceiling around $1,200-1,400 — about a quarter of what's proposed.

**Skeptic:** The 1/churn formula assumes an infinite constant-hazard tail; with zero customers ever observed past month 36, any lifetime estimate beyond that is extrapolation, not data — unfalsifiable against the company's own history.
Even the most generous case (every customer surviving the full 36 months, which none do) tops out at $6,480 revenue / ~$4,500-4,900 margin-adjusted — already at or below the $5,000 ask before correcting for real average tenure; better to gate CAC on payback period than on an extrapolated ratio.

**Pragmatist:** The $15,000 figure is fabricated math, not a forecast — it prices customers on a lifetime that's never once happened. Don't anchor a $5,000 ceiling to it.
Capped-horizon, margin-adjusted LTV lands near $3,700; applying 3:1 gives a working CAC ceiling of about $1,200, treated as a floor to retest upward once real month-37+ retention data exists.

**Critic:** $15,000 is an extrapolation dressed as a fact. A defensible ceiling today is roughly $1,000-1,500, not $5,000.
At $5,000 CAC against ~$126/month margin (assuming 70% gross margin), payback takes ~40 months — longer than any customer has ever stayed — and the 1.2% blended churn is itself flattered by survivorship: young cohorts haven't had time to churn yet, so true new-cohort churn is probably worse than 1.2%.

### Verdict
- **Consensus:** Reject $15,000 as a planning input. All four voices converge on the same two corrections independent of each other: truncate the lifetime assumption to the ~36 months actually observed (not the implied 83), and convert revenue to margin before comparing against CAC. Doing just that puts honest LTV at roughly $3,700-4,900 and a defensible CAC ceiling at roughly $1,000-1,500 — a quarter to a third of the $5,000 being proposed. Independent gut-check: at $5,000 CAC, payback on margin takes ~40 months, longer than the company has existed.
- **Strongest dissent:** Not whether to trust $15,000 — that's unanimous. The real fault line is whether ~$1,000-1,500 is itself miscalibrated, and in which direction. Skeptic and Pragmatist warn against overcorrecting: retention curves often flatten with age (survivors get stickier), so a rigid 36-month cap could throttle a channel that's actually fine, and the number should loosen as later-month data arrives. Critic pulls the opposite way: the 1.2% blended rate is inflated toward "healthy" by survivorship bias — it's dominated by cohorts that already shed their weak-fit customers — so the churn that new CAC dollars will actually face is probably higher than 1.2%, making even $1,000-1,500 optimistic. Nothing in hand resolves this; it's a real, unresolved disagreement.
- **Premise check:** Yes. - **Recommendation:** Don't set CAC at $5,000 off this number. Immediate: get the real gross margin figure — this analysis assumed 70-80% and that single unknown moves the answer by thousands of dollars. Then rebuild LTV as a capped-horizon (36-month), margin-adjusted figure (~$3,700-4,900) instead of an infinite-horizon one, and set the CAC ceiling off payback period (target 12-18 months of margin) rather than a ratio on an extrapolated LTV — it lands in the same ~$1,200-1,400 range and holds up better against immature data.

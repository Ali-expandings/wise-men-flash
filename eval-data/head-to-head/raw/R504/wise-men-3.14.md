## Recommendation

Don't trust the $15,000 — it isn't a measurement, it's an extrapolation running almost twice as long as your company has existed. A defensible estimate is roughly $4,000–$6,800, not $15,000, which puts a $5,000 CAC close to break-even rather than comfortably inside the 3:1 safety margin the original number implied. Don't set a new permanent ceiling off either figure: fix an interim cap now using a conservative payback rule, then raise it in stages as two specific facts this question doesn't supply — your real gross margin, and how fast you need CAC back — get filled in from data you already have.

## Why

$180 in monthly revenue divided by a 1.2% monthly churn rate is the textbook shortcut for lifetime value, but the division itself is a projection: it assumes a customer keeps paying, at that same rate, for an average of 1/0.012 ≈ 83 months — just under 7 years. Your company is 36 months old. No customer, anywhere in your history, has lived out even half of what the formula assumes. Splitting the same formula at month 36 makes the extrapolation explicit: on the churn rate your head of growth used, only about $5,300 of the $15,000 (35%) is revenue collectible within the 36 months you've actually observed; the other roughly $9,700 (65%) is attributed to months your business has never had a customer reach. That's not a rounding error in an otherwise-sound estimate — it's the majority of the number.

Two further problems compound the extrapolation, independent of how far forward it projects. The $180 is revenue, not gross profit — money spent serving a customer (hosting, support, payment fees) never reaches you, so counting all of it overstates what a customer is actually worth. And a dollar collected years from now is worth less than a dollar today; an estimate that doesn't discount for time overstates the same cash flows. Correcting for a plausible gross margin (70-80% is typical for software; not verified for your company) and a modest discount rate cuts $15,000 by roughly 40-60% before the extrapolation problem is even addressed.

Once the estimate is capped near what's actually observed, put on a margin basis, and discounted, the defensible range lands around $4,000–$6,800 — roughly a quarter to under half of $15,000. That range narrows or widens depending on two things this question doesn't supply: your real gross margin swings the estimate by roughly 2x on its own across a plausible range, and whether the 1.2% blended churn rate — measured on a base that, three years into growth, is necessarily weighted toward its newest, shortest-tenure cohorts — actually holds for customers you haven't kept long yet, or for the different customers a bigger acquisition budget tends to buy. Neither fact is knowable from the two numbers in the question; both are checkable from your own billing data.

The $5,000 figure is exactly $15,000 divided by 3 — the common "3:1 LTV-to-CAC is healthy" rule of thumb, applied directly to the inflated number. Apply the same 3:1 rule to the honest range instead and the ceiling that comes out is roughly $1,300–$2,300, not $5,000. At $5,000, the ratio on an honest estimate is closer to 1:1 or 1.4:1 — not obviously unsafe, but nowhere near the safety margin the original framing implied, and the time to earn $5,000 back in gross profit, after churn, runs 45-67 months under plausible assumptions — longer than your company has existed.

## What to do

Every credible path through this data points to the same fix, which the "approve $5,000 or don't" framing skips entirely: stop pricing acquisition spend off a single lifetime-value number this early, and set it by what you can actually recover, raising it in steps as real data replaces assumption.

1. This week (1-3 analyst-days, off billing data you already have, no new instrumentation): audit how the 1.2% figure was actually calculated — the denominator used, whether it's counted by customer or by revenue, and whether it already reflects customers on annual contracts who haven't reached a renewal decision yet, any of which would make the headline rate look better than what a monthly-committing customer actually experiences. In the same pass, build an actual cohort survival table — the percentage of customers from each signup month still paying at 6, 12, 24, and 36 months since signup — instead of relying on one blended figure. The 1.2% headline predicts roughly 87% surviving at 12 months, 75% at 24, and 65% at 36; if your oldest cohorts come in meaningfully below that, the churn rate itself is already optimistic before any extrapolation happens. Pull your actual gross margin per customer (after hosting, payment processing, support, onboarding) in the same pass — every dollar figure above scales directly with it.
2. Set an interim ceiling now, before that data lands, using a payback rule rather than a lifetime multiple: cap new spend at what a customer repays in gross profit within about 18-24 months. On the numbers given and a typical margin assumption, that's roughly $1,500–$2,800 — a number you don't need better data to defend today, and well under $5,000.
3. Once the cohort and margin data are in, raise the ceiling in stages, gated on evidence: move toward the $4,000–$5,000 end of the honest range only if (a) actual margin comes in at 70% or higher, (b) your real cost of capital or cash runway can tolerate a payback closer to 4-5 years than 2, and (c) new cohorts acquired at the higher spend show the same early retention your existing base showed at the same age. Each stage needs roughly 200-400 newly acquired customers before its early retention is a reliable read.
4. Retire the $15,000 figure from any spending decision. Use it, if at all, as a labeled upper bound ("what this could be worth if retention holds for years we haven't seen yet"), never as the number a CAC ceiling gets divided out of.

## Risks of this plan

Setting the interim ceiling too low has a real, easy-to-miss cost: a customer who would have been profitable at $5,000 gets turned away, a competitor wins them instead, and keeps them for years if the underlying churn math is right — a cost that never appears on any budget line you'd actually look at.

Setting it too high has the opposite failure mode, and a slower one to detect: the extra budget goes to channels and segments the 1.2% rate was never measured on, those customers are typically less committed than the ones who built your current numbers, and because paying that back in gross profit takes roughly 4-6 years even under favorable assumptions, a bad batch of acquisitions doesn't show up as a mistake for a year or more — by which time a lot of budget is already out the door, and loyal existing customers dilute the bad signal in any blended number you'd be watching.

Both risks are real; what should break the tie is which one you can afford to be slower to discover. Overspending is reversible in the sense that a channel can be shut off within a month of seeing bad early retention, but dollars already spent on an acquired cohort take years to resolve either way. Underspending is fully reversible — the ceiling can always be raised once better data exists — and costs nothing you can't recover by spending more later. That asymmetry, not the point estimate, is the real argument for starting conservative and moving up on evidence rather than starting at $5,000 and adjusting down.

## Strongest counter-position

The case against starting conservative: capping near-term spend by a strict payback rule may itself be the costlier mistake, not the safer one. On a full-lifetime, margin-and-discount-adjusted basis, $5,000 breaks even against a customer whose churn after month 36 stays anywhere below roughly 3-5% a month (several times today's rate, at a reasonable margin) — a bar the existing base would have to fall a long way to miss. As one line of this argument puts it, the objection "that no customer has stayed past 36 months, is the weakest critique available" — a churn rate is estimated from observed customer-months, not completed lifetimes: every month a customer doesn't churn is real evidence, and censoring biases naive averages of completed lifetimes, not a properly-estimated hazard rate. Cutting the estimate off at month 36 "isn't more honest, just wrong in a different way: it treats the ~65% of each cohort still paying at month 36 as leaving the next day" — and because customers differ in how likely they are to leave, the mathematics of that heterogeneity means the true average lifetime is, if anything, understated by naively averaging churn, not overstated. Add the asymmetry argued above — an approved-and-wrong customer is a bounded, visible, fixable mistake, while a declined-and-would-have-been-profitable customer is an invisible, uncapped one handed to a competitor — and the "safe" conservative number is arguably the riskier bet over a long enough horizon.

This case wins if actual gross margin is 70% or higher and real cost of capital or cash position can tolerate a 4-5 year payback — exactly the two facts step 1 above is designed to surface. It loses if margin comes in lower or the business needs spend back faster than that: the same math that supports a ceiling near $5,000 at 80% margin and a 10% cost of capital supports about $2,700 at 50% margin and a 30% cost of capital — itself inside the conservative interim range above, not below it. This isn't a disagreement about values — it's two consistent lines of arithmetic anchored to different guesses about numbers the business already has and the question didn't supply.

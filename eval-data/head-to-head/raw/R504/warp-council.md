## Why

- **The formula bakes in three silent assumptions nobody stated out loud**: 100% gross margin (revenue treated as profit), a 0% discount rate (cash seven years out valued the same as cash today), and constant 1.2% churn forever. Solve backward and the "healthy-looking" 3:1 LTV:CAC ratio (15,000 ÷ 5,000) only holds at exactly 100% margin — at any realistic margin (60–80%), it's actually 1.8–2.4:1, already below the standard 3:1 bar, before the censoring problem even enters the picture.
- **The censoring problem you flagged is the big one, and it's quantifiable**: 1 ÷ 0.012 = 83.3 months of implied average customer lifetime against 36 months of company history means every dollar this formula credits to month 37 onward is extrapolated into a zone with no data. Bounding the estimate to only the observed 36-month window caps revenue-basis LTV at roughly $5,300 (about $3,700 margin-adjusted at an illustrative 70%) — before any extrapolation at all.
- **CAC payback kills the $5,000 number on its own, independent of the LTV debate**: at realistic gross margins, a simple CAC-to-monthly-contribution payback is already 35–46 months; properly accounting for customers who churn along the way stretches that to roughly 54 months, and to around 77 months once discounted for time value. Every version of that is longer than the company has existed — a new customer would need to outlive your company's entire track record just to break even, before generating a dollar of actual profit.
- **Three independently-reasoning models, using three different methods** (strict evidence-window bounding, industry payback-benchmark bounding, discounted-scenario modeling), converged on the same ballpark ceiling — roughly $2,000–2,600, with outer bounds near $3,300–3,500 — without seeing each other's work. That convergence is itself evidence the number isn't arbitrary.
- **To be fair to the head of growth**: constant churn isn't obviously wrong, just unproven — subscription churn often falls with tenure as survivors get stickier, which would push true LTV up, not down. But the council's own sensitivity run shows that even the optimistic case (churn improving to 0.6% after month 36) only lifts the properly discounted, margin-adjusted LTV:CAC ratio to about 1.5:1 at a $5,000 CAC. Optimism about the tail doesn't rescue the number once margin and time value are put back in.

## Tradeoffs and risks

- Every figure above rests on illustrative assumptions (~70% gross margin, ~10–20% annual discount rate) because the real ones weren't given. Getting actual gross margin and a chosen hurdle rate from finance will move the honest estimate by thousands of dollars in either direction — that's the single highest-leverage next input.
- The 1.2% blended churn figure is itself suspect, separate from the censoring issue: if the customer base has been growing, the average skews toward younger, less-proven cohorts, and the direction of that bias isn't resolvable from what's known today.
- Don't overcorrect into a new false precision — "the true LTV is $X" is the same mistake being diagnosed here. The honest output of this exercise is a range with a stated confidence level, not a replacement magic number.
- Small-sample risk compounds the extrapolation risk: only the earliest cohorts have reached 30+ months of tenure, so even the "observed" tail of the 36-month window is thin data with wide error bars.

## Final call

Don't raise the CAC ceiling to $5,000. Set an interim ceiling around $2,000–2,600 (roughly what clears a 3:1 LTV:CAC or a 12–18 month payback under realistic margin assumptions), and in parallel: pull actual gross margin from finance, build cohort-level retention curves (by signup month, tracked to current tenure) instead of one blended churn number, and pick a discount rate. Re-run the LTV math on that basis, and only move the ceiling above $2,600–3,000 once month-6/12 retention data from customers actually acquired at the new CAC confirms the churn assumption holds — not before.

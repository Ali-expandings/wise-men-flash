## Recommendation

Don't commit to either option as posed. Spend two weeks validating the real cause with a won-versus-lost deal review (not just a review of the seven losses) and pricing out a license-or-embed alternative, then fund a scoped build — a few specific automations, not a full parity builder — while taking an all-cash acquisition of most of the cash reserve off the table. If the two-week check forces an immediate binary choice with no time to validate, build is the safer of the two named options.

## Why

The seven-of-ten figure is sales' own loss reason, logged after the fact, and it counts only losses — it says nothing about whether automation was also present in the deals that were won, or whether it was ever a decisive factor rather than the most convenient explanation a rep can give that doesn't point at their own pricing, discovery, or qualification. Win-loss data collected this way is a well-known weak source; before it justifies spending most of the company's cash or two quarters of engineering, it's worth two weeks and one analyst outside sales to check it against actual deal records — was automation a written requirement before the competitor entered each evaluation, what stage did the deal end at, and was price or timing also in play.

Set the loss data aside for a moment and the acquisition still doesn't fit the problem. Buying a company to get one feature is a weak bet on its own terms. Large-sample research on acquisition outcomes disagrees sharply on the topline number — depending on how "success" is defined, published estimates of the share of deals that fail to deliver their expected value range from roughly a quarter (Bain's tracking of strategic acquisitions against internal targets) to a large majority (a 40-year, 40,000-deal analysis widely reported in 2024, and KPMG's 2025 integration survey) — but the same research agrees on what predicts the outcome: acquisition experience is the strongest single predictor of success, and Bain's work on acquirer shareholder returns finds that companies making an infrequent or first acquisition underperform serial acquirers by a wide margin. An 80-person company buying a startup to close one feature gap would be making exactly that kind of one-off, inexperienced acquisition — in the highest-risk position on that curve — while committing most of its cash to landing in the minority that works. Layer the deal-specific risks on top: a startup willing to sell for most of what an 80-person buyer can pay is more plausibly one whose independent path has stalled than one turning down better offers; its product is typically built on its own data model and stack, so "buy it and you have the feature" usually understates months of integration work rather than a clean drop-in; and the people who understand the code commonly leave once their retention terms are satisfied, which is exactly when the buyer needs them most.

Building is the safer of the two named options because it's the reversible one — a slipped internal project can be descoped, delayed, or reallocated, where a cash acquisition that has consumed most of the reserve cannot be undone. But "two quarters to build a polished automation builder" is itself oversized for the actual goal, which is to stop losing deals over this gap, not to out-build a competitor who has been polishing their product for longer. Products like Asana's Rules, monday.com's automation recipes, and HubSpot's Workflows all shipped narrow first and broadened later; a scoped build of just the automations that show up in the deal review, backed by an immediate stopgap (a Zapier/Make/webhooks integration, shippable in weeks), answers the competitive gap faster and for a fraction of the cost and risk of either matching the competitor feature-for-feature or buying a company to get there.

## What to do

Week 1-2: have someone outside sales — a PM or ops analyst, not the team whose pipeline benefits from the feature being funded — pull the last 20-30 mid-size deals, wins and losses, and tag each for whether automation was a written requirement before the competitor entered the evaluation, what stage it ended at, and whether price or timing were also factors. In parallel, get scoping quotes from one or two embeddable/white-label automation vendors, and if a specific acquisition target is already in view, ask what they'd take structured as a license with an option to acquire, paid against integration and adoption milestones rather than cash upfront — a seller confident in the product will take that structure; insistence on cash now is itself useful diligence.

Decision point at the end of week 2: if the review shows automation was an early, written, decisive requirement in most of the seven losses — not simply the reason logged afterward — commit to a scoped build (roughly one quarter, a small team) covering only the automations the review surfaced, with the stopgap integration live within the following two to three weeks. Take the acquisition off the table as a cash purchase; keep it alive only as a license/embed structure, and only if the vendor conversations support it. If the review instead shows price, qualification, or timing were equally or more present in the losses, redirect the effort there instead — neither building nor buying an automation feature fixes a problem that wasn't the automation.

Escalate or change course if the scoped build's first milestone slips past one quarter, or if the deal review shows buyers are evaluating the builder itself rather than specific automations — either is the signal to move to the license/embed structure instead of extending an in-house build indefinitely.

## Risks of this plan

Two weeks of validation costs two weeks of continued exposure to the same loss pattern if automation really is decisive — but against seven losses accumulated over what was already more than two weeks, that delay costs on the order of one additional deal, not the outcome of the decision. A scoped build can still lose a head-to-head evaluation if buyers are judging the builder as a category rather than specific automations, which is why builder-as-category evidence is an explicit escalation trigger above rather than something this plan assumes away. The validation step only works if it's actually run by someone without a stake in the answer; if "outside sales" review is nominal and the same people who want the feature funded end up shaping it, it reproduces the original bias instead of correcting it. And a scoped build, like any estimate, can still run long — the discipline has to be the one-quarter checkpoint actually triggering a course change, not a plan that reads well now and quietly extends later.

Cost of being wrong: this plan is highly reversible. Two weeks of analysis, a stopgap integration, and a one-quarter scoped build can each be adjusted, extended, or dropped without lasting damage to the company. The path this memo argues against — an all-cash acquisition of most of the reserve — is close to irreversible: if the integration or retention risk lands, which is the base-rate outcome for this kind of deal, the company ends up with both a half-integrated feature and a depleted cash cushion, with no clean recovery path short of emergency financing or cuts elsewhere.

## Strongest counter-position

The strongest challenge to this memo isn't a case for buying — it's a case that the entire premise underneath both paths is unverified. Look at who produced the number: "a buyer who says no gives the rep the least awkward reason, and an impersonal feature gap is the least awkward," and the loss log "doesn't point at their discovery, pricing or qualification." More basically: "only losses were counted, so nothing shows whether automation separates losses from wins or just comes up in every mid-size evaluation." If that's right, a scoped build and a license/embed deal are still solving a problem that may not be the real one — just more cheaply than an acquisition would have. That is exactly why this memo's first step is the won-versus-lost comparison rather than a build commitment, and the counter-position wins outright if that comparison is skipped, done superficially, or run by people with a stake in the answer.

A second, opposite case also deserves a hearing: speed may matter more than this memo assumes. If the pattern is real and a capable competitor keeps extending its lead while an internal build ships in stages, a fast, well-priced acquisition could close the gap in one motion instead of several sequential bets. This wins if validation confirms the cause and a specific target turns out to be unusually well matched — compatible architecture, founders willing to stay past a retention cliff, priced well under "most of the reserve" — which is evidence this memo doesn't have and recommends gathering before ruling the option out, not after.

## Confidence

High that committing most of an 80-person company's cash reserve to a single acquisition, to close one competitive feature gap, is disproportionate to the problem as described — this follows from the acquisition base-rate evidence and the reversibility asymmetry regardless of how the loss-cause question resolves. Medium that a scoped, roughly one-quarter build is the right-sized response rather than either extreme — this depends on the still-unverified assumption that buyers need specific automations rather than a full multi-step builder as a category, which the deal review is designed to test. Low, and the material unknown the whole plan is built around: whether "missing automation features" is the true cause of the seven losses or the most convenient thing to log — nothing offered in the original framing distinguishes these, which is why resolving it comes before either path gets funded.

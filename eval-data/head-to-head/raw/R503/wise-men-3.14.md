## Recommendation

Neither option as posed is the right first move for a company this size. Don't commit to a two-quarter feature-parity build, and don't spend most of the cash reserve on the acquisition yet — run a fast, cheap check on the real evidence in parallel with starting the one piece of engineering work both paths require anyway, then make the build/license/buy call from that within about a month. If forced to choose between exactly the two options as stated, with no time to check anything first: build, scoped down — not the acquisition.

## Why

The number driving this — seven of the last ten mid-size losses citing missing automation — is weaker evidence than it looks. It's a small sample: even taken at face value, a 7-out-of-10 result is statistically consistent with anywhere from roughly a third to over ninety percent of the true underlying rate, wide enough that it shouldn't alone justify betting most of the reserve. It's also self-reported by the people with the least to gain from blaming their own execution or price instead of the product, and it only looks at losses — it says nothing about whether deals you won needed automation too, or how many prospects ruled you out before a deal ever reached a rep.

Buying doesn't actually buy back the two quarters. Whichever path you take, you need the same underlying plumbing: your product emitting events, an API for outside logic to act on your data, permission checks, and an audit trail. A bought automation engine can't do anything native in your product until that layer exists on your side, and closing a small acquisition plus wiring the target's engine onto your login, data model and compliance scope typically consumes about as much engineering time as building a scoped version yourself. Net, the acquisition's speed advantage over a scoped build is close to zero or negative in the realistic case — unless the target's product is already running against a system like yours, for comparable customers, which would be a real, checkable head start rather than an assumed one.

Spending most of an 80-person company's cash reserve is also a different category of risk than spending two quarters of engineering time. A build that turns out to be wrong can be descoped, delayed, or stopped — expensive, but survivable. A cash-heavy acquisition that turns out to be wrong empties the buffer you'd otherwise have against a slipped integration, a lost customer, a bad quarter, or a hard fundraise, with no equivalent stop button once it's spent.

## What to do

This week, run two tracks in parallel — not one gating the other:

1. Start the shared groundwork your product needs regardless of which path wins: the events/API/permissions/audit layer any automation — native, licensed, or acquired — has to run on. This is a no-regret move either way, and it's usually where the real schedule risk hides.
2. Spend one to two weeks actually checking the diagnosis, almost for free, with data you already have: pull the call recordings, RFPs and CRM notes on the seven losses and on a similar number of recent mid-size wins; have someone other than the deal's own rep talk directly to a handful of the lost buyers; check whether any deals disqualified themselves before ever reaching a rep at all. You're testing whether automation actually separates wins from losses, not just whether it gets mentioned.

Set a decision date about four weeks out. At that point:

- If the evidence doesn't hold up — automation shows up just as often in deals you won, or the losses trace mostly to something else — don't spend product budget chasing it; fix the real cause instead.
- If it holds up and buyers named a specific, bounded set of workflows: cover those with the cheapest reversible option (an integration, a template, or a licensed embeddable builder) while your native version reaches early customers.
- If it holds up but buyers are judging the depth and polish of a full builder, not a checklist: that's a bigger commitment either way — see Confidence.
- Only commit real money to acquiring the startup if, by that date, all three hold: its product is demonstrably already running against a system like yours for comparable customers (a real head start, not an assumption); the value of the deals you'd realistically win back clears the all-in cost — price, fees, retention, and the integration work you still owe — by a healthy margin, not a coin flip; and you'd still have well over a year of runway left afterward in a conservative case. If you go this way, don't pay mostly cash — structure it as a license with an option to buy, or a deal paid mostly in stock or earn-out with retention tied to milestones rather than a single cliff, so a bad integration doesn't also cost you the reserve. (If it gets this far, treat this as the start of real diligence, not the end of it — deal mechanics, the target's own customer base, and security review still need their own pass.)
- Whatever you use as an interim stopgap, pick it on purpose: something meant to be temporary has a way of becoming permanent once customers build workflows on top of it, so don't back into a long-term platform commitment through what was supposed to be a two-week patch. And don't let sales put delivery dates for unbuilt features into contracts in the meantime — those promises can create their own revenue complications later, on top of ones you may not keep.

Two plain accounting facts worth knowing before you structure anything: money paid to an acquired team that's conditional on them staying employed counts as an ongoing compensation cost after the deal closes, not as a one-time part of the purchase price — it shows up in your results for as long as the retention period runs, so get your accountant involved before you agree to the structure, not after. And commitments to acquired customers or unbuilt features can affect when you're allowed to recognize the related revenue. Neither is a reason to avoid a deal; both are reasons to loop in your accountant while you're still negotiating terms, not after signing.

Set the actual success test now, before spending anything: your mid-size win rate against this competitor, before and after. Hold it loosely for the first quarter or two — that's unlikely to be enough deals to reliably prove or disprove the fix, so don't over-read an early flat result as proof the diagnosis was wrong.

## Risks of this plan

The biggest risk runs the other way: letting "check the evidence" become a slow, sequential gate instead of two weeks run alongside real engineering work. That's the one cost this company can't get back — every week of delay hands the competitor more room to extend its lead, on top of whatever the seven-deal figure is already costing. Time-box the check and keep the groundwork moving regardless of what it finds.

The check itself can mislead: buyers tend to give a polite, impersonal reason for saying no, and a handful of interviews is still a small sample — treat it as a strong signal, not certainty. A scoped fix might also turn out not to be enough at all (see counter-position below) — the four-week check is what's supposed to catch that before real money moves, not after. And if an acquisition does happen later, the most common failure is the people who actually understand the acquired system leaving once retention money vests — spread that money over multiple milestones instead of concentrating it in one payout.

Cost of being wrong: this plan is built to be cheap to reverse. If the four-week check says "commit big" and it's right, you've lost nothing — you needed that evidence either way. If it says "commit big" and the counter-position turns out to be the real story instead, you'll know that from the buyer conversations, not by guessing, before you've spent the reserve. Skipping the check and going straight to either extreme is the version of this decision that's hard to undo.

## Strongest counter-position

This plan assumes a scoped, verified fix — something short of a full two-quarter build or a company acquisition — can actually close the gap. The strongest case against that: "missing automation" may not be a specific, fixable checklist item at all. It may be the most visible symptom of buyers seeing this company as the less mature, less complete platform overall, in which case matching a feature only removes today's stated objection while the same buyers, or the next cohort, find a new one next quarter — because the comparison was never really about one feature. Under that reading, a version built in weeks, or a bolted-on licensed tool, doesn't fix anything durable; it just delays the same conversation. If that's the real shape of the problem, the honest choices are starker than "scoped build vs. cheap license": either commit to automation as a sustained, properly funded, permanent part of the product — which argues for more investment than the scoped plan above, not less — or accept that this competitor currently wins on platform maturity and compete on a different axis (segment, price, service, or a different technical strength) instead of trying to out-polish a company with a multi-year head start. This is exactly what the four-week check needs to settle — not just whether automation was involved in these losses, but whether buyers wanted a checklist item or a maturity signal — and it's the one question this plan cannot answer for you in advance.

## Confidence

High: don't spend most of the cash reserve on this now, and don't take the ten-deal sales tally at face value without a cheap check first. Both hold under nearly any plausible values for the missing numbers — deal size, reserve size, burn rate — and there's no realistic combination of them where committing the reserve now, on this evidence alone, beats spending a few weeks to check it first.

Medium: that building the shared technical groundwork now and responding with a scoped, deliberately-sequenced fix is the right shape of response, rather than either extreme. This assumes buyers need specific workflows covered rather than overall platform polish — the load-bearing unknown in this whole plan (see counter-position) — and it's the first thing the four-week check should resolve.

Low, and deliberately unresolved here: whether the acquisition ever becomes the right call. It turns on one fact this company doesn't have yet — whether the startup's product already runs against a system like this company's, for comparable customers, which would give it a real rather than assumed head start.

Material unknowns, roughly in order of how much they matter: whether buyers are responding to specific missing workflows or to overall platform maturity; whether the startup's automation engine is already integrated with systems like this company's; the actual size of the cash reserve and monthly burn — whether "most of the reserve" means a survivable stretch or a genuine solvency risk; and the average value and volume of the mid-size deals actually in play.

Three duties pull in different directions here, and the resolution is to let the tip change your *process*, not your *verdict*.

**The candidate** is owed a decision based on evidence they had a chance to address, not on an anonymous, unresolved allegation. "Left during an investigation" is consistent with being pushed out, resigning under pressure, resigning for unrelated reasons while it was still open, or being cleared entirely — your colleague doesn't know which, and neither do you. Treating an unresolved investigation as a proxy for guilt isn't fair to the candidate, and if this decision is ever questioned, "I heard a rumor" is not a basis you want in a rejection file. The glowing formal references don't cancel this out, but not for the reason it feels like they should — references are chosen by the candidate precisely because they'll be glowing, so they were never strong evidence against this kind of concern in the first place. Their being positive doesn't corroborate anything; it just reflects normal selection bias.

**The people who'd report to them** have the most at stake and the least voice in this process — they can't vet their future manager, you're doing it on their behalf. The asymmetry is real: a wrong "no hire" costs one strong candidate a job they can get elsewhere; a wrong "hire" puts several junior people under someone with a live pattern, repeatedly, backed by the authority of the role. That doesn't mean you convict on hearsay. It means how hard you dig before deciding should scale with how much power the role holds over vulnerable people — independent of this tip, but the tip is a reason to prioritize it now.

**Your former colleague** is owed protection as a source. She shared something that could cost her if it traces back to her, on the express condition it goes no further. Don't repeat the specific claim to the candidate, HR, or other interviewers in any form attributable to her — not her name, not "someone who worked with them told me." If it surfaces at all in how you explain your process, keep it generic ("this is a people-manager role, I want deeper reference checks than usual") — true on its own terms, untraceable to that one conversation.

What you can actually do with something you can't verify or cite: use it to raise your diligence, not to reach a conclusion.

- Go beyond the reference list the candidate gave you. Find former peers or reports through LinkedIn or mutual connections and ask open, behavioral questions: how they handled conflict with a junior report, what turnover looked like on their team, whether people would work for them again.
- Build interview questions around management of junior staff specifically — concrete past examples (a hard feedback conversation, a mistake by a report, a conflict they had to resolve), not abstract leadership philosophy. Specific, unrehearsed detail is harder to fake than a values statement.
- Don't put the original claim to the candidate directly. It's unverifiable and unattributable, and confronting them with it either breaks your colleague's confidence or forces you to lie about your source.
- If expanded diligence turns up nothing more, decide on what the process actually produced — interviews and checks you can stand behind — not on the original tip. At that point you've done what's reasonable; you don't get to hold a candidate hostage to a rumor indefinitely.
- If you do hire them, hedge the risk without pre-punishing them for it: earlier-than-usual check-ins with their new reports, an accessible way for those reports to raise concerns, skip-levels sooner than standard. Good practice for any new manager, and it happens to cover this specific risk too.

Bottom line: one secondhand, unresolved, unattributable claim isn't enough to reject someone. It is enough to justify real time finding out more before you decide — and for a role with authority over junior people, you'd want to do most of that digging anyway.

## Council Verdict: Weighing an Unverifiable Misconduct Tip in a Hiring Decision

### Where the Council Agrees

Four of five reviewers independently named the First Principles response strongest, for the same three reasons each time: unverifiability is a property of the colleague-channel, not of the underlying fact, so corroborating it elsewhere doesn't require burning her; splitting "act on the tip" from "disclose the tip" dissolves the source-protection-versus-future-reports conflict the original framing treats as a fixed trade-off; and the asymmetric stakes — a passed-over candidate has recourse, a quietly-hired pattern of mistreating juniors is silent and compounds under someone's new authority — set a principled, non-arbitrary threshold for how hard to dig. Multiple reviewers converging on the same underlying mechanism, unprompted, is the single highest-confidence result here.

Every advisor that engaged with the "bury it or convict on it" framing rejected it and landed in the same place: treat the tip as a lead that directs attention, verify through channels that don't run through the colleague, never name her anywhere.

The council also converged, by three independent routes, on deflating "glowing formal references" as a real counterweight: such references are what you'd get whether the allegation was true, false, or never resolved; candidates hand-pick their own referees; and standard references rarely probe treatment of junior staff at all. Three arguments, one conclusion — stop treating "complete and glowing" as a proxy for "verified."

On tactics, three advisors independently proposed pulling references from the candidate's actual former direct reports rather than their chosen referees; three independently proposed a direct question about why they left plus a behavioral question on managing junior staff, without mentioning any investigation; two independently proposed a routine HR call to confirm dates, title, and rehire eligibility.

All five reviewers flagged the Expansionist response as weakest, for reframing a disclosure about possible mistreatment of junior staff primarily as upside for the hiring manager and recasting the colleague as a source to cultivate rather than someone who confided under an explicit request for confidence.

### Where the Council Clashes

**Diagnosis versus playbook.** One reviewer broke from the rest and named the Executor response strongest, because it's the only one that turns all four questions into a sequence that actually runs. The majority prized First Principles instead, for getting the underlying reasoning right. Both are defensible: First Principles explains why the diligence is proportionate and why the source conflict dissolves, but never produces a script; Executor supplies the script but never explains why this much effort is warranted rather than overkill.

**Where the legal exposure actually sits.** The Outsider treats defamation and negligent-hire risk as separable and manageable through silence plus independent verification. The Contrarian locates a danger the Outsider doesn't address: fully protecting the source while quietly acting on the claim produces a rejection with no citable reason — exactly the fact pattern a discrimination claim looks for. Both can be right at once; neither reconciles the two into one picture.

**Solo judgment call versus institutional resource.** The Outsider alone notes this is being handled as a personal ethics puzzle when most organizations have HR or legal built for exactly this situation. The other advisors implicitly treat this as something the hiring manager executes personally. Reasonable people disagree here for a real reason: looping in HR adds institutional cover and shared accountability, but also creates a formal record focused on one candidate off the back of an uncitable tip — its own fairness risk unless applied to every finalist identically.

### Blind Spots the Council Caught

Multiple reviewers caught that every advisor's mitigation for the hiring manager's own bias was self-monitoring, even though the Contrarian's own diagnosis names a failure mode self-monitoring can't catch: absorbing the claim, then unconsciously reading later ambiguous behavior as confirmation. The missing fix: a second interviewer, blind to the tip, independently assessing the same candidate, or a decision standard written down before any extra digging starts.

Reviewers also caught that every advisor wrote as though the tip probably points at something real — none weighed how weak "left while an investigation was underway" is as evidence standing alone, and none planned for the likeliest outcome: verification stays inconclusive by the time a decision is due.

A further catch: every proposed verification step targets only the tipped candidate. Scrutinizing one finalist and not the others is disparate treatment on its own, and a second, unguarded channel for the exact motivated reasoning the Contrarian warned about.

One reviewer caught that the council never turned its own skepticism on its own fix: cold-messaged former reports and HR reference calls carry selection bias and the same legal-muzzling dynamic used to dismiss the formal references in the first place. Another caught the one artifact nobody proposed: a contemporaneous record showing the verification steps were standard practice for the role, not triggered by the tip. And two low-cost, unmentioned moves surfaced: a plain news/litigation search on the candidate and former employer, and going back to the colleague — gently, without pressure — to ask whether anyone at that company might speak on the record.

### The Recommendation

Run one verification process, applied to every finalist, not an investigation of one person. Take the First Principles reasoning as the standard and the Executor's tactics as the mechanism.

For every finalist for this role — not only this candidate — run a plain news and litigation search, pull two or three references from people who actually reported to them rather than names they hand-picked, ask the same direct question about why they left a past role plus a behavioral question on managing conflict with junior staff, and place a routine call to former-employer HR. Decide this before knowing which candidate it will flag.

Never name the colleague, her claim, or the fact that a tip exists — not to HR, not to the candidate, not in a note to yourself. That's the entire and fully achievable scope of what's owed to her, because acting on information and disclosing where it came from are different acts. If it ever feels natural, ask her once, without pressure, whether anyone at that company could speak on the record — a fair question, not a requirement, and her answer, including no, is final.

Hold the bar asymmetric, not neutral: a wrongly-passed-over candidate has recourse and other doors open; a wrongly-hired pattern of mistreating juniors is quiet, hard to prove even when real, and compounds under someone's new authority over people with little power to push back. That justifies real effort here a routine hire wouldn't get. It does not justify treating an unresolved investigation as a proxy for guilt — investigations open on unsubstantiated complaints too, and this one's outcome is unknown even to the person who mentioned it.

If the independent channels come back clean — or if the decision deadline arrives before anything resolves either way, the more likely outcome — treat both the same: there is no citable basis to reject the candidate, and none should be manufactured. Hire, and build in early 30/60/90 check-ins with their new reports that specifically ask about management style, as cheap insurance if the concern was real and nothing else caught it. If a channel corroborates the concern, that's now the hiring manager's own finding, not secondhand hearsay — document it and act on it directly. Either way, keep a contemporaneous record showing the verification steps were standard practice for the role, run identically across every finalist.

### The One Thing to Do First

Before contacting anyone — not the colleague, not the candidate, not a former report, not HR — decide and write down that whatever verification steps get run, they get run identically on every finalist for this role, starting today. That commitment is what turns everything that follows from a targeted investigation triggered by an uncitable tip into a defensible hiring process that happens to also answer it.

## Recommendation

Don't act on the tip directly — you can't verify it and couldn't defend a decision built on it — but don't let "unverifiable" become a reason to do nothing either. Put one uniform, written question to every finalist for this role, tighten reference-checking so you're not only hearing from referees the candidate chose, and commit in writing, before any offer goes out, to real post-hire oversight with named checkpoints and a clear trigger for escalation. Keep your colleague's identity, and the fact that she told you anything at all, out of every record, permanently.

## Why

What you owe each party follows from what they actually asked for or need, not from how strong the tip is. The candidate is owed the same process as every other finalist and a decision built on evidence they had a chance to answer — not a quiet pass based on a claim they never heard, and not a confrontation with hearsay they can't respond to. The people who would report to them are owed proportionate diligence now and a safe way to raise concerns once someone starts — not the tip itself, and not false reassurance from the references already on file: those referees were chosen by the candidate, and many employers, wary of defamation liability, coach managers to confirm only dates and title or stay blandly positive — a common enough pattern that a warm reference alone says less than it sounds like it does. Glowing references are close to the typical outcome for departing employees generally, so they don't offset a specific claim like this one. If anything, an investigation commonly closes without any formal finding once its subject resigns, so "she doesn't know how it ended" may simply mean it never formally ended at all. Your former colleague is owed discretion about her identity, not inaction on what you now know — she asked not to be named or traced back to, not that you do nothing with the information. Those are two different requests, and honoring the first doesn't require honoring the second.

The tip itself earns a specific, narrow kind of weight. It's more particular than an ordinary rumor — a named category of complaint, a departure that overlaps it in time — and it cost the source something to share: she risked an awkward conversation and asked for confidentiality, which isn't the profile of someone angling for anything. That's enough to justify one uniform check. It is not enough to justify a verdict.

A single written question, asked of every finalist and not just this one, does two things a quiet background check cannot. It treats every candidate identically, which is both fairer and more defensible than singling one out on the strength of a private tip. And it converts something you can't cite into something you can: the candidate's own answer, or a false "no," becomes evidence you're allowed to act on, where a secondhand tip never was. Reference-checking still adds information, but only once you reach past the referees the candidate selected — a former direct report you choose from a full list, or an independent contact through your own network, tells you something a hand-picked reference cannot, because a manager who manages up well can, often enough, produce genuinely warm references from above while still treating juniors badly.

Because the people who would report to this hire carry the downside of a wrong call for as long as they work there, while a passed-over strong candidate generally has other options, the standard of care tips toward the future reports whenever your top two finalists are otherwise close. It does not extend to rejecting a clearly stronger candidate on a claim you have never been able to test.

## What to do

This week: look at the references you already have for this candidate and check whether any come from their manager or a direct report at the last job, or only from people they managed upward to — that gap, if it's there, already tells you something. In parallel, have your HR or legal partner confirm, for your jurisdiction, that you can put a standard written question to every finalist for the role: "Have you ever been the subject of a workplace complaint or investigation related to how you managed people, including one that was still open when you left a position? Please describe it." Frame it, in writing and out loud, the same way for everyone: a past complaint isn't automatically disqualifying, an untruthful answer is.

Also this week, without naming your colleague or repeating what she told you to anyone else: ask her once whether her knowledge is firsthand or secondhand, and whether someone with firsthand knowledge might be willing to talk to you off the record — leave that to her, don't chase it yourself. Separately, for every finalist, require the most recent manager plus two former direct reports that you select from a full list, not only the names each candidate supplies, using the same fixed questions for everyone: would you work for them again, and why; what should their next team know.

Hold yourself to your normal finalist-decision date for both the disclosure question and the expanded references — don't extend the process specifically to chase this further, and don't reopen the decision later on the strength of the same tip alone. Decide on what you actually have by then: if the candidate discloses something and gives a coherent, checkable account, or the independently-sourced references are specific and positive, proceed. If the candidate flatly denies any such process and you have reason to doubt that, or independently-sourced reports hedge specifically on how this person treats people with less power than them, that's grounds to pass — on the record you built, not on the original tip.

If you hire: commit in writing, before day one, to skip-level conversations you personally run at roughly 30, 60 and 90 days, and decide now what you'd need to hear to end the hire. A vague "I'll keep an eye on it" does no real work — new reports rarely volunteer concerns about the person writing their reviews. Never put the tip, or your colleague's name, into the applicant-tracking system, interview notes, or any scorecard, before or after the hire.

## Risks of this plan

If the tip is true and you proceed anyway: the disclosure question and the expanded references might still come back clean. A practiced answer can minimize a real problem as easily as it resolves a false alarm, and a manager skilled at managing up can often produce genuinely warm references from people above them while still treating juniors badly. A post-hire mechanism that isn't real — a token check-in instead of a committed structure with a clear trigger — won't catch what the pre-hire steps missed, and the people who pay for that are the future reports, who had no voice in this decision at all.

If the tip is false, or unrelated to anything real, and it's allowed to shape the process more than this: you risk an unfair, hard-to-defend rejection of your strongest candidate over a claim you were explicitly asked to keep private and could never test. If your colleague's role in it ever surfaces, even indirectly, you will have spent her trust for nothing and lost access to this kind of warning in the future. Waiting too long, or extending the process indefinitely to keep digging, risks losing the candidate to another offer while gaining little — the checks that would actually move your confidence either produce something concrete quickly, or they don't produce anything at all.

This plan is largely reversible and low-cost if the tip turns out to be false: you will have added one uniform question and a somewhat tighter reference process to your pipeline, which is fair and defensible regardless of the outcome. It is much less reversible if the tip is true and the post-hire piece is allowed to be token rather than real, because by then the harm has already landed on people with less power in this than you have — which is exactly the failure this plan exists to prevent, and the one piece of it most worth protecting if everything else here turns out to be too optimistic.

## Strongest counter-position

The strongest case against this plan doesn't defend the candidate — it argues that the pre-hire half of it is closer to theater than protection. On this view, a manager who flatters upward and treats juniors badly "gets glowing references from above by construction," and a rehearsed, technically true answer to a direct question — there was a process, it closed with no finding, I left for unrelated reasons — does the same work a hand-picked referee does: checks like these come back "clean" about as often whether the tip is true or false, and clean results get "filed as proof of innocence," producing a defensible record for the hiring manager while "the juniors carry all the leftover risk." If that's right, the disclosure question and the expanded references are still worth doing for fairness and process reasons, but neither should be mistaken for real evidence of safety — the only piece of this plan actually capable of protecting future reports is the post-hire mechanism, which means it can't be treated as a backstop. It has to be the part that's actually load-bearing.

This view wins if the post-hire commitment drifts from a structured, personally-run check with a genuine escalation trigger into an informal "I'll keep an eye on it" — the default outcome once a hire is made and other priorities compete for attention. It loses wherever the disclosure question or the expanded references produce something concrete and checkable — a specific admitted incident, a specific and consistent hedge from an independently sourced report — at which point they've stopped being process and become actual evidence.

## Confidence

High: what you owe your colleague is discretion about her identity, not inaction on what you now know. She asked not to be named or traced back to, not that you do nothing with the information — those are separable commitments, and you can honor the first fully without honoring the second.

Medium: a uniform, written disclosure question put to every finalist is a fair and practically useful way to convert an uncitable claim into a citable answer. It depends on your jurisdiction's employment law treating the question as legitimate, which needs a one-time check with HR or legal before you send it, not a judgment call to make alone.

Medium: expanding references beyond candidate-selected referees adds real signal. It depends on whether anyone reachable that way is actually willing to speak candidly, which fails in a small or tightly networked industry, or wherever a settlement or NDA is already keeping people quiet.

Low, deliberately: whether the underlying claim is true. It is likely to stay unknown, and this plan doesn't depend on ever resolving it — it depends on treating every finalist the same way and building in real oversight regardless of the answer.

Material unknowns: whether your colleague's own knowledge is firsthand or secondhand; how close this candidate actually is to your next-best alternative, which changes how much weight an unresolved signal should carry; and whether your organization's process currently permits the kind of independent, off-list outreach this plan assumes you can do.

## Council Debate: The Unverified Investigation Tip on a Team-Lead Candidate

**Council Members:**
- **Renata** — HR business partner & employment-risk counsel (process integrity, legal exposure)
- **Jordan** — duty-of-care advocate, former manager who inherited a team after a toxic lead (protecting future reports)
- **Theo** — fairness & due-process skeptic, workplace-investigations background (evidentiary quality, bias risk)
- **Wren** — talent-acquisition pragmatist (what's actually actionable)

**Rounds:** 3 (Positions → Responses → Synthesis)

### Round 1: Initial Positions

**Renata (HR & employment-risk counsel):**
Here's my problem with where this is heading before anyone even says it out loud: the moment that tip touches your decision-making — even a gut hesitation you can't quite explain in the loop — you've built a discrimination and defamation fact pattern with no paper trail to defend it. You can't cite an anonymous secondhand claim about an unresolved investigation. If challenged, you can't produce it, can't verify it, can't even name your source without burning her. That's not risk-aversion talk, that's what a plaintiff's attorney does with "a former colleague told me" on day one of discovery.

But don't mistake my caution for "do nothing." Unverifiable doesn't mean unactionable — it means it can't be the actionable thing itself. Extend the process. Add a structured, behavior-based reference call that probes management style directly, for every finalist, not just this one. Loop in HR and legal now, source unnamed. That's the proper channel. Freelancing a rumor is not diligence — it's exposure wearing diligence's clothes.

**Jordan (duty-of-care advocate):**
I spent eighteen months cleaning up after a manager whose formal reviews looked fine too. Glowing references are the least informative artifact here — they're the one document the candidate got to shape, directly or through who they chose to list. An internal investigation into treatment of junior staff, still open when someone leaves? That's a meaningful signal on its own, whatever it eventually concluded. Leaving mid-investigation is itself the data point.

Here's what I reject: the idea that doing nothing is the safe, neutral choice. It isn't. It just moves the risk onto the most junior, least powerful people on the future team — the ones without a colleague willing to burn trust to warn the next manager. My obligation runs to them first. Not because the candidate doesn't matter, but because the candidate has leverage here and the reports don't.

**Theo (fairness / due-process skeptic):**
Let's be precise about what we actually have. One person, speaking secondhand, asking not to be named, admitting up front she doesn't know how it ended. I've sat through investigations opened on a disgruntled peer's complaint that closed with full exoneration, and I've watched "there was an investigation once" get deployed as a weapon in ugly exits, with zero relation to what actually happened. That's not a pattern — it's a rumor wearing a job title. Meanwhile you have named references, people staking their own credibility on specific praise, checkable if you actually call them instead of skimming the letter. Don't let unverifiable beat verifiable just because unverifiable feels juicier.

My real worry is what happens next: once you "know" this, you'll unconsciously fish for it in the interview — leading questions, reading neutral answers as confirmation. That's how you end up convicting someone on an investigation that may well have cleared them.

**Wren (talent-acquisition pragmatist):**
Both camps are debating in the abstract. "The references are glowing, trust them" ignores that formal references are curated by the candidate — they're close to worthless for this specific question. "This is a red flag, be wary" just means stewing in suspicion with nothing to do about it. Neither is an action. Here's the actual move: go around the reference list. Back-channel two or three people who worked under this person but weren't nominated — LinkedIn tells you who reported to them. Run structured behavioral questions on management style specifically: how they've handled a direct report pushing back, a performance conversation gone sideways, ask it three different ways and listen for inconsistency. Then sit down with the candidate and ask, open-ended, why they left. Watch how they handle it unscripted. None of that cites my colleague or the investigation. It's just doing the diligence I should be doing anyway.

### Council Synthesis

**Areas of Convergence:**
- The tip itself never gets cited, written down, or named to anyone — not to HR, not to legal, not to the candidate. It functions only as a private prompt to look harder, never as evidence.
- The response is a structured, identical diligence step, run for *every* finalist, not just this candidate: back-channel references outside the submitted list, behavioral questions specifically about handling conflict with less-powerful people (asked more than one way), and a direct, open-ended question to the candidate about why they left.
- Uniformity is what does the protective work: it blocks the rumor from silently tilting the decision (Renata's and Theo's fear) while blocking "can't verify it" from becoming an excuse to look away (Jordan's fear).
- Nobody on the council would confront the candidate with the accusation, name the colleague to HR/legal, or let the tip alone decide the outcome either way.

**Remaining Disagreements:**
- How much the bare fact of "left mid-investigation" is worth on its own. Jordan treats it as real behavioral signal independent of outcome; Theo and Wren treat it as an inference no more trustworthy than the rumor it came from — investigations end in exoneration too, and people leave jobs for unrelated reasons.
- What happens if the structured process comes back ambiguous. Jordan wants ties to break toward caution, arguing the harms are asymmetric — a false negative costs one candidate a job, a false positive costs a future junior employee their safety. Renata, Theo, and Wren don't sign on to a built-in thumb on the scale; Theo explicitly wants the named references weighted as real signal, not discounted.
- Whether "I'd run this process regardless of the tip" is fully honest. Wren and Renata frame the new rigor as tip-blind good practice; Jordan calls that a polite fiction — the tip is the actual reason the scrutiny is happening now, even if the method stays evidence-blind. Unresolved.
- Theo's proposal to keep whoever heard the rumor off the interview panel wasn't taken up by the others.

**Recommended Path:**

*To your colleague:* keep the confidence exactly as asked. Her name, her account, and the fact she told you anything at all stay out of any file, any HR conversation, any interview. If you loop in HR or legal, frame it as "I want to add a structured diligence step to this hire" — never as "someone told me X."

*To the candidate:* what's owed is a decision made on checkable, uniform criteria, not a verdict on a charge they never get to hear or answer. Concretely: the same reference depth and the same behavioral questions you'd want for any finalist, plus a real, open-ended chance to explain their own account of why they left. It also means not letting glowing formal references function as an automatic pass — the council agreed those are weak evidence precisely because the candidate chose who wrote them.

*To the people who'd report to them:* the duty of care is real, and it's discharged by doing more work, not by quietly marking the candidate down on a tip nobody can name. Go find independent, checkable information about how this person actually treats people with less power than them.

*What's realistically doable:* back-channel two or three references who worked under the candidate but weren't on the submitted list; ask every finalist for the role — not just this one — structured, scenario-based questions about handling pushback, tough performance conversations, and conflict with junior staff, phrased more than one way; ask the candidate directly and open-endedly why they left, and pay attention to how they handle an unscripted question about their own record. Base the actual call on what that process turns up. Silence from it isn't proof of nothing, but it's what you can defensibly act on — which an anonymous, unverifiable, outcome-unknown tip never will be. What it can't become is an accusation, a paper trail naming your colleague, or a private asterisk you carry into every interaction with this person if they're hired.

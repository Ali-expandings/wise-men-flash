## Recommendation

Don't act on the tip directly — don't reject the candidate over it, don't confront them with it, don't write it down or name the source — but do let it push you toward real, uniform diligence on the one dimension formal references are worst at testing: how this person treats people with less power than them. Decide only on what that diligence turns up, and build in the same safeguards you'd want for any new manager if you hire them.

## Why

- **Glowing references are weak evidence on exactly this question.** All three reviewers converged here independently: candidates choose their own references, severance and non-disparagement terms routinely soften what a former employer will say, and reference calls almost never ask pointedly about how someone treated the people below them. "Clean references" and "the tip is accurate" aren't in tension — they're what you'd see either way.
- **The tip can change what you look at, not what you conclude.** It's secondhand, outcome-unknown, and explicitly off the record. Using it to reject, tie-break, or document anything turns a private favor from a colleague into either a decision you can't defend to the candidate — a setup for a pretext claim once anyone asks why your top-ranked candidate didn't get the job — or a leak that exposes the person who trusted you not to repeat it. Every reviewer flagged this as the riskiest move on the table, on both fairness and liability grounds.
- **Doing nothing isn't neutral either.** It delegates the mistreatment question to a reference-check process that isn't built to answer it, and puts the downside entirely on the people with the least power in this story — the future reports who never got a vote. If a pattern repeats, "the references were glowing" is a defense that ages badly against "a colleague warned you and you didn't even look."
- **The realistic middle path is uniform, generic diligence — applied to every finalist, not just this one.** Structured behavioral questions on management style and conflict-handling, an open non-accusatory question about the departure itself, and references expanded to include a former direct report rather than only the candidate's chosen list, scored against criteria set before anyone talks to anyone. This probes the real question without ever citing the allegation, and it only works if it's standard practice — singling this candidate out both tips your hand and is unfair on its own terms.

## Tradeoffs and risks

- **Consensus across all three reviewers:** never attribute, record, or repeat the tip; never let it be the stated or actual deciding factor; widen diligence for all finalists rather than investigating this candidate specifically; whatever you decide, build standard post-hire safeguards — skip-levels with new reports, an early upward-feedback pulse, real substance in the probation review — into this hire regardless of outcome. That's the backstop for the error you can't fully engineer away.
- **One split worth knowing about:** the duty-and-risk review argues you should go back to your colleague first — ask how she knows, tell her plainly you won't name her or write anything down but can't promise confidentiality survives a subpoena, and ask whether she'd consent to the content (never her name) going further. Neither other reviewer raised this step, but it's a good one: one slightly awkward conversation tells you whether you're weighing a first-hand account or a garbled rumor, without committing you to anything.
- **A real cost, not just a risk:** extended diligence and any pause cost calendar time in a live hiring market — you may lose this candidate or your second choice to a faster offer. That's a genuine price for doing this carefully, not a hypothetical one.
- **A caution the panel raised against itself:** an unverified secondhand claim is also a weak basis for suspicion, not just a weak basis for clearance. People who manage assertively, or who came out of a bad process, get reported on too; a complaint isn't a finding, and "left mid-investigation" is consistent with being cleared, being pushed out unfairly, or being genuinely in the wrong — you don't get to know which. Whatever you design has to survive being wrong about the tip, not just being right about it.
- **What stays unresolvable no matter what you do:** whether the allegation is true, what the investigation would have found, and whether your own judgment stays unprimed now that you know this. All three reviewers were confident in the overall shape — diligence over accusation, uniform over targeted, safeguards regardless of outcome — and explicitly flagged that any legal-sounding point here (negligent hiring, defamation, pretext) is general knowledge, not jurisdiction-specific legal advice; get real counsel before formalizing any new interview question or reference-check policy.

## Final call

Do now, before making an offer: have the one careful, consent-seeking conversation with your colleague; add identical structured departure and management-style questions plus one added direct-report reference to every finalist's process, not just this candidate's; keep the tip itself out of every note, panel discussion, and stated reason. Decide on what that turns up — clean, hire; something independently citable, act on that instead of the original tip.

Do regardless of outcome, as standard practice rather than special handling: build skip-level check-ins with new reports and an early upward-feedback pulse into the first 90 days of whoever becomes team lead. That's the piece that actually protects the junior staff this decision is about, and it's the one lever you still have after the hiring decision is made.

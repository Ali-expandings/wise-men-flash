**Round 1**

❓ **Q1** - **Runway**: You call them your "top candidate" but don't say whether an offer's out yet. Every move available to you (extra reference, extra interview question, more time) only exists if the process isn't already closed.

➡️ Recommended: assume you still have some room — "top candidate" language usually means finalist-stage-before-offer, and as hiring manager you can typically buy a few days without anyone asking why.

✅ Assumed: pre-offer, decision pending. Enough runway for one or two added steps, not enough for an open-ended reinvestigation.

❓ **Q2** - **Confidentiality scope**: Does "don't repeat it" mean *don't let this touch your decision at all*, or *don't attribute it / assert it as fact to anyone*?

➡️ Recommended: take the narrow reading. A promise not to repeat something binds your mouth, not your judgment — you can think about what you know, you just can't pass it on or cite her as a source. The broad reading is also unenforceable (you can't un-know it) and just hides the same bias with no check on it.

✅ Assumed: narrow reading — silence on content and source, not blindness.

---

❓ **Q3** - **Evidentiary weight**: No corroboration, no known outcome, one source — but a source you trust enough that she risked telling you, on exactly the axis (power over junior people) a team-lead role tests hardest.

➡️ Recommended: weight it as a reason to look closer, not a reason to conclude anything. Smoke alarm, not verdict.

✅ Assumed: moderate, action-triggering, non-dispositive.

---

❓ **Q4** - **Baseline duty to future reports**: Independent of whether this tip is true, false, or never resolved — what do you owe anyone who'd report to a new team lead, structurally?

➡️ Recommended: build real oversight into any new manager's first 90 days as standard practice — skip-level check-ins, an escalation path that doesn't run through the new manager, an early explicit read on how reports are experiencing them. Cheap, standard, protects people without singling anyone out.

✅ Assumed: you build this in as normal practice, not as a measure aimed at this candidate.

**Round 2**

❓ **Q5** - **What diligence do you actually add**: Given some runway (Q1) and "look closer, don't conclude" (Q3), what are the concrete next steps?

➡️ Recommended: two moves, both source-blind — (a) a direct, neutral behavioral interview question you'd ask any team-lead finalist ("tell me about a time a junior person pushed back on you, or you had to manage someone out"), and (b) one reference you source yourself — a former direct report via LinkedIn, a mutual contact — not one the candidate hands you, since candidate-supplied references are structurally optimistic.

✅ Assumed: you run both, nothing more invasive.

---

❓ **Q6** - **What the hiring committee hears**: If others are involved in this decision, does the tip — or its existence — enter that room?

➡️ Recommended: no. Frame any added step as standard rigor applied evenly ("we're adding a people-management reference check for all team-lead finalists"), not suspicion about this candidate. Protects your colleague fully, keeps an unproven claim out of any record.

✅ Assumed: committee hears "we tightened this part of the process," never hears the tip existed.

---

❓ **Q7** - **What you say to the candidate**: Do they get to know this exists and respond to it?

➡️ Recommended: no direct disclosure — you can't offer a fair rebuttal to a claim with no content, no source, no outcome; that's pressure, not process. What you owe them instead is a genuinely fair shot: same bar as anyone else, a real question they can answer well or badly on their own merits, a decision driven by what you find — not by what you were told in confidence.

✅ Assumed: tip stays fully undisclosed to the candidate; their fairness comes from a clean process, not a confrontation.

❓ **Q8** - **What you owe your former colleague**: She handed you something risky to say. What do you give back?

➡️ Recommended: thank her, tell her plainly you won't repeat it or name her, don't ask her to formalize it, go on record, or dig further — she already told you she doesn't know how it ended, and pushing turns a private favor into personal exposure for her.

✅ Assumed: a quiet thank-you and a firm boundary, nothing traceable back to her.

❓ **Q12** - **Self-protection**: how do you handle this procedurally, for your own exposure?

➡️ Recommended: never write the original tip down anywhere — no email, no ATS note, no Slack message naming the claim or the source. That's the real leak/exposure risk. ✅ Assumed: zero written trace of the tip; a normal, defensible record of the legitimate process only.

---

**Final Recommendation**

To your former colleague: full protection. Thank her, never name her or repeat the claim, don't ask her to go further — she already gave you more than she had to.

To the candidate: not disclosure of an unattributed, unresolved rumor — that's unfalsifiable pressure, not due process. What they're owed is a genuinely fair, unbiased evaluation: the same bar as any finalist, a real behavioral question on managing junior staff, and a decision built on evidence you can actually name.

To the people who'd report to them: a baseline of structural protection that doesn't depend on this rumor being true — skip-level check-ins, an escalation route that doesn't run through their manager, an early honest read on how the team experiences them. Build it in regardless; it costs little and covers the risk either way.

What you can realistically do with information you can't verify or cite: treat it as a private prompt to look harder, never as a citable reason. Run two source-blind checks — a targeted interview question, and one reference you find yourself instead of one the candidate supplies. Let *that* outcome, not the original tip, drive the call: clean diligence clears the candidate outright; independent corroboration becomes fully actionable on its own footing; the likelier inconclusive result means proceed if the rest of the case is strong, paired with real oversight of how they manage early on. Whatever you decide, keep the original claim out of every written record — it never gets more solid than the day you heard it, so it should never carry more weight than that, either.

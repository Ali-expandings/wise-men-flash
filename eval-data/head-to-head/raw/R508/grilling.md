❓ **Q1** - **Price and financing structure**: What's the asking price relative to the business's actual earnings (not revenue), and how exactly is the purchase financed — how much is savings (down payment) vs. loan, is it an SBA loan, and is any of the price seller-financed or held back?

➡️ Get the price checked against an outside number before treating it as final. Small landscaping companies typically sell around 2.5–3x seller's discretionary earnings (SDE) — higher, up to 4–5x+, for maintenance-heavy operations with a diversified client base that don't depend on the owner personally; lower for project-driven shops the owner runs solo. Have an accountant or appraiser check the price against real discretionary earnings, not the boss's word. Confirm it's structured as an asset purchase (standard for this size, and it keeps old liabilities off you), and check whether an SBA 7(a) loan is in play — those typically need only about 10% down, which would leave far more of "most of our savings" untouched than the current plan implies.

❓ **Q3** - **Testing client loyalty**: Is there a way to find out — not guess — whether clients are loyal to the business or to your boss personally, before you commit?

➡️ This is the highest-leverage unknown, so make it answerable, not fated. Standard red flags: any single client over roughly 20–25% of revenue, or a top five over half of revenue. In landscaping specifically, recurring contracted maintenance tends to carry institutional loyalty; one-off or project work the boss personally sold tends to leave with him. Ask him directly for revenue-by-client and tenure-by-client, and which accounts he personally bids versus which run through the crew/office. Factual and gettable — not a guess.

**Final Recommendation**

The leaning is reasonable. Eight years in, already running the crews, already known to most clients, genuinely motivated — that's a stronger starting position than most people buying a small business ever have. But "reasonable to lean yes" and "ready to sign" are different places, and right now the leaning is running ahead of the diligence: the price hasn't been checked against real earnings, the post-closing reserve is likely thin, the books/bidding gap has no funded plan, client loyalty is a felt guess rather than a measured one, and the boss's actual post-sale commitment hasn't been tested. None of that is a reason to walk away today — it's the reason the decision isn't finished yet.

Walk away if, after running down the above:

1. No independent check (accountant or appraiser) confirms the price against the business's real discretionary earnings — buying on the boss's word alone.
2. After the down payment, there's no real cash cushion left — under roughly 3 months of combined business-and-household burn, with no seasonal slack.
3. The boss won't agree to any shared-risk structure at all — no transition period, no retention-linked holdback or earn-out, no help transferring client relationships. Total unwillingness to carry any of the goodwill risk he's asking you to absorb in full.
4. When actually tested — not guessed — a large share of revenue turns out concentrated in a handful of accounts personally tied to him, with no realistic path to transfer them.
5. There's no funded, concrete plan for the books/bidding/cash-flow gap — "I'll figure it out" instead of a bookkeeper, a course, or a real mentorship commitment from the boss.
6. The partner's nervousness never converts into specific, met conditions. Proceeding over an unresolved "I'm scared" is a relationship risk stacked on top of the business risk, and it doesn't go away just because the deal closes.

Use the year before closing as an explicit diligence-and-negotiation runway, not a waiting period: get the price checked, get the client-level numbers, ask for a transition/earn-out structure, take over a real slice of client-facing work as a live test, and line up the bookkeeping help. Revisit the decision once those come back — rather than deciding today on the parts still unknown.

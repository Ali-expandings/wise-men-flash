## Tradeoffs and risks

- **Client loyalty is the real unknown, and it's testable, not a guess.** Find out who takes renewal calls and who gets the call when something goes wrong — you, or him. Check written contracts vs. handshake/seasonal clients. Look at what happened to revenue the last time he was out for two-plus weeks. If he's the one people call, expect real attrition in year one even with the same crews on the trucks.
- **Money risk exists independent of the business being good.** Committing most of your savings plus a loan to one illiquid asset that's also your only income source means a bad year hits your job and your net worth at the same time, not separately. Structural red flag, not a judgment on this particular business.
- **Bidding is the highest-leverage gap you named.** Books are cheap to outsource fast. Bidding sets your margin on every job, and a bad bid doesn't show up as an obvious mistake — it shows up as a thin year you can't explain. Worth more of your pre-close attention than the books.
- One member (Opus) ran an illustrative example, not your actual numbers: financing a roughly 3x-earnings price mostly with debt can leave loan coverage around 1.4x — and a ~15% revenue dip alone can drop that below 1.0x, where the loan stops covering itself. Treat this as the shape of the risk, not a forecast. Run the real math on his real numbers before signing anything.
- **Your partner's nervousness is data, not an obstacle to manage.** They have as much at stake as you and aren't carrying eight years of insider confidence pulling them toward yes. If they're still nervous after you've done the verification below, that's a second, independent signal worth weighing on its own.

## Walk-away criteria

Walk away, or refuse these terms, if any of these hold:

1. He won't open three years of financials (tax returns, bank statements, revenue by client) to you or a CPA before you commit.
2. The earnings depend on unreported/off-book cash you can't verify.
3. He won't carry any seller financing or agree to any retention-linked earn-out — a seller unwilling to keep skin in the game is telling you something about his own confidence in what transfers.
4. He refuses to let you run point on a real bid season, or handle client renewals, before closing.
5. When you actually check, client by client, most relationships turn out to run through him personally, not through the crews or written contracts.
6. The numbers don't survive a stress test: if a 15–20% revenue drop breaks your ability to cover the loan, payroll, and basic household expenses, the deal is over-leveraged as structured.
7. Your partner is still a no after items 1–4 are done, not before.

None of these are guesses — items 1–4 are yours to demand before you sign anything, not things to find out after.

## Final call

Do now, before any commitment: ask for a full bid season shadowing him (not just crew-running), ask for three years of real financials and a client-concentration breakdown, and have a direct conversation with him about seller financing or an earn-out tied to retention. This costs you time and one awkward conversation, and it turns "I can't tell if clients are loyal to the business or to him" from a guess into an answer.

Optional hardening once the above checks out: an independent valuation or a CPA-led quality-of-earnings review before agreeing on final price, and a written post-close transition period (30–90 days minimum) so you're not learning bidding and books in a vacuum the week he leaves.

If he balks at the verification itself — not the price, the verification — that refusal is your answer. Walk.

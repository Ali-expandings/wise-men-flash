## Recommendation

Your leaning is reasonable as a direction, not yet as a decision. Before any money moves, convert the offer from a price he names into a verified, staged deal — books checked independently, part of his payment at risk on whether clients actually stay, and your walk-away lines agreed with your partner in writing before you negotiate price.

## Why

You already carry the risk least likely to sink this deal: you can run the crews, and clients recognize you. What you can't yet see is exactly the risk that sinks buyouts like this one — his numbers, his pricing judgment, and whether the relationships people have are with the business or with him personally. Notice that every reason you gave for leaning yes describes the job you already do; every gap you named describes the job you'd be taking on. That's not incidental to how you wrote the question — it's the actual shape of the decision: competence at the work you know isn't evidence about the parts you don't.

The client-loyalty question you can't answer is more knowable than it feels. You don't need a new survey — the answer is already sitting in eight years of history: which clients call you when something goes wrong versus who asks for him, which accounts predate you, what happened on the jobs he handled personally (design work, big commercial or HOA contracts, anything he priced himself), and how much revenue sits in a handful of accounts. Whatever that shows, the honest fix isn't to resolve the ambiguity before you decide — it's to make the price depend on the answer, so you're not the only one betting on it.

That's also why "most of our savings plus a bank loan" is the wrong risk to carry alone. A loan sized for this kind of purchase typically comes with a personal guarantee — your household stands behind the business, not just your savings — which is exactly what your partner's nervousness is correctly picking up on. It isn't an obstacle to manage; it's a second, independent read on the same downside you're closer to and less able to see clearly. Loop your partner into the real numbers and agree your limits before you're in a negotiation, not after — that's the only point where their voice has real leverage, because once the money and the guarantee are in, it can't be taken back.

One assumption is doing more work here than it looks like: every way of making this deal safer — a seller note, a holdback, a retention-linked price, a supervised handover before you pay full price — only works if he's willing to defer part of his payout or share the downside if clients don't stay. Nothing here confirms he will. If he needs or wants a clean, all-cash exit, none of these structures are available to you, and the decision gets much starker: pay full price for goodwill you can't verify, or walk away. Ask him this directly, early — his answer tells you which situation you're actually in.

You likely have more leverage in that conversation than "leaning toward yes" suggests. A retiring owner of a small, owner-dependent service business usually has weak alternatives to selling to you — a discounted sale to a competitor, or liquidating the equipment piecemeal. You are probably his easiest, and possibly only good, exit. That's a reason to ask him directly for verified books, a shared-risk structure, and a supervised transition — not a reason to feel grateful for a number he hasn't yet had to defend.

## What to do

Before discussing price: ask him for three years of tax returns and bank statements, plus revenue broken out by named client rather than a summary, and have an independent accountant reconcile the two. From those same records, tag each major account by who actually sells, prices, and handles complaints for it — that answers most of the loyalty question directly, from evidence you already have access to. Alongside the financial check, have a lawyer run the standard checks a business sale needs — liens on the equipment, whether the yard lease and any licenses actually transfer to you, whether anyone on the crew should have been an employee rather than a contractor — problems here don't show up in a P&L but become yours the day you close. In parallel, ask your partner what specific limit would make them comfortable — a floor under your savings, whether your home would ever be on the line, a minimum cash reserve after closing — and agree it in writing before you're negotiating with him.

Once you have real numbers: work out what the business would pay you in a conservative year — assume some of his personal accounts don't renew, and back out a full loan payment, a wage for whoever replaces you on the crews, and normal equipment replacement — and compare that to what you make today. Then propose the structure, not just the price: buy the trucks, equipment, and client list into a new company of your own rather than taking over his existing one, so his debts and liabilities stay his, not yours; hold back or defer part of the payment against which named clients actually stay over the following year; put a non-compete on him; and build in a transition period where you handle bidding and the books while he's still there to correct you. His response to that ask answers the loyalty question for you — a seller confident the business, not himself, is what's valuable has every reason to agree.

## Risks of this plan

Tell him it's over and walk if any of these show up:
- He won't open the real books — tax returns reconciled against bank deposits — to your own accountant, or the price only makes sense if you assume income the records don't show.
- He won't accept any structure where part of his payment depends on clients actually staying, and insists on a clean, all-cash exit with no shared risk at all.
- A handful of accounts — the ones tied to his personal relationships or pricing, not to the crew's work — make up a large share of revenue.
- In a conservative year (assuming some of his personal accounts leave), the business can't comfortably cover both the loan and a wage for the work you're now doing unpaid as owner.
- Closing would leave no cash reserve — for the household or for the business's own slow season.
- Your partner, looking at the real numbers, sets a limit this deal can't meet.

Moving too slowly has a real cost too: if he has a genuine backup buyer or a fixed deadline, asking for verification and a shared-risk structure could cost you the deal, and the alternative — a stranger buying it, or it simply closing — doesn't protect your job or your crew's either. But the two failures aren't symmetric. If you wait and verify and it costs you the deal, you lose an opportunity and keep your savings. If you skip the verification and it goes wrong, you can lose the house deposit along with it — that asymmetry is why verification comes first even though it isn't free.

## Strongest counter-position

The case above still treats this as "a good direction — now go negotiate the details." The stronger challenge to that: the two questions folded into "should I buy this business" don't have the same answer. Whether you should ever own this business is a fair maybe. Whether you should move toward paying a fixed price, funded by most of your household's savings and a bank loan, for a business whose real books you've never seen, whose bids you've never written, and whose clients you can't yet place — that deserves a clean no, not a hedged yes-in-progress. On this reading, running the crews and knowing the clients by name isn't proof you should buy the business — you're already the part of it that works, and a trusted crew lead whom half the clients know has real options of their own (check any non-compete or non-solicit in your employment agreement before you assume how real those options are). That means your leverage is larger than "leaning yes" implies, and the right move this year isn't to begin negotiating a structure — it's to tell him plainly that you're not deciding yet, and that he should spend his last year proving the goodwill is real: let you run next season's bids and books, and introduce you to his key accounts as his successor, before either of you talks price.

This wins if he has no comparably good alternative to you — likely, for a small owner-dependent landscaping business — in which case waiting costs you nothing and gets you everything the staged approach above is trying to buy. It loses if he has a genuine competing buyer or a fixed timeline; then a flat "not this year" could cost you the business rather than improve your terms, and asking for the same verification and shared-risk structure while still actively negotiating is the safer version of the same instinct.

## Confidence

High: verify the real numbers independently and loop your partner into them, in writing, before you negotiate. This holds regardless of what the books turn out to show, and it's the one piece of this you could start this week with no downside.

Medium: that a staged, shared-risk structure — a seller note, a holdback, or a supervised handover before final price — is actually available to you. Every way of making this deal safer assumes your former boss is willing to defer or risk part of his payout, and nothing here confirms that. His answer to that specific ask is the fact that would most change everything else in this plan.

Medium: the exact debt-service cushion a lender will want, and what portion of the price a bank will let a seller defer, vary by lender and loan program — get the current numbers from whoever will actually finance this rather than assuming a figure.

Low, and worth naming rather than guessing at: whether the clients are loyal to the business or to him. That's genuinely unknown from here. It's answerable from records you already have access to, and it's the fact the purchase price should be built around, not resolved before you decide.

## Council Verdict: Full-Text Search — Postgres vs. Dedicated Search Cluster

### Where the Council Agrees
Not one of the five advisors argues for standing up a dedicated or self-hosted cluster right now — not the Outsider, who is most alarmed about the team's inexperience, and not even the Contrarian, whose entire job is puncturing the other four's assumptions. Four of five land on an explicit recommendation to build Postgres full-text search first; the Contrarian alone declines to recommend anything, but never argues for the cluster either. Five independent starting points, one opening move.

Four of five (everyone but the Expansionist) treat "relevance ranking quality degrades past a certain corpus size" as unverified as stated — no source, no number, no test behind it. The Executor calls it a rumor outright. The Outsider asks who reported it and how much it degrades. The First Principles Thinker points out Postgres FTS runs at tens of millions of rows elsewhere, so 4 million isn't self-evidently "past" anything. Three independent advisors reaching for the same conclusion before seeing each other's work is a signal, not a coincidence.

Where they land is sharper than "unproven": it's a permanent ceiling, not a scale cliff. ts_rank is a crude term-frequency scorer; it was never BM25. That gap exists at 400,000 documents as much as at 4 million — it just gets louder as query volume and corpus diversity grow. Three of five peer reviewers independently picked this reframe as the strongest single insight in the entire set.

The council also agrees the brief drew Option B too narrowly. "Dedicated search cluster" quietly means "self-hosted cluster you operate" — but a managed service (Elastic Cloud, OpenSearch Service, Typesense Cloud, Algolia) is a different bet with a different risk profile, and it's the self-hosted version that should actually worry a five-person team with zero search-ops experience. The Executor, Contrarian, and First Principles Thinker all draw this line explicitly.

And there's agreement on method: stop arguing, test it. Load the real 4-million-document corpus, run real representative queries, see what the ranking actually looks like. If it holds up, done. If it doesn't, the agreed next step is a managed service or a BM25-grade Postgres extension like ParadeDB's pg_search — not a self-hosted cluster.

### Where the Council Clashes
The real fault line isn't Postgres-versus-cluster — everyone lands on Postgres for now. It's how much confidence the unverified relevance claim deserves, and the Expansionist stands alone against the other four. Where they treat the claim as unproven-but-worth-testing, the Expansionist treats it as already debunked — "inherited folklore," "a tuning problem, not a wall" — and builds a forward-looking case on that dismissal: live joins against permissions, inventory, and pricing with zero replication lag, and pgvector sitting beside full-text for hybrid search later. That architectural point is genuinely new — nobody else raised it — though one reviewer punctures a specific overreach in it: Elasticsearch and OpenSearch also support document-level security and denormalized enrichment, so the claim that competitors "structurally can't ship this" doesn't hold up.

What makes this clash worth naming is what happened in peer review: one reviewer called the Expansionist's response the single strongest in the set, precisely for that original architectural thinking. All five reviewers, independently and without exception, also flagged it as the biggest blind spot in the set — for the same reason, an unquantified claim asserted with more conviction than evidence. That's the most unanimous result anywhere in this process, and it lands on the one response that is simultaneously the most valuable and the least trustworthy.

A quieter second clash: the Outsider treats team inexperience as the whole decision — one of five engineers becomes an accidental search-cluster operator, learning by outage, with data drift compounding a skill nobody has. Real argument. But it never mentions managed search, which is exactly the option that defuses most of it. "Zero search-ops experience" is a much smaller problem if nobody on the team ever touches a shard count or a JVM heap flag.

### Blind Spots the Council Caught
The single most-repeated miss, caught by three reviewers independently: resource contention. Full-text search on the production Postgres instance means GIN index scans and ts_rank computation on every query, plus GIN write-side maintenance on every document ingested — all competing for the same CPU and I/O as the live application traffic that instance already serves. "One fewer system to run" quietly assumes that capacity is free. Not one advisor proposed a read replica or an isolated connection pool for search load.

Second: the council's own fix has the same flaw as the brief it's criticizing. "Benchmark it, run real queries, look at the results" was the near-universal answer to an unquantified claim — but "look at the results" is an eyeball test, not a measurement. No one proposed an actual relevance metric (precision@k, labeled judgments, NDCG), and no one set a numeric pass/fail bar for what the benchmark would need to show to count as a pass.

Third: nobody asked how often these documents get written or updated. That number drives the actual cost on both paths — GIN maintenance overhead on ingest for Option A, drift severity and sync lag for Option B — and it never came up.

Fourth, the sharpest catch: the Expansionist's confident dismissal of the ranking risk is the same failure mode as the brief's original unsourced claim, just pointed the other way. An assertion with no citation and no proposed test isn't more credible for being optimistic instead of cautious.

Two smaller catches: nobody proposed hiring or contracting search expertise to close the team's skill gap directly, even though that gap is the Outsider's entire case. And nobody priced either path in dollars, despite cost being half of what "operational burden" usually means.

### The Recommendation
Build full-text search in Postgres. Not as the cautious fallback while the team works up to something better — as the correct call on the evidence actually in front of you. The one specific risk cited against it is unsourced, and the council's best technical read is that it's mischaracterized besides: a permanent ranking-quality ceiling that exists at any corpus size, not a cliff that appears at 4 million documents. The cost on the other side isn't hypothetical: five engineers with no search-cluster experience would take on shard and replica sizing, a sync pipeline that can silently drift from the source of truth, and a second on-call surface, starting from zero. One of those risks is a rumor. The other is a certainty no matter how it turns out.

Treat it as a two-step decision, not a one-shot bet. Step one: implement it — generated tsvector column, GIN index, tuned ts_rank_cd — and benchmark it properly against the real corpus and real queries, scored with an actual relevance metric instead of a vibes check. Step two, only if step one fails on that evidence: escalate to a managed search service (Elastic Cloud, OpenSearch Service, Typesense Cloud) or a BM25-grade Postgres extension like ParadeDB's pg_search — both keep the team out of the business of operating distributed infrastructure from scratch. Self-hosting a cluster isn't the next step even in the failure case; it's a separate, later decision, made with far more evidence than exists today, and only after managed search has been tried and specifically found wanting.

Keep the Expansionist's upside case — live joins against permissions and live data, pgvector next to full-text for hybrid search down the road — as a genuine bonus reason to prefer Postgres. Just don't let it be the reason. It was argued with more conviction than evidence, and this decision shouldn't lean on that claim any more than it should lean on the unsourced one on the other side.

### The One Thing to Do First
Load the real 4-million-document corpus into a tsvector/GIN implementation, run it against a real sample of actual user queries, and score the results with an actual relevance metric — precision@k or labeled judgments on a sample, not eyeballing the output. That single step replaces the entire debate with evidence. It's the one fact nobody in this council has, four of five advisors converged on needing it, and doing it with a real metric — which every advisor forgot to specify — is what turns "we think Postgres is fine" into "we know."

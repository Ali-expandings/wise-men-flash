## Recommendation

Don't decide from the size number alone — benchmark Postgres full-text search against your own query patterns for 1-3 engineer-days before committing to either path. Build a weighted `tsvector` column with a GIN index and run your real (or logged) queries against a production data restore, measuring p95 latency and spot-checking top-10 relevance on multi-term queries. If it clears a latency/relevance bar you set in advance, ship it, behind a small search interface that makes a later swap contained rather than a rewrite. If it doesn't, move to a managed dedicated search service rather than a self-hosted cluster.

## Why

4M documents is not, by itself, past Postgres's range — GIN-indexed full-text search routinely handles corpora many times this size. What actually determines whether ranking holds up is two things the document count doesn't tell you: how many documents a typical query's terms match (Postgres scores and sorts the full matching set before applying your result limit, so cost tracks match count, not corpus size), and how long your documents are (`tsvector` silently loses position data past 16,383 lexeme positions and fails outright past 1MB, which degrades ranking precisely on your longest documents). Separately from size, Postgres's built-in `ts_rank` has no inverse-document-frequency term at any scale — it can rank a document that repeats a common word ahead of the one document that's actually about a rare, specific term. That's a real limitation, but it's a scoring-algorithm gap, not something that gets worse as you add more documents.

The framing of the choice also collapses two different things into "dedicated search cluster": using a real search engine, and running one yourself. A five-person team with no search-cluster experience is a strong argument against self-hosting Elasticsearch or OpenSearch — the standard failure mode is a disk watermark crossed at 95%, indices flipping read-only, and a 3am page, plus an ongoing operational tax on one engineer indefinitely. It is not, by itself, an argument against using a managed search service (Elastic Cloud, OpenSearch Serverless, Typesense/Meilisearch's own cloud offerings, Algolia), which gives you real BM25 relevance while shard sizing, heap tuning, and failure recovery stay on the vendor. At roughly 4M documents you'd likely sit in these services' cheaper tiers (typical low hundreds of dollars per month, not a large system-design cost) — treat that as a ballpark, not a quote.

There's also a third path your framing leaves out entirely: a BM25-capable Postgres extension (ParadeDB's pg_search is the current example) can close the relevance gap while staying in one system, avoiding the second-copy problem altogether. It's worth 15 minutes to check whether your hosting provider's extension allowlist permits it (unverified — most managed Postgres offerings restrict non-core extensions, but this is specific to your provider and needs a direct check) before ruling it in or out.

On drift: it's a real cost of a second copy, but it's a bounded and solved one — an outbox or CDC feed plus a periodic checksum reconciliation job (comparing `id`/`updated_at` between the two stores) catches and repairs it, and a full reindex of 4M documents is a background job measured in minutes to a couple of hours. Teams that get burned by drift are the ones who dual-write from request handlers and skip the reconciler, regardless of which engine sits on the other side. It is a real, ongoing piece of infrastructure to build and own, but not the open-ended risk "can drift" implies on its own.

## What to do

First step (1-3 engineer-days): add a `GENERATED ALWAYS AS (...) STORED` `tsvector` column weighted by field (title vs. body), build it with `CREATE INDEX CONCURRENTLY ... USING gin` (raise `maintenance_work_mem` for the build), and parse user input with `websearch_to_tsquery` rather than `to_tsquery` (the latter throws on raw input). Replay real or logged queries against a production data restore and measure: p95 latency, and match-count distribution per query (what fraction of 4M rows typical queries hit) — this second number, not the document count, is the one that predicts whether you'll have a problem. In parallel, spend 15 minutes confirming whether a BM25-in-Postgres extension is installable on your actual hosting platform, in case it's needed.

Set your bar before you look at the results (e.g., p95 under ~300ms, and multi-term relevance that looks right on manual spot-check). If Postgres clears it, ship it behind one `search(query, filters, limit)` interface so the rest of the app doesn't know which engine answers it — this is what keeps a later swap a contained change instead of a rewrite. If it doesn't clear the bar, or your product roadmap already requires things Postgres structurally can't do well (faceted counts at scale, typo tolerance, sub-50ms as-you-type, heavy multilingual tokenization), move to a managed dedicated search service — not a self-hosted cluster — paired with an outbox feed and a scheduled reconciliation job from day one.

## Risks of this plan

- A benchmark against a stale data snapshot or a hand-picked query sample (instead of real logged queries) will give false confidence in either direction — use production-representative data and queries.
- Shipping Postgres FTS without the search-interface abstraction turns a later escalation into a rewrite instead of a swap; build the seam even if you're confident you won't need it.
- The BM25-in-Postgres extension path is the least proven of the three options — smaller ecosystem, uncertain production maturity, and it may not even be installable on your specific managed Postgres provider — do not commit to it without a direct check.
- Sharing your OLTP and search workload on one Postgres instance can starve transactional queries under search load; the usual fix is a read replica, which quietly reintroduces a second copy of the data even on the "one less system" path.
- Cost of waiting: the benchmark is 1-3 days against a decision that's expensive to get wrong in either direction — weeks of unnecessary cluster-operations overhead if you over-provision a dedicated system you didn't need, or a painful mid-flight migration off Postgres, done under more load and less slack than you have today, if you under-provision. The few days of measurement is cheap insurance against either.

## Strongest counter-position

The case for skipping the Postgres benchmark entirely and going straight to a managed dedicated search service: "A relevance ceiling in native FTS is discovered nine months in, after you've built product surface on it, and the only exit is exactly the migration you were avoiding, now with more users and less slack" — whereas index drift "is detectable, bounded, and repairable" by a reconciliation job. On this view, the two risks this decision balances aren't symmetric: a Postgres ceiling hit late costs more than drift caught early, so the benchmark's few days of savings aren't worth the tail risk. This view also holds that "the complexity you actually can't afford is cluster operations, and that is precisely the part you can buy" — meaning the team's lack of search-cluster experience, the reason Postgres looks safer, stops being relevant once you're buying a managed service instead of running your own. This case wins outright if your product roadmap already commits to things Postgres can't do well regardless of tuning (faceted counts at scale, typo tolerance, sub-50ms as-you-type, heavy multilingual search) — at that point the benchmark is a delay, not a decision aid, and the right move is to go straight to managed dedicated search.

High that 4M documents alone is not the constraint, and that query-match-count and document length are what actually determine whether Postgres ranking holds up — this follows from how `tsvector`/GIN/`ts_rank` mechanically work, not from a size threshold. Low / unverified on whether a BM25-in-Postgres extension is a live option here — it depends on your hosting provider's extension policy and the extension's production maturity, neither of which is checkable from the question as asked.

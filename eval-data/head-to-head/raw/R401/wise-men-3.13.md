## Recommendation

Extend Postgres now, but don't decide based on "relevance gets bad past a certain size" — that's folklore, not a documented threshold. Spend the first week measuring real query latency and relevance quality against your actual corpus and query logs, and let that decide whether to stop at tuned Postgres, add a same-database ranking extension, or bring in a dedicated engine. If a dedicated engine turns out to be necessary, prefer a managed service — a team with no cluster experience shouldn't be operating Elasticsearch or OpenSearch itself — though a self-hosted single-binary engine like Typesense or Meilisearch is a much smaller, defensible exception.

## Why

"Relevance ranking gets bad past a certain size" conflates two separate, well-understood mechanisms, and neither one is really about size. Postgres's built-in ranking (`ts_rank`/`ts_rank_cd`) scores each document only from its own contents — term frequency, position, and weight class — with no corpus-wide term-rarity weighting. BM25-family engines (Elasticsearch, OpenSearch, and BM25-scoring extensions) explicitly discount common terms based on how many documents in the whole corpus contain them. That's a fixed qualitative gap in Postgres's default ranking, not a threshold you cross — its damage becomes more visible as vocabulary gets broader, which happens to correlate with corpus size, which is where the folklore comes from. Separately, Postgres's index (GIN) has no way to retrieve results in rank order — it must fetch and score every matching row before applying a limit, so query cost is driven by how many rows a given query matches, not by how many documents exist in total. A narrow query against 4 million documents can be fast indefinitely; a broad, common-word query is the one to worry about, and that same query shape would cause the identical problem at a much smaller corpus too — the mechanism has nothing to do with crossing 4 million specifically.

The team's own stated constraint — five people, nobody has operated a search cluster — is real and is the strongest single argument in this whole decision, but it's an argument against self-hosting a cluster, not against search technology in general. A managed search service removes exactly that risk while keeping the option open. And the "second copy that can drift" concern is real, but its sharpest edge isn't staleness — if these documents have any per-user or per-tenant access control, a sync that mirrors content but not permission changes drifts toward over-permissive, silently, which is a confidentiality problem, not just an out-of-date one.

Finally, waiting for the ranking problem to "become a measurable complaint" before acting on it is a weaker plan than it sounds, because bad relevance rarely produces an alert, an incident, or even a support ticket — it shows up as users quietly not using search, which is easy to never notice. That's a reason to measure deliberately and immediately, not a reason to skip Postgres and jump straight to a dedicated engine.

## What to do

1. **This week**: pull your real query patterns (query logs, or whatever proxy exists — even your current search-adjacent feature). Identify roughly your top 100-200 real queries, and separate the broad/common-word ones from the narrow/specific ones. At the same time, check whether a meaningful share of the corpus is in Chinese, Japanese, or Arabic — Postgres's default search configuration does not word-segment CJK text and handles Arabic normalization and diacritics poorly. If a meaningful share of your documents are in one of these languages, that alone can outweigh the rest of this plan and points toward a dedicated engine regardless of what the rest of the measurement shows.
2. **This week, in parallel**: run those queries against a Postgres `tsvector` + GIN index built on your real ~4M-document corpus, and measure two things directly: (a) how many rows each query actually matches (a match-cardinality histogram — this answers the latency question), and (b) real p50/p95/p99 latency using `websearch_to_tsquery`, ranking and highlighting only the already-limited result page, not the full match set.
3. **This week, in parallel**: build a small relevance harness — about 50 real queries, two people independently score the top 10 results as good or bad, compute precision@10. This is the step that tells you whether ranking quality is actually a problem here, instead of assuming it from document count.
4. **Before deciding**, gather a short list of facts that change the answer independent of the measurement above: whether full-text search is a core, load-bearing feature of what these documents are for or a secondary/internal capability (ask whoever owns the product — this changes the bar for "good enough" more than any measurement will); your Postgres major version and the total/average size of the indexed text (affects index size and whether it stays cached in memory); how often searchable fields are updated (affects index upkeep and how much sync lag you could tolerate with a second system); and whether faceted search, typo tolerance, or synonym handling are actual stated product requirements — each is, on its own, a legitimate reason to prefer a dedicated engine regardless of what the relevance and latency measurements show.
5. **Decide on the evidence**: if latency and precision@10 both hold up against your real queries — including your broadest, highest-match-count ones — and none of the flip-risk facts above apply, stay on Postgres. That's a fully legitimate outcome for many teams at this scale, not a compromise. If precision@10 specifically fails on multi-word or common-term queries, check with your Postgres hosting provider whether a BM25-scoring extension is available and production-ready before reaching for a second system — availability varies by provider and must be checked directly, not assumed. If neither tuned Postgres nor an in-database extension clears the bar, or one of the flip-risk facts applies, add a dedicated engine.
6. **If a dedicated engine is needed**: make a managed service your default choice given the team's inexperience. A self-hosted single-binary engine like Typesense or Meilisearch is a defensible exception — it needs no JVM tuning or cluster coordination and is a fundamentally smaller operational commitment than Elasticsearch or OpenSearch — but don't self-host an ES/OpenSearch-class system with nobody on the team who has run one before.
7. **Regardless of which path you choose**: keep permission-checking inside Postgres. If you ever add a second index — extension or external engine — have it return candidate document IDs ranked by relevance, and do the actual permission-checked fetch of content back in Postgres. That turns "the copy can drift" into, at worst, a stale result, not a confidentiality leak, and it's worth doing even if you stay on plain Postgres.
8. **If you do add a dedicated engine**: build the operational minimum before the first real document syncs — idempotent upserts, delete/tombstone propagation, a scheduled full reindex from Postgres with its wall-clock time measured and written down, a lag metric with an alert, and a flag to fall back to Postgres search in a single deploy.

## Risks of this plan

- Measuring with a convenient or curated query set instead of real production query logs can produce a false pass: the failure mode here is driven by specific query shapes (broad, common-term queries) and match-set size, not average-case behavior or raw document count. The measurement has to deliberately include your worst-case, highest-match-count real queries, or it will tell you nothing useful.
- If the measurement is skipped or done informally, and the team instead just waits to see if users complain, the signal may never arrive even when it should — mediocre relevance rarely produces an incident or a ticket, it just looks like quiet non-use of the search box. That's the real cost of waiting, more than any specific timeline.
- If a cluster ends up self-hosted despite the team's inexperience, the highest-risk window is typically the first several months to roughly a year and a half of operation, and the characteristic failures (disk-space-triggered lockouts, JVM/heap tuning, a forced reindex after a mapping change) are exactly the kind of problem a team with no dedicated search operator is least equipped to diagnose quickly.
- Skipping the ID-only permission architecture in favor of a simpler sync means a pipeline that mirrors document content but not permission changes will drift toward over-permissive, silently — a confidentiality bug that can go undetected far longer than a relevance complaint would.
- Running search on the same Postgres instance as transactional traffic means a slow or heavy search query can degrade unrelated application performance, with no isolation — true whether or not a second system is ever added.
- Cost of being wrong: this is not truly irreversible in either direction at this corpus size — a derived search index is disposable and rebuildable, and a Postgres-only setup can be extended or migrated later — but every path costs real engineer time (roughly one to two engineer-weeks, as a rough planning estimate, to do Postgres properly; roughly three to five for a dedicated engine with the operational minimum in place — unverified estimates, treat as a planning ballpark, not a quote). The more expensive mistake is skipping the measurement and guessing in either direction, not picking the "wrong" technology per se.

## Strongest counter-position

The case for skipping the measure-first sequencing and going straight to a managed (not self-hosted) dedicated search service now: "dedicated search cluster" is not the same thing as "we operate a cluster" — buying managed search deletes the exact hazard the entire stay-on-Postgres argument rests on. Once self-operating is off the table, the comparison is no longer "simple system vs. risky system," it's "bolt-on ranking vs. purpose-built ranking," which is not close on quality. The sharpest part of this case is an asymmetry: cluster failures are loud and produce visible incidents and postmortems, while Postgres relevance failures are silent — they surface only as users quietly abandoning the search box, never as an alert. That means the public record of teams reporting "Postgres full-text was enough for us" is systematically enriched for teams whose search was never load-bearing to begin with, and reading your own risk off that reassuring sample is a mistake.

This case wins outright if either of two things is true: full-text search is actually core to what these four million documents are for — a real search product rather than an internal lookup — or the team is unlikely to run the proposed measurement rigorously (real production query logs, deliberately including the broadest and highest-match-count queries, graded by two people) rather than defaulting to an informal test that can produce a false pass. Under either condition, the cost of a wrong "stay on Postgres" call compounds silently, and managed search removes the one risk — self-operating unfamiliar infrastructure — that justified staying on Postgres in the first place.

## Confidence

High — the ranking and latency mechanism explained above; this is a matter of established Postgres/search-engine internals, not something that depends on your specific data.

High — a five-person team with no prior search-cluster operating experience is a real, present-tense risk for self-hosting, not a hypothetical one.

Medium — whether Postgres, tuned and possibly extended, ends up being good enough here. This depends on your real query-shape distribution and relevance expectations, neither of which is stated, and should be treated as medium confidence until measured.

Material unknowns, most important first: (1) whether a meaningful share of the corpus is in Chinese, Japanese, or Arabic, which Postgres's default configuration handles badly and which can outweigh every other finding in this plan — check this first; (2) whether full-text search is a load-bearing, core product surface for these documents or a secondary/internal capability — likely the next most decision-changing fact, and it isn't stated anywhere in the question; (3) whether your Postgres hosting provider allows installing a BM25-scoring extension such as ParadeDB's pg_search or a similar option (unverified — check your provider's current extension allowlist directly); (4) whether these documents carry per-user or per-tenant access control (unverified — changes how much weight the drift-as-authorization-risk finding above deserves); (5) the real distribution of query shapes your users run, and whether facets, typo tolerance, or synonym handling are actual product requirements — together these determine whether the mechanisms above actually bite at your scale.

## How seriously to take it

Seriously enough to raise with her doctor. Not seriously enough to panic, and not seriously enough to act on your own. A 30% relative-risk increase out of a multi-million-record study sits exactly in the range where a real drug effect and pure confounding produce identical-looking numbers — that's not a dodge, it's the honest state of this literature. This pattern almost certainly traces back to a well-known 2016 German claims-data study (large insurer dataset, adults 75+) that first raised the PPI-dementia signal, followed by roughly a decade of attempted replications with mixed results — some databases reproduce an association, others don't, and nobody has produced the dose-response curve, the mechanism, or the trial-level evidence that would upgrade "statistical association" to "this drug causes dementia." No major gastroenterology or neurology body has changed prescribing guidance off this signal alone.

## Why records-based studies like this go wrong

- **Confounding by indication.** People on a reflux drug for a decade are sicker on average than people who aren't — more comorbidity, more polypharmacy, more contact with the health system. Any of that can drive dementia risk on its own. Adjusting for the comorbidities researchers thought to code for never fully closes this gap.
- **Reverse causation.** Dementia has a prodromal phase lasting years before diagnosis. Undiagnosed early cognitive decline changes eating, self-care, and symptom reporting in ways that can increase acid-reducer prescribing — the "exposure" can be an early symptom of the disease rather than a cause of it.
- **Detection bias.** Patients who see a doctor regularly enough to stay on a chronic prescription get screened more. More contact with the system means more chances to catch a diagnosis, independent of the drug.
- **Immortal time / survivorship bias.** To count as a "5+ year user," someone has to survive, stay in the dataset, and remain undiagnosed for 5+ years. That requirement alone can distort who lands in each comparison group.
- **Missing confounders.** Claims and EHR data usually don't capture education, diet, exercise, smoking history in detail, or genetic risk (APOE4 status) — all independently tied to dementia. No statistical adjustment fixes a variable that was never recorded.
- **Comparator choice.** Studies comparing PPI users to non-users are really comparing "has reflux disease and treats it" to "doesn't have reflux disease" — two different populations before the drug enters the picture. Studies that instead compare PPIs against another acid-suppressing drug (H2 blockers), which holds "has reflux disease" constant, tend to show a smaller or null effect. Worth knowing which design the specific study used.
- **Multiple-testing / fishing.** Large claims databases make it cheap to test hundreds of drug-outcome pairs. A striking single result, especially one that emerged from a database built for something else, deserves more skepticism than a result from a hypothesis specified in advance.
- **Coverage bias.** The scary finding gets the headline; the null or contradicting replications rarely do. Public sense of "the evidence" is shaped by what got covered, not by the full record.

**What would move this from "signal" to "convincing":** a dose-response relationship, replication across independent populations, persistence when PPIs are compared against another acid-reducing drug instead of against non-users, and a corroborating mechanism (reduced amyloid clearance, B12 deficiency, gut-microbiome effects — all proposed, none confirmed). Absent those, "30% more likely" in one large records study is a hypothesis worth tracking, not a verdict.

## For your mother specifically

The stronger reason to review a decade-long PPI habit isn't the dementia study — it's the better-established downside list for long-term PPI use: fracture risk, B12 and magnesium deficiency, an association with chronic kidney disease, higher risk of C. diff and other enteric infections, and rebound acid hypersecretion if the drug is stopped abruptly. Standard deprescribing guidance already recommends periodically checking whether someone still needs daily long-term therapy, at the lowest effective dose, regardless of this dementia signal. That gives you a way to act that's grounded in firmer evidence than the study you read.

Practically: don't have her stop on her own, and don't lead with "this might be giving you dementia" — that claim rests on the weakest part of the evidence, and untreated reflux carries its own real risks (esophagitis, strictures, Barrett's esophagus), so panic-driven stopping can trade a speculative risk for a concrete one. Instead, raise it at her next appointment as a routine medication check: is she still on the lowest dose that controls symptoms, does she still need daily therapy or could she step down to on-demand dosing or an H2 blocker, and has anyone revisited why she started it 10+ years ago. That accomplishes the same protective goal without the alarm, and it doesn't require resolving a contested causal question first.

## Why

- **What this study type can and can't do.** A multi-million-person health-records study is good at flagging population-scale patterns cheaply; it cannot, by itself, prove the drug causes dementia. For this exact kind of claim ("long duration of drug X → ~30% higher dementia diagnosis rate in records data"), the three usual failure modes are confounding by indication (decade-long users tend to be older and sicker in ways that independently raise dementia risk), reverse causation (undiagnosed early dementia can change stomach symptoms and prescribing patterns years before diagnosis), and detection bias (more prescription refills means more doctor contact means more chances for a dementia diagnosis to get made and coded).
- **The evidence is genuinely split, not settled.** Several large cohort studies (different countries, different databases) report a duration-linked association going back to the mid-2010s European claims-data work, since echoed in other national registries. But the designs built specifically to resist confounding — a cognitive-outcomes substudy nested in a randomized trial, and a genetic (Mendelian-randomization) analysis — reportedly came back null. Two different reviewers converged on those same two studies independently, which raises confidence they're real findings, though exact figures should still be verified before quoting them anywhere.
- **A rough sanity check on "how big a signal is this."** For a relative increase this size (hazard ratio ~1.3), an unmeasured confounder that itself roughly doubled both "likelihood of long-term use" and "dementia risk" would be enough to fully explain the whole association (this is the "E-value" concept in epidemiology). Given how many things correlate with a decade of chronic medication use in older adults — polypharmacy, cardiovascular disease, depression, general frailty — that bar isn't hard to clear.
- **Duration-response cuts both ways.** More years of use tracking with more risk is a real point in favor of taking this seriously (it's not nothing). But it's also exactly what confounding-by-indication predicts on its own: people who stay on the drug longest have generally been sicker, older, and in more contact with the medical system for longer.
- **The 30% is relative, not absolute.** It says nothing about her actual odds without her age-specific baseline dementia risk. Even taken completely at face value, it likely translates to a modest absolute increase, not "30% dementia risk."
- **There are better reasons to review this prescription than the dementia study.** Independent of dementia, a decade on this drug already justifies checking B12, magnesium, and bone density, and reviewing infection risk — all better-established than the dementia signal, and useful regardless of what you conclude about it. Current US gastroenterology deprescribing guidance reportedly doesn't even list dementia among its reasons to reconsider long-term use — it's driven by these other, firmer concerns.

## Tradeoffs and risks

- **Risk of overreacting:** stopping this cold turkey. Long-term acid-suppressant withdrawal can cause rebound symptoms worse than baseline for weeks — easily mistaken as proof she "needs" the drug, and a real reason not to let her make a unilateral change. If she has Barrett's esophagus, severe esophagitis, an ulcer history, or is on long-term NSAIDs or blood thinners, staying on it is probably still correct, and she should hear that plainly so she isn't scared off something that's protecting her.
- **Risk of underreacting:** treating "it's an observational study" as a reason to change nothing. That reflex can become an excuse to skip a medication review that's overdue anyway.
- **Where the reviewers actually disagreed:** the methodology-focused and clinically-focused readings both leaned toward "probably mostly confounded" and would anchor a doctor conversation in the firmer B12/magnesium/bone risks rather than the dementia headline. The contrarian read pushed back that cross-country replication and the duration gradient are real evidence that shouldn't be discounted just because the more rigorous studies came back null, and would keep the dementia study explicitly on the table as a stated reason for review. Note the disagreement is about how much causal weight to assign the finding — not about what to actually do next, where all three landed in the same place.
- **Unknowns worth naming to yourself:** which exact study this is (several similar ones exist), whether her drug is this class at all (most of this literature concerns proton-pump inhibitors specifically, not the older H2-blocker class of acid reducers — worth confirming which one she's on, since it changes how much of this applies), her original reason for being prescribed it, and her current labs. Treat any specific study names, sample sizes, or hazard ratios floating around this topic (including some above) as approximate until confirmed, not settled citations — this literature is large, mixed, and easy to oversimplify even when read carefully.

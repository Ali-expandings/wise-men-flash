## Council Debate: How Seriously to Take a Records-Based Study Linking Long-Term Acid-Reflux Medication to Dementia Risk

**Council Members:** Dr. Saskia Voss (pharmacoepidemiologist & biostatistician), Dr. Femi Adeyemi (geriatric clinical pharmacist, deprescribing clinic), Harlan Cho (science journalist tracking drug-safety scares), Dana Whitcombe (patient-advocate & risk-communication specialist)
**Rounds:** 3 (Positions → Responses → Synthesis)

### Round 1: Initial Positions

**Dr. Saskia Voss (pharmacoepidemiologist):**
Let me be precise about what that number means. A hazard ratio near 1.3 in a claims database tells you there's a statistical association worth investigating — it does not tell you the drug caused anything. These studies can't randomize, so they're structurally exposed to confounding by indication (sicker, frailer people end up on long-term PPIs), reverse causation and protopathic bias (undiagnosed dementia changes eating, reflux symptoms, and prescribing years before formal diagnosis), and detection bias (more medical contact means more chances to catch a diagnosis). "30% more likely" is relative-risk language dressed up to sound absolute — against a substantial baseline lifetime dementia risk in the elderly, that translates to a modest absolute increase, if it's causal at all. My position: statistically real in this dataset, not proven to be caused by the drug. That distinction should drive everything the daughter does next.

**Dr. Femi Adeyemi (geriatric clinical pharmacist):**
Here's my honest take: the dementia signal is the least interesting part of this for me. What should worry the daughter more is that nobody's asked in ten years whether her mother still needs this drug at all — that's the real finding buried under every scary headline. Decade-long PPI prescriptions run on autopilot far more than they get reassessed. Fracture risk, B12 and magnesium deficiency, C. diff, chronic kidney disease — these have sturdier evidence behind them than the amyloid story, and they're actionable today, not fifteen years from now. So: book a proper medication review, don't stage an intervention over a headline. And please, don't let her stop it cold on her own — ten years of acid suppression means real rebound hyperacidity risk, and if there's an NSAID or anticoagulant sitting underneath the original reflux diagnosis, unsupervised discontinuation could trade a hypothetical cognitive risk for a real GI bleed. This needs a taper, supervised, by someone who can see her whole med list.

**Harlan Cho (science journalist):**
I've covered three cycles of this exact scare. The original German claims-data study in 2015–16 made global headlines with a relative-risk number that sounded terrifying — and every scary "huge database" finding since then has followed the same arc: replication attempts using better confounder adjustment, active-comparator designs, or longer look-back windows shrink the effect, sometimes to nothing. That's the base rate here, not the exception. What her mother's doctor should weigh isn't the topline "X% more likely" — that's almost certainly a hazard ratio around 1.3, not an absolute risk — but whether protopathic bias explains it: people in early undiagnosed dementia change eating habits, develop more reflux symptoms, get prescribed more PPIs, years before diagnosis. That's not causation, that's the disease writing the prescription. I'd read this as "worth monitoring," not "stop the drug."

**Dana Whitcombe (patient-advocate):**
What matters is what this daughter does Tuesday morning, not the hazard ratio. The real, immediate risk in that house this week is panic: Mom stops the pill cold turkey because a headline scared her, and ends up in the ER with rebound reflux or a bleed because she's also on an NSAID or blood thinner. That's a guaranteed harm versus a contested, probably-modest, decades-out maybe. So: nobody touches the pill before a doctor visit is booked. And the daughter doesn't walk in saying "your pill causes dementia" — untrue, and it'll either terrify Mom or make her defensive and dig in. Better script: "you've been on this ten years, let's ask if you still need it, and get your B12 checked." That's a routine med review, not a crisis. Concrete beats correct.

### Council Synthesis

**Areas of Convergence:**
- **Don't stop the medication unilaterally or abruptly** — every member, independently and repeatedly, flagged rebound acid hypersecretion (and added GI bleed risk if an NSAID or blood thinner is also in the picture) as a real, near-term harm that outweighs a contested, long-term maybe. This was the single most stable point across all three rounds.
- **"30% more likely" is almost certainly a relative-risk figure, not an absolute one.** Nobody on the council disputed this. Against a real but non-trivial baseline dementia risk in the elderly, a hazard ratio in that range usually implies a modest absolute risk increase — if the association is causal at all, which none of the four treated as established.
- **This kind of study is structurally vulnerable to specific, well-known failure modes**, named independently by multiple members: confounding by indication (people who end up on long-term PPIs differ systematically from those who don't), reverse causation / protopathic bias (early, undiagnosed dementia can change symptoms and prescribing years before diagnosis — "the disease writing the prescription"), and detection bias (more medical contact creates more chances to catch a diagnosis). This is the council's direct answer to "what are the ways it usually goes wrong."
- **The single converged action, restated by all four in Round 3 in near-identical language**: book a real medication review with her prescriber or pharmacist, framed as "you've been on this ten years — do you still need it, at this dose, and can we check your B12 (and magnesium)?" If discontinuation ever happens, it should be a supervised taper, not a cold stop.
- **A decade-long prescription deserves periodic reassessment on its own merits**, independent of the dementia question — this is the one place where the "take it seriously" and "don't overreact" camps fully agreed, just via different roads.

**Remaining Disagreements:**
- **How much weight the study itself deserves.** Harlan is closest to dismissive ("let the dementia study die like its predecessors"; "least reliable reason in the room to act"). Saskia is calibrated-neutral (statistically real in this dataset, not proven causal — worth noting, not worth alarm). Femi treats the causal question as nearly beside the point next to the other PPI risks. Dana treats the entire statistical argument as the wrong altitude for what the daughter actually needs.
- **Whether the "boring" PPI risks (fracture, B12/magnesium, kidney, C. diff) are meaningfully better-established than the dementia signal, or just as confounded.** Femi and Harlan argue they've survived more replication and are more consistently reproduced. Saskia is unconvinced that any of these claims-database associations should be graded "easy" — she sees the same observational architecture underneath all of them, just a longer track record on some.
- **How much of the methodology should reach the mother at all.** Dana and Femi push to compress this into one plain, calm sentence; Saskia resists the idea that plain-spoken and rigorous are in tension, arguing the calm is earned by the hedging, not a substitute for it.

**Recommended Path:**
Take the study seriously enough to act on, not seriously enough to panic over. It's a real statistical association in a large dataset — not a demonstrated cause of your mother's cognitive future — and it arrives with the same well-documented vulnerabilities that have deflated most large-database drug-dementia scares before it: confounding by indication, reverse causation/protopathic bias, detection bias, and a relative-risk number that sounds far more dramatic than the likely absolute risk. (Note, honestly: this council can't confirm which exact paper you read — this specific hypothesis has been tested multiple times over the past decade with inconsistent results, so it's worth checking who ran the study, in what population, and what they adjusted for, rather than trusting the headline number alone.)

The action that all four members converged on independently, regardless of how they weighed the statistics, is the same one: don't change anything yourself, and book an actual medication review with her doctor or pharmacist this week or next. Use the plain version — "you've been on this ten years, do you still need it at this dose, can we check your B12 and magnesium" — rather than leading with "your pill causes dementia," which is neither established nor a fair thing to hand her unhedged. That conversation is worth having regardless of which side of this debate turns out to be right, because it's the same conversation good geriatric care recommends for any decade-old prescription. If her doctor does decide to reduce or stop it, that should be a gradual, supervised taper, not a unilateral cold stop — the near-term risk of rebound symptoms (and worse, if she's also on an NSAID or anticoagulant) is the one thing in this entire debate nobody contested.

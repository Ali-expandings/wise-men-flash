## Council: How much to worry about the PPI–dementia study

**Architect:** Give it modest weight — a real signal worth a doctor conversation, not proof, and not a reason to act alone in either direction.
This exact drug/outcome pairing has a weak replication record, and a "30% more likely" headline is easy to produce through confounding or reverse causation without any true drug effect.

**Skeptic:** The number is doing more rhetorical work than evidentiary work; the likelier story is that people on a decade of daily medication are older, sicker, and more medically watched — not that the drug is causing dementia.
This literature has flipped between positive and null findings for years, which means any real effect is small and fragile, not confirmed.

- **Recommendation:** Take the study seriously enough to act, not seriously enough to alarm. The standard ways studies like this go wrong, in order of relevance here: **confounding by indication** (long-term users are systematically older/sicker/more medically monitored than non-users, so the drug can look guilty for reasons that are really about who takes it); **reverse causation** (undiagnosed cognitive decline changes symptoms and prescribing years before a dementia diagnosis lands, making the drug look causal when it's a bystander); **relative risk with no baseline** ("30% more likely" is almost always a small absolute shift, e.g. roughly 4-in-100 becoming 5-in-100, dressed up in the scarier-sounding number); and **non-replication** (this exact drug-outcome pair has swung between positive and null findings for years, which is what a small or absent true effect looks like, not what a real one looks like). Don't raise it with your mother as "your medication might be causing dementia" — raise it as "when did you last check whether you still need this, at this dose." Book that review, bring the three questions above, and let her prescriber weigh the actual risk-benefit for her specific case. That respects both risks at once: it doesn't sit on a signal that might be real, and it doesn't scare her into abruptly stopping a drug that's controlling a real condition.

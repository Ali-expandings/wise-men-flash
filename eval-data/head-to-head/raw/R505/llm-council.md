### Blind Spots the Council Caught
One reviewer caught a mechanism none of the five advisors named: reverse causation. Early, undiagnosed dementia can itself cause GI symptoms or drive more medical contact — cognitive decline pulling someone toward more PPI use and more diagnostic scrutiny, rather than the drug causing the decline. That's distinct from confounding by indication and detection bias, not a restatement of either.

Every reviewer, independently, caught the same second gap: none of the five advisors asked whether this finding replicates. A single study — however large — sits inside a broader PPI-dementia literature that is genuinely mixed, and studies using more rigorous designs (active comparators, new-user designs that avoid enrolling people mid-treatment) tend to produce smaller or null effects. Large N was treated across the board as the main quality signal, when sample size fixes noise, not bias — a biased study with several million records is still biased, just with a tighter confidence interval around the wrong number. One reviewer added a related point nobody else raised: claims-database research routinely tests many drug-outcome pairs in the same dataset, which raises the odds that some pairings clear statistical significance by chance alone.

The biggest practical gap, flagged by four of five reviewers: the user asked how to talk to their mother, and the council mostly answered how to get her to a doctor's appointment instead. Three of the five responses convert "how do I approach her" into "book a review" or "call the pharmacist" — useful, but it routes around the actual conversation rather than equipping the user for it. Nobody gave language for what to say if she brings up the headline herself, how to avoid her feeling either dismissed or frightened, or how to include her as a decision-maker rather than someone to be managed toward an appointment.

### The Recommendation
Take the finding seriously as a prompt to act, not as evidence the drug is dangerous. A 30% relative-risk increase from a registry study doesn't clear the bar for "this drug causes dementia" on its own — confounding by indication, detection bias, survivorship selection in the "5+ years" cutoff, and a plausible reverse-causation pathway can each produce a number like this with no causal effect at all, and the fact that swapping in an active comparator tends to shrink this specific effect is a strong sign that's exactly what's happening. It's also a relative number with no baseline attached: a 30% bump could mean a jump from 5% to 6.5% absolute risk over a decade — real, but nowhere near as alarming as "30% more likely" makes it sound. Millions of records make that estimate more precise, not more true. Set the 30% number itself aside; it isn't the thing to act on.

What's worth acting on is independent of whether the dementia link is real: a decade of daily PPI use with no re-indication review is already worth fixing, on the strength of PPI risks that are far better established than the dementia signal — fracture, C. diff, B12/magnesium depletion, kidney disease. Find out why she started it — a real anatomical finding like Barrett's esophagus or erosive esophagitis versus garden-variety heartburn nobody's revisited in ten years — and get a medication review on the calendar, framed as routine maintenance of a long-running prescription, not a response to a scary headline. Whether that review covers just the PPI or her whole regimen is a genuine judgment call; lean toward whole-regimen only if she already sees a pharmacist regularly or carries several long-term prescriptions, since a bigger ask risks becoming its own barrier.

None of this is grounds for panic, and none of it is grounds for her stopping anything on her own — tapering under a prescriber is the only safe way off a long-term PPI, because stopping cold causes rebound acid hypersecretion that will feel like proof she needed the drug when it's actually withdrawal. The evidence clears the bar for "worth a conversation," not "worth alarm."

### The One Thing to Do First
Before calling anyone: find out why your mother started the PPI in the first place — ask her directly, or check old records. That one fact determines everything downstream. A serious original indication means the medication is close to non-negotiable and this is a non-event; heartburn from a decade ago that nobody's re-checked since means there's a real, low-drama conversation to have. It costs ten minutes, needs no appointment, and tells you whether what comes next is a five-minute call or an actual sit-down.

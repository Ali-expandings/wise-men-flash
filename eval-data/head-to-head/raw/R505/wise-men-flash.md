## Recommendation

Get her proton-pump-inhibitor prescription reviewed by her doctor or pharmacist — specifically, whether she still has an ongoing reason to take it, and whether her B12 and magnesium have ever been checked — rather than acting on the dementia finding directly in either direction. This finding belongs to a class of study that turns out to be wrong more often than right, but it hasn't been cleanly explained away either; the one thing worth doing about it is worth doing regardless of how that question eventually resolves.

## Why

A large records study reporting a 20–50% relative-risk increase between a long-term drug and a slow-developing disease is a specific, recognizable type of finding, and that type is wrong more often than it's right. The reasons are structural, not particular to this drug: people who take a medication daily for years differ systematically from people who don't — older, sicker, seen by doctors more often, on more other medications — and records data rarely captures enough of that difference to fully correct for it (confounding by indication). Dementia makes this worse than most outcomes, because the underlying brain changes typically start ten to twenty years before diagnosis, so early undiagnosed decline can itself change symptoms, care-seeking, and prescribing years before anyone records a diagnosis (reverse causation) — and heavier medical contact by itself produces more chances for a diagnosis to get recorded (detection bias). None of this is fixed by a bigger sample: millions of records shrink the statistical noise around an estimate, but do nothing about bias in what was measured, so "several million people" makes a finding precise, not necessarily true.

Against that backdrop, this specific finding is better described as genuinely unresolved than as cleanly explained away. It has been reported in more than one independent dataset and country (unverified), and — in at least one dataset built on direct assessment of cognitive status rather than insurance diagnosis codes — the excess appeared specifically above several years of cumulative use (unverified), which plain reverse causation doesn't obviously predict. Other studies, including at least one national registry, found no excess except in people who'd started the drug shortly before diagnosis (unverified) — exactly the reverse-causation pattern, and it argues the other way. There's also a proposed biological route — reduced B12 absorption and its cognitive effects, and laboratory findings on the drug's effect on amyloid processing (unverified) — that's plausible but unconfirmed. The one randomized trial run on this drug class followed people for about three years (unverified) — long enough to rule out several other suspected harms, which it did, but not long enough to say anything about ten-plus years of continuous use. Its silence on long-term use isn't evidence of long-term safety; it's a question the trial was never positioned to answer. These specifics are the shape of the literature as it's generally understood, not numbers freshly checked this session, and should be treated as approximate. The overall pattern, though — a signal that's replicated inconsistently, tracks duration of use in at least one better-diagnosed cohort, and has a plausible but unproven mechanism — is a fair description of where this sits: not an argument for panic, and not an argument for dismissal.

The size of the effect matters as much as its existence. A 30% relative increase sounds alarming, but relative risk multiplies a baseline that's fairly low at any given age — for a woman in her seventies, baseline dementia risk over the following decade runs roughly in the low-to-mid teens percent (unverified, order-of-magnitude only), so taking the 30% figure entirely at face value adds a few percentage points to her absolute odds, not a doubling of her risk. That's not nothing — a few points of absolute risk on something this serious is worth a real conversation — but it's a shift at the margin, not a near-certainty.

Finally, the question as posed — scare her into stopping, or wave the risk away — leaves out the option that actually fits the evidence: find out whether she still needs the drug at all. A large share of people on long-term acid reducers no longer have the condition that justified starting them, and prescribing guidance for older adults already cautions against open-ended use without a specific ongoing indication, for reasons that have nothing to do with dementia. That review is the right move whether or not the dementia link turns out to be real.

## What to do

To size up any study like this one — this one, or the next one someone sends her — five questions do most of the work. What was the comparison group: other people being treated for the same underlying condition with a different drug, or people who never needed any such drug at all? The first is a harder, more convincing test than the second. Does the excess risk appear specifically after a period of sustained use, or mostly right around the time of diagnosis? The latter usually means reverse causation, not cause. Has it shown up in more than one independent dataset, ideally using more than one way of identifying cases? Is there a specific, proposed mechanism, or is the drug "linked to" a long list of unrelated conditions — a long list is usually a sign of who takes the drug, not what the drug does. And what does the relative-risk number mean in absolute terms for someone of her age and health — a "30% increase" means nothing on its own without a baseline to multiply.

For her specifically: raise it with her prescriber as a routine "do you still need this" review, not as a dementia warning. That's both the more honest framing, given how unsettled the evidence actually is, and the one least likely to frighten her over a number that, translated to her real risk, is modest. Ask two things at that visit: whether she has an ongoing reason to be on it — a prior bleeding ulcer, Barrett's esophagus, severe erosive esophagitis or a narrowed esophagus, or regular use of aspirin, a blood thinner, or an anti-inflammatory painkiller that needs stomach protection — and whether her B12 and magnesium have ever been checked, since years of acid suppression measurably reduce absorption of both, an established and fixable contributor to cognitive symptoms independent of whatever the dementia research eventually shows. If she has one of those ongoing reasons, the right move is to keep her on it at the lowest dose that controls it; this finding shouldn't change that. If she doesn't, tapering to a lower dose or as-needed use, under her doctor's guidance, is worth doing regardless of the dementia question. Either way, don't let her stop on her own initiative: quitting a long-term acid reducer abruptly causes a rebound surge in stomach acid that mimics the symptoms she started the drug for, which tends to convince people they need it even when they don't.

## Risks of this plan

Treating this as routine rather than urgent has a real cost if the less comfortable reading is right: the strongest version of this signal is specifically about long-duration use, she's already past the duration where a real effect would show up, and every additional year of unreviewed use is more exposure on the table if there's something to it. Skipping the B12 and magnesium check has no comparable upside on the other side of the ledger — that pathway is established, not speculative — so it shouldn't be treated as optional even though it's bundled here into a routine step.

The opposite failure carries its own cost. If she's frightened into stopping abruptly, or a change is made without first checking whether she has a genuine ongoing indication, she risks rebound acid symptoms at minimum and, if she's also on an NSAID, a blood thinner, or has a bleeding history, a real gastrointestinal bleed at worst — a concrete, well-established harm traded for a dementia risk that remains uncertain and, even taken at face value, modest in absolute terms.

This plan is cheap to be wrong about in either direction, which is exactly why it's the right plan under this much uncertainty: a medication review and a blood test cost one appointment and a blood draw, and if her doctor confirms she needs the drug, nothing is lost but the conversation. The two mistakes that aren't cheap are the ones already named at the start of this: frightening her into stopping a drug she needs, and letting a decade-old prescription run unexamined for another decade no matter which way the dementia evidence eventually breaks.

## Strongest counter-position

The case for treating this as more urgent than a routine review doesn't depend on the study being right — it depends on the fact that it hasn't been shown wrong in the ways that would normally let you set a finding like this aside. Bias can explain almost any single result of this shape; the real question is whether this particular one survived the tests that a bias-driven finding usually fails, and on the available account it did better against those tests than a pure-confounding story predicts: the excess tracked cumulative years of use rather than clustering right before diagnosis, it turned up in more than one country's data, and it appeared even in at least one dataset that used direct cognitive assessment instead of relying on whether a diagnosis code happened to get entered.

There's a direct precedent for taking this kind of signal seriously rather than assuming it's detection bias: a comparable-sized, similarly long-duration association between long-term hormone therapy and breast cancer (unverified specifics) was initially attributed to women on the drug simply getting more mammograms — and a later randomized trial confirmed the effect was real. And because the only trial run on this drug class followed people for about three years, "no trial contradicts the long-term risk" will remain true indefinitely whether or not a real ten-year effect exists. Treating the absence of trial evidence as reassurance, for an exposure duration no trial has tested or is ever likely to test, mistakes an unanswerable question for a settled one.

This position wins if studies that compare PPI users against people taking a different reflux drug for the same condition — the harder, more convincing test — continue to show a similar effect rather than the association shrinking toward nothing the way a pure confounding story would predict.

The single most decision-relevant unknown isn't in the research literature at all — it's what her actual ongoing indication for the drug is, and whether her B12 and magnesium have ever been tested. No amount of reading about the study substitutes for that conversation with her prescriber.

## Recommendation
Treat the 30% as a ceiling demonstrated somewhere, not a forecast for you — put minimal independent weight on it until you've replaced it with a number derived from your own ticket data, and don't commit budget-significant money against the vendor's figure alone.

## Why
A single case study is produced by a process that runs one direction: the vendor selects, and the customer confirms, the most favorable result out of however many deployments they have; customers whose results were mediocre or who churned don't get featured, and the person quoted is typically the internal champion whose own credibility is now tied to the purchase looking like a win. On top of that selection effect, three further distortions are common in this exact evidence pattern and are worth checking for specifically: regression to the mean (a customer often gets picked partly because their ticket volume was unusually bad right before adoption, so part of any "improvement" would have happened anyway); confounds (case-study customers frequently change other things — a help-center rewrite, a pricing change, a staffing shift, a product fix — in the same window, and the tool gets full credit); and definitional drift (auto-closes, bundled contacts, and chatbot "resolutions" get counted as deflected tickets in ways that may not match how you count a ticket today). None of this means the vendor is lying or the number is fake — it means one confirmed data point, selected for you rather than sampled at random, is weak evidence about what happens in your environment specifically, in either direction.

The number that actually predicts your outcome is not in the case study at all — it's in your own ticket log. Whatever fraction of your ticket volume falls into categories this tool can structurally address is a hard ceiling on what it can do for you, independent of how honest or skilled the vendor is; a mismatch between your ticket mix and the case-study customer's makes 30% unreachable no matter what the contract says. There's also a second, separate risk that sits after the measurement question: even a fully real 30% drop in ticket count only shows up as budget savings if something concrete changes on your cost side — headcount you reduce, a BPO contract you scale down, hiring you avoid. Against fixed salaried staff with no headcount plan attached to this purchase, a genuine 30% deflection is likely to show up as slack capacity, not dollars, in the first year.

## What to do
Start with your own data, not the vendor's: pull the last two quarters of your ticket log, bucket by contact reason, and total the share of volume that falls into categories this tool could plausibly touch (account actions it can't take, billing disputes, and bug-driven contacts usually can't be deflected by a knowledge or chat layer; documentation-answerable and status-check questions usually can). That share is your realistic ceiling — if it comes in well under 30%, the vendor's number was never on the table for you regardless of their honesty, and you have a concrete, vendor-independent basis for the rest of the negotiation.

In parallel, put four specific requests to the vendor, all of which should already exist in their systems: the distribution of realized ticket-volume change across their full customer base (median and bottom quartile, not just the mean or the featured logo — refusal or inability to produce this is itself informative); the exact metric definition (what counts as a ticket, whether auto-closes/bundled contacts/bot sessions are included, and the measurement window); gross/net revenue retention and churn for this product line, as the hardest-to-spin proxy for whether customers who bought it kept it; and a reference call, with someone you select from their full customer list against criteria you set (your vertical, your ticket volume, your channel mix), not someone they nominate — include their willingness to connect you to a churned or downgraded customer as a signal in itself.

Then negotiate structure rather than resolve belief: a time-boxed paid pilot (roughly 8-12 weeks is standard for this) measured against your own ticket definitions and your own pre-existing trend line, ideally with a holdout segment or staged rollout so you can separate the tool's effect from normal seasonal variation; contract terms tied to a measured reduction rather than a flat fee — service credits or phased payment scaled to shortfall against a trend-adjusted target checked at defined intervals; an initial term short enough to exit without a multi-year sunk cost if the pilot doesn't hold up at scale; and, before signing anything, a one-line commitment from whoever owns support headcount or BPO spend on what specifically gets reduced if the tool delivers, so the savings have somewhere to land.

## Risks of this plan
This adds weeks to the purchase, and if the tool is genuinely as good as claimed, that delay has a real cost — a quarter of foregone ticket reduction and, if the vendor's price or promotional terms are time-limited, possibly a worse deal later. Some of what you're asking the vendor for (full customer-outcome distributions, unscreened reference contacts) may be commercially sensitive information they can't share even in good faith, which can look like stonewalling when it isn't; if they decline the distribution data specifically but agree readily to a holdout pilot and outcome-contingent pricing, weight the willingness to take pricing risk over the refusal to share other customers' data. A short pilot has its own distortions — a small-scale or short-duration test can show an inflated result because staff and customers behave differently while everyone's paying close attention (a Hawthorne effect), and it may miss slower failure modes like ticket volume creeping back up after the novelty wears off. And the ticket-mix ceiling calculation is only as good as your tagging discipline; if contact-reason data is inconsistent or missing, that analysis will understate its own reliability, which argues for fixing the tagging before trusting either the vendor's number or your own. Weighed against all of that: the cost of skipping this and being wrong is a budget-significant multi-year contract paid for volume reduction that either doesn't materialize or never converts into money you can point to, plus the sunk integration and content-maintenance effort needed to get there.

## Strongest counter-position
The case for heavy discounting rests on treating this case study as a selected outlier rather than a representative result — but that's a strong prior, not a certainty, and it is specifically falsifiable: if the vendor produces the full outcome distribution across their customer base and this case study sits near the median rather than at the top of it, most of the discount stops applying, and the number deserves real weight rather than a heavy haircut. That is also the single piece of evidence every angle on this converges on demanding, which makes it a fair test rather than a rhetorical escape hatch — the vendor's response to that specific request (produces it, plausibly declines citing confidentiality, or simply can't) is closer to a verdict on the 30% than anything else available before a pilot runs.

## Confidence
High that a single vendor-selected case study is weak evidence for your specific outcome — selection bias, regression to the mean, and definitional mismatch are well-established mechanisms, not speculative. High that your own ticket-mix ceiling is the more decisive number and is something you can compute now. Medium on the pilot-with-holdout structure working as designed — it depends on the vendor agreeing to terms that share their risk, which is a negotiation outcome, not a fact in hand. Low, and explicitly a rule of thumb rather than a measured figure, on any specific "realistic outcome is X% of the headline" fraction — treat any number like that, including ones you'll hear from consultants or your own gut, as a placeholder for the ceiling calculation above, not a substitute for it. The material unknown that would move all of this: what your own ticket-reason breakdown actually shows, which nobody in this analysis has seen.

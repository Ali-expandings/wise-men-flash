### Blind Spots the Council Caught
- **Nobody computed breakeven.** First Principles Thinker, Expansionist, and Outsider flagged this independently in review: nobody asked what deflection rate would actually have to be true for this contract to pay for itself against the stated budget. That number, not the vendor's 30%, is the real yardstick — and nobody weighed the cheapest alternative either, such as whether a self-serve or documentation fix could capture part of that reduction for free. If breakeven turns out to be low, the skeptics' zero-weight stance on the case study still clears the bar comfortably, and the real risk flips from overpaying to moving too slowly.
- **The proposed fix carries the vendor's disease.** The Contrarian's review turns the same skepticism on its own camp: running your own pilot sounds rigorous until you notice it's still one data point, generated by people who know what outcome the decision-makers want to see.
- **Nobody addressed deal mechanics.** The Executor's review catches a gap shared by all four diligence-focused answers: no comparison of tuning/ramp-up time to contract length (you may not discover it's failing until you're already locked in), no question about exit or data-portability terms if a mid-contract pilot goes sideways, and no suggestion to check whether 30% is specific to this vendor or just typical of this tool category — a quick benchmark against a competitor would answer that.

### The Recommendation
Put no standalone weight on the 30% figure. It's one uncontrolled data point that happens to match the marketing pitch exactly, and no discount factor — half credit, ten percent credit — turns an anecdote into a probability statement about your organization. Four of five advisors and the entire peer-review layer agree on this, and the reasoning holds up.

But "the number is weak" is a diagnosis, not a decision. Do four things, in order:
1. Calculate your own breakeven deflection rate from the contract cost and your support cost structure. This tells you how much diligence effort is warranted and gives you a real target — likely far below 30%.
2. Bucket last quarter's tickets by root cause to find your own ceiling. If repetitive how-to/FAQ traffic is 15% of volume, 30% is impossible regardless of what the vendor showed you; if it's 55%, the number may be conservative.
3. Run a paid pilot against your own tickets, scored against your own definition of "resolved" rather than raw ticket count, with your breakeven number as the pre-agreed target, an exit clause, and a design a support rep can't quietly game.
4. Get your own reference calls — sized and mixed like your business — and ask explicitly for customers who missed the number. A vendor who can't produce one just told you the case study is the whole pitch.

Do not sign the annual contract on the strength of one case study. And do not take the Expansionist's negotiating advice yet — the instinct behind it is worth keeping (this is exactly the kind of decision where under-committing can cost as much as overpaying), but it's a second-stage move. Pricing leverage, ratchets, expansion terms: revisit all of it after the pilot clears your breakeven number, not before.

### The One Thing to Do First
Before the next vendor call, before designing a pilot, before a single reference check: calculate the minimum ticket-deflection percentage that would make this contract pay for itself against your actual support cost structure and budget. That breakeven number — not the vendor's 30% — is the yardstick everything else gets measured against, and it's the one number on this entire list you can produce today, from data you already have, without needing anything from the vendor.

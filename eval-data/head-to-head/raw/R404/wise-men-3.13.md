## Recommendation

Discount the 30% figure heavily as a forecast for your own results — treat it as a best-case existence proof, not an expected outcome — and resolve this less by scrutinizing the vendor's evidence further and more by restructuring the deal so that being wrong about it is cheap. Before you spend any more time evaluating their case study, spend a few days checking your own data; before you sign, make the contract itself absorb the risk the case study can't resolve.

## Why

A single customer case study is weak evidence for what you'll see, independent of whether the vendor is honest. It's a selected, self-reported result — vendors and their reference customers show their best outcome, not a typical one, and the customer's own account has no reason to separate "the tool worked" from "we also rewrote our help center and fixed a bug the same quarter," or from "fewer logged tickets" meaning customers gave up or routed around support rather than got helped. None of that requires bad faith; it's what one uncontrolled before/after data point looks like even when everyone involved is telling the truth. The people closest to the number also aren't the ones who pay if it's wrong: the sales rep is paid at signature, the reference customer is validating a decision they already made, and an internal champion gets credit at rollout, long before the real result is known. You're the only party whose payoff depends on whether 30% is actually true for you, which is exactly why nobody upstream has done that work for you.

There's also a mechanical reason the number matters less than it appears to: cutting ticket volume doesn't automatically cut cost. Automated deflection tends to remove your easiest, cheapest contacts first, leaving a smaller queue of harder ones with rising average handling time — so a real 30% volume drop can coexist with close to 0% cost savings, especially if your support cost is salaried headcount rather than pay-per-ticket. This cuts against over-investing in verifying the number at all: if your support cost is fixed, the honest answer to "how much should I trust 30%" may matter less than the honest answer to "does a ticket-volume drop even move my costs."

## What to do

Treat this as free, fast checks on your own data first, then demands on the vendor, then a contract structure — in that order, before you spend more time on their case study:

- Pull several months of your own tickets and cluster them by intent. This tells you your deflection ceiling: what share of volume is even the kind of thing a tool like this could plausibly resolve, versus billing disputes, bugs, and account issues it can't. If your top repeat intents don't add up to a large share of volume, 30% is arithmetically unlikely for you no matter how good the vendor is; if they do, the claim is at least plausible.
- At the same time, check whether your support cost is fixed (salaried headcount that doesn't flex with ticket count) or variable (contractors, BPO, per-ticket or per-minute billing). This one fact determines whether a ticket-volume reduction — even a real one — actually shows up as savings. If your cost is fixed, be explicit with yourself that you're really buying capacity headroom or team relief, not the dollar savings the 30% number implies.
- Before committing to this specific tool, put two cheaper alternatives on the table against the same ticket-taxonomy findings: fixing your highest-volume intents directly (help-center content, a confusing form, a product bug) and an agent-assist layer that speeds up your own team (suggested replies, summarization) rather than depending on customers self-serving. Both are frequently competitive with a dedicated deflection tool, and their effect lands directly in your own agents' handle time, so it doesn't carry the vendor tool's measurement problem.
- Ask the vendor for their outcome distribution across their full customer base — median and bottom-quartile results, not just their best logo — and for the exact definition behind the 30% (human-handled ticket reduction, or something softer like bot-containment or session counts). Ask to talk to a reference customer you pick, not one they hand you. A vendor confident in the number will have this data and share it; resistance or "we don't track it that way" is itself an answer.
- Ask them to put pricing behind the claim: some or all fees at risk against a target measured in your own ticketing system, on a short initial term. A vendor who believes their own number will accept some version of this; refusal is the cheapest, fastest signal you'll get, cheaper than any reference call.
- If you proceed, structure the contract so the case study stops mattering: a bounded initial term with a real exit, the success metric and baseline defined and agreed in writing before go-live, and — if you can manage it — gate success on cost per contact or repeat-contact rate rather than raw ticket count, so the vendor can't win by making customers give up instead of get helped.

## Risks of this plan

Running your own ticket-taxonomy analysis takes real time from someone who could be doing other things, and if your ticket data is messy or your volume is low, the analysis itself may not be conclusive — you can end up with an ambiguous ceiling estimate and have to fall back on judgment anyway. Pushing hard on outcome-linked pricing and reference flexibility can also slow the deal down or read as adversarial to a vendor (and to an internal champion) who's already invested in getting this signed; that's a real cost if the underlying tool is in fact a good fit and you're leaving value on the table by over-negotiating. There's a cost to waiting, too: every quarter spent re-verifying is a quarter of whatever ticket relief the tool might have genuinely provided, foregone.

Weigh that against the cost of being wrong the other way: signing at full term and full price on the strength of one case study, discovering a year in that your result looks nothing like 30% (or that it does, but costs didn't move because your support is salaried), with a multi-year commitment and no exit. That failure mode is expensive and slow to unwind. The failure mode of this plan — spending a few days on your own data and pushing for a shorter, outcome-linked deal — is comparatively cheap and fully recoverable: worst case, you spend some internal time and the vendor says no to terms you'd have wanted anyway, which is information, not loss.

## Strongest counter-position

The case for not discounting the number as hard as the above implies: a 30% deflection claim is mechanistically ordinary, not extraordinary, given how concentrated support contact volume typically is around a small set of repeat intents — "if your top intents carry half your volume, 30% deflection only requires the tool to handle roughly 60% of the easy half." On this view, treating vendor case studies as something to be automatically divided down is its own kind of sloppy thinking, applied asymmetrically against a claim that happens to come from outside: "'divide vendor claims by three' is folklore applied asymmetrically: your own baseline forecast... is equally unvalidated and nobody discounts it." The better move isn't a blanket discount factor, it's checking your own mechanism headroom directly — and if that headroom is there, the number has earned more trust than reflexive skepticism gives it, especially once the vendor is willing to back it with their own money. This view wins if your ticket concentration is genuinely high and the vendor accepts outcome-linked pricing; it loses if your ticket mix is more evenly spread across many low-volume intents, in which case even a mechanistically "ordinary" 30% claim has no floor to stand on in your specific data.

## Confidence

High that a single, vendor-supplied case study is insufficient evidence on its own to plan a budget-significant commitment around — the selection-bias, incentive-misalignment, and no-counterfactual problems here are structural, not specific to this vendor. High that ticket-volume reduction and cost reduction are frequently different things, especially under fixed support costs — that's a mechanical relationship, not an estimate. Medium on any specific number for what you'd actually see (illustrative outside-view estimates from this kind of case center in the low-teens percent, with realistic outcomes ranging from roughly zero to the full claimed 30%) — that range is a reasoned prior, not a measurement, and your own ticket-taxonomy check will tell you more than any further discounting of theirs. Low, and genuinely unknown until you check: whether your specific ticket mix has enough concentrated, automatable volume to make 30% plausible at all, and whether your support cost structure is fixed or variable — both are cheap to resolve and both change this answer more than anything else here.

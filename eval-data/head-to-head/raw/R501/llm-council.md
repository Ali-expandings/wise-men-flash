## Council Verdict: Row-Level Security vs. App-Layer Tenant Isolation

### Where the Council Agrees
All five advisors land on RLS, independently. That unanimity is the strongest data point here — five different reasoning styles (adversarial, first-principles, commercial, outside-view, operational) converged on the same answer without coordinating, and no peer reviewer dissented from the verdict either. Every critique in the peer review layer is about reasoning quality and gaps, not about whether RLS is the right call.

They converge on the mechanism, too. The Contrarian, First Principles Thinker, Outsider, and Executor all make the same underlying point in different language: the application-code option isn't a new control, it's a description of the control that already exists and already failed. The ORM's filter was mandatory in exactly the sense new lint rules would be — mandatory for code that goes through the blessed path, invisible to anything that doesn't. The near-miss was raw SQL. Lint rules run at code review and CI, neither of which a one-off report, a psql session, or an ad hoc admin script ever touches. Tightening the rule doesn't close the gap that failed; it extends the same perimeter a little further.

There's also convergence that this isn't really either/or. Four of five advisors (First Principles Thinker, Expansionist, Outsider, Executor) state explicitly that they'd keep the application-layer wrapper and lint rules alongside RLS — for ergonomics, as a first line of defense, or because it's cheap and catches mistakes early. The Contrarian doesn't address the wrapper's fate directly but never argues for removing it either.

On the specific technical objection raised — PgBouncer transaction-mode pooling — the two advisors who engage it in depth (First Principles Thinker, Executor) converge on the identical fix: `SET LOCAL` inside each transaction, not a session-level `SET`, set at the same point the ORM currently injects customer_id.

### Where the Council Clashes
The disagreement isn't over the verdict — it's over the register of the argument and how much confidence RLS deserves once adopted.

The Expansionist argues from commercial upside: RLS as a roadmap unlock (safe BI access, data-scientist queries against prod, a premium isolation tier) and a procurement argument (database-layer enforcement is a stronger security-questionnaire answer). The other four argue from engineering risk: the control that almost failed needs to be replaced with one that structurally can't fail the same way. That split shows up sharply in each advisor's "what would change my answer" — the Expansionist's condition is business trajectory ("if you're certain this business never goes upmarket"), while the other four's are technical (an unfixable performance regression, query-volume mix, a future contractual demand for physical isolation). Peer review was unanimous that tying a safety decision protecting 2,000 tenants' financial data to go-to-market strategy is a weak move, not a valid alternative axis.

There's a real split on how much to trust RLS once it's on. The Executor and First Principles Thinker treat pooling, debugging, and performance as solved engineering problems — apply the known fix, verify in staging, move on. The Contrarian treats RLS as a control with its own silent-failure surface: `BYPASSRLS`, table-owner and migration roles that skip policies by default, and per-table opt-in that reproduces "forgot to configure it" one layer down. Both can be true at once, but they point to different postures — ship with confidence versus ship and then go actively hunting for what it doesn't cover.

The Outsider introduces the one scenario where the tradeoff gets genuinely harder rather than staying one-sided: if raw SQL isn't an edge case but the bulk of query volume — an analytics-heavy product — RLS's costs land on the core path, not just scripts and admin tools. And the First Principles Thinker floats a different ceiling than the rest: if enterprise contracts will eventually demand physical isolation, don't keep tuning RLS indefinitely — jump to schema-per-tenant. That's a different bet about the future than the Executor's incremental table-by-table rollout, not a contradiction of it, but a question of how far this needs to go.

### Blind Spots the Council Caught
Every one of the five reviewers, independently, named the Expansionist as the weak link, for compounding reasons: it never engages the pooling, debugging, or performance objections the question specifically raised — its technical content is one line ("keep the lint rules regardless"). Worse, its central pitch, that RLS makes raw production access safe by default for BI tools, data scientists, and auditors, is never checked against the Contrarian's own finding that RLS has real bypass surface. Taken literally, recommending wider raw access on the assumption RLS is airtight is riskier than what the company has today. A clean 5-for-5 callout on the same advisor for the same underlying reason is the loudest signal the review layer produced.

The bigger finding: not one of the five advisors, including the Contrarian, who raised survivorship bias as the sharpest point in the whole set ("a near miss is only the one someone caught"), proposed a way to verify, on an ongoing basis, that isolation actually holds after the decision ships. All five reviewers landed on some version of the same gap — no CI check that every tenant table has a forced RLS policy, no adversarial test suite that attempts cross-tenant reads under real application and admin credentials and asserts they fail, no alerting on `BYPASSRLS` or superuser usage, no audit of historical query logs to check whether this already happened before, undetected. The original incident was caught because a human happened to be looking at the right report at the right time. The council designed a better lock and never designed anything that checks, automatically, whether it's latched.

A related gap three reviewers raised independently: RLS enforces at the Postgres query layer only. Once data leaves through replication, warehouse ETL, backups, or a BI tool pointed at a read replica without the RLS-scoped role, isolation doesn't travel with it — directly undercutting the Expansionist's "safe by default" pitch. Two reviewers pushed this further to the sharpest question in the whole exercise: where does the report that caused the actual near-miss run? If it's against a replica or warehouse rather than primary Postgres, RLS on the primary doesn't touch the system that almost caused the incident.

### The Recommendation
Turn on RLS, enforced at the database, and keep the application-layer wrapper and lint rules running alongside it. This is what four of five advisors independently proposed and the fifth never contradicted — not a compromise, the actual convergent answer. RLS is the only control here that a raw SQL query cannot route around by construction, and the near-miss happened specifically because every other control could be. Nobody, advisor or reviewer, produced a counter to that. The pooling objection has a known answer two advisors reached independently — `SET LOCAL` per transaction, not session state, set at the exact point the ORM currently injects customer_id. Performance and debugging are empirical questions, closed by piloting on staging with real query plans, not by argument.

Two conditions on that recommendation, both surfaced only by peer review and both non-negotiable at this stake level. First, don't treat "RLS is on" as done — `BYPASSRLS`, table-owner and migration roles, and per-table opt-in are real gaps in RLS itself, and closing them takes an explicit inventory of every role that touches these tables plus a default that new tables get RLS automatically, not by someone remembering. Second, build the detective control the whole council skipped: an automated job that logs in as one tenant, tries to read another tenant's data, and fails the build if it succeeds — running continuously, not checked once at launch. Also confirm where the report that caused the actual near-miss runs; if it hits a replica, warehouse, or BI tool rather than primary Postgres, RLS alone won't cover it, and that path needs its own fix.

### The One Thing to Do First
Write the RLS policy for the invoices table — the one that almost leaked — and in the same piece of work, write and run the adversarial test that logs in as one tenant and tries to read another tenant's invoices, and confirm it fails. Ship both together, this week, in staging, before touching any other table.

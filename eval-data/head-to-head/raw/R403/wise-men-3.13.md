## Recommendation

Don't hand leadership a number yet. Take two weeks to check the number being built on and the reason people actually leave step three, then set the target as a range tied to a downstream metric — not to raw step-three completion — with the redesign's scope and the remaining budget adjusted to whatever that diagnostic finds. Treat "redesign step three" and "one quarter" as provisional until then, not as already decided.

## Why

"Cut it in half" is a round number chosen before anyone has looked at why people actually drop off, and it is more aggressive than it sounds. Cutting the drop-off from 40% to 20% raises full onboarding completion from 60% to 80% — a 20-point absolute gain, a 33% relative increase in the number of people who complete onboarding, from one change to one step. That is a strong outcome to promise. Single-step redesigns typically produce something like a 5-25% relative reduction in that step's drop-off (40% → roughly 30-38%) — a general planning figure, typical rather than universal, and unverified for this specific funnel (unverified). The better benchmark is closer at hand and cheap to check: the realized effect size of the last five to ten onboarding changes this team has actually shipped. If the median past result is a few points and the best ever achieved is ten, a twenty-point ask is a bet on beating your own record by a wide margin; if swings that large have already landed here before, "half" is a far smaller stretch than the general figure suggests.

The 40% figure itself deserves scrutiny before any target gets built on it. A 20-50% drop at any single step of a multi-step onboarding flow is ordinary, not automatically a defect — so 40% only signals a real problem if step three is worse than the steps around it, and nothing so far establishes that. Three things routinely inflate a number like this: counting people at the session level rather than the user level (someone who abandons on one device and finishes on another, or returns a week later, gets scored as a permanent drop when they are not one); a completion event that doesn't fire the same way on every client; and bundling several different cohorts — good and bad channels, devices, or plan tiers — into one blended average. There is also a selection effect worth naming plainly: step three was singled out because it looked worst, and picking the worst-looking thing tends to look somewhat better on its own at the next measurement, with or without a fix.

The metric behind the target is also easy to move without actually fixing anything — the textbook Goodhart's Law problem, where a measure that becomes the target stops being a good measure of the thing anyone actually wanted. Step-three completion can be raised by making the field optional, deferring the ask to a later step, or making the screen harder to leave — each would improve the number while moving the loss downstream (to step four, or to 30-day activation) or making the product worse for people who had a legitimate reason to hesitate. Whoever owns this number should expect that incentive to shape scoping decisions across the whole quarter unless a downstream guardrail metric is attached to the target from the start, not added after the fact.

Redesign is not yet the established fix, either. Drop-off at a step can come from several different places, each with a different ceiling: something genuinely confusing or poorly prioritized on the screen (fixable by rearranging it); a hard external requirement the person can't satisfy right there — a document, a permission, someone else's input — where no amount of visual redesign helps; an expectation mismatch set up by an earlier step, where step three is just where the disappointment lands; or, less happily, people who were never going to convert regardless of the screen. A quarter of redesign only pays for itself if the first category dominates, and that has not been checked yet.

A handful of things are answerable within a week from data that most likely already exists, and any one of them could change the plan: how steps one, two, and four drop off (if they drop similarly, step three isn't special, and a step-three-only redesign won't move the total much); whether the 40% is session-scoped, and what fraction of "droppers" actually finish within 7 or 30 days; and the drop-off rate in the best-performing segment (by channel, device, or plan), which sets a rough, practical floor on what any fix — redesign or otherwise — can realistically achieve. Whatever that floor turns out to be, "half" should be judged against it, not against the raw 40%.

Sample size matters too, in a way that cuts both ways. Detecting a true halving takes very little traffic — roughly 80 people reaching the step, per arm, at standard significance and power. Detecting a more realistic 3-6 point improvement takes on the order of 15 to 50 times that many. Whatever the actual weekly volume through step three is, it determines whether "did we hit the number" can be measured at all inside a quarter, or whether the target is effectively unfalsifiable by the time anyone tries to check it. In a strange but useful way, the sample size a claim would need to detect it is itself a check on how big that claim plausibly is: if the effect really were as large as a halving, confirming it would be cheap.

## What to do

1. This week: don't deliver a percentage. Ask directly whether leadership means this as a committed number or a stretch/aspirational one — those carry different rules for how a miss should be read — and separately, deliver a date and a shape: a commitment to return in ten working days with a target expressed as a range tied to a downstream metric (activation or 30-day retention) rather than step-three completion alone.
2. Days 1-3: validate and segment the baseline. Confirm how the 40% is actually measured (session- vs. user-scoped, cross-device handling, bot filtering), compare step three's drop-off against its neighboring steps, check what fraction of drops return and complete within 7 and 30 days, and segment by channel, device, and plan tier to find the best-performing cohort as a rough floor on what's fixable. In parallel, pull the realized effect size of the last five to ten onboarding changes this team has actually shipped — likely the single highest-value check on this list, since it replaces a generic industry figure with this team's own track record.
3. Days 1-5, in parallel: pull whatever already exists on *why* people leave — session recordings, support tickets, and error/latency telemetry on the step itself — and sort the likely cause into on-screen confusion, an external requirement people can't meet in the moment, or a technical fault. If there's no hard legal or compliance requirement tied to the step, consider running a reversible test that makes the step skippable or deferrable for a slice of new users for two weeks; it doubles as a cheap fix and the sharpest available diagnostic, but only where nothing forces the step to stay mandatory.
4. Week 2: run the sample-size math against actual traffic, so the eventual target is one that can be measured within the quarter rather than argued about at the end of it, and price the downstream value of a marginal rescued completer (typically less than an average one) before finalizing how much of the quarter is worth committing.
5. End of week 2 — decision gate: set the target as a range derived from the above, sized to whatever cause turns out to dominate, and adjust the redesign's remaining scope and budget to match, including reallocating part of the quarter if step three turns out not to be the outlier or the drop-off turns out to be doing useful qualification work rather than losing good customers.

## Risks of this plan

- Leadership reads the two-week delay as stalling. Frame it explicitly as "date and shape now, number in ten days," not silence, to keep it from reading as avoidance.
- The team hits whatever number gets set by making the metric easier to move rather than the product better — cutting a required field, deferring it, or adding friction to leaving — while activation or retention quietly falls. A downstream guardrail metric has to be in place before the redesign ships, not bolted on afterward, or this failure mode won't be visible until it's too late to fix cheaply.
- If actual traffic through step three is low, even a real, worthwhile improvement can be statistically indistinguishable from ordinary week-to-week noise inside a quarter. Decide up front whether the claim can actually be measured on the available volume, or whether success will have to be argued qualitatively instead.
- The diagnostic could show that step three is functioning as a qualification gate rather than a defect — that is, the people it filters out were unlikely to be good customers anyway (unverified, and worth checking directly before spending anything on this). If so, halving the drop-off could mean more support burden and a worse cohort downstream, arriving underneath an improving dashboard.
- Cost of waiting two weeks: on the order of one analyst-week plus partial engineering time, against a full quarter of committed work — a favorable trade even if the diagnostic ultimately confirms the original plan, because it converts a guessed number into an earned one.

## Strongest counter-position

The strongest case against this plan: the two weeks might not buy anything. Everything above — the segmentation, the replay review, the power calculation, the skip test — assumes reasonably fast access to analytics tooling, session replay or support tickets, and enough traffic and experiment infrastructure to run something real. None of that is confirmed. If it doesn't exist, "spend two weeks diagnosing first" is not a more careful version of the plan; it produces the appearance of rigor and then lands on the same blind guess anyway, just later and with less of the quarter left to act on it — the team may be structurally forced into a blind bet regardless of how well-designed the diagnostic looks on paper. This wins if trying to actually run steps 2-3 above reveals that basic segmentation or session data takes longer to get at than the two-week window itself — and if it does, the honest move is not to promise a diagnostic that can't run: say so plainly, pick the least-bad number with wide, stated error bars, and spend the time on whatever partial signal is actually available, even if that's just reading existing support tickets by hand, rather than on a diagnostic plan borrowed from a company with better instrumentation.

A narrower, related version of the same challenge: the number may not be negotiable at all — already promised upward in a board deck or a commitment made before the team doing the work was looped in. Ask that directly, in the very first conversation. But even a firm "no, it's locked" doesn't remove the deeper issue: a number set on top of an undiagnosed baseline is a guess whether or not there was ever room to negotiate it, and the two weeks are still worth spending on the guardrail metric and the measurement plan even when the target itself can't move.

## Confidence

High: committing to a specific number before any diagnosis is a mistake, and step-three completion alone is the wrong thing to commit to — it is easy to move without fixing anything and says nothing about downstream value. This holds regardless of what the diagnostic finds.

Medium: that step three is even the right step to fix. Plausible given the 40% figure, but unconfirmed against how the neighboring steps perform — the single most decisive two-day check available.

Low: any specific number, including "half," until the segmentation and likely cause are known. The 5-25%-typical-versus-top-decile-halving framing above is a planning prior drawn from general product experience, not a fact about this particular funnel.

One shared assumption sits underneath nearly all of the reasoning above and deserves to be named on its own: that a reasonably fast, cheap way to run this diagnostic — existing analytics segmentation, session replay or support-ticket access, basic experiment infrastructure — actually exists at this company. If it doesn't, the two-week diagnostic plan has to shrink to whatever is actually available, and some of the specific checks above (especially a live skip/defer test) may not be executable on that timeline. This is worth confirming before the ten-day clock above starts running.

Material unknowns, roughly in order of how much they'd change this answer: whether the diagnostic tooling described above actually exists; whether the target is genuinely negotiable or already committed upward; the actual traffic volume through step three, which decides whether any target chosen is measurable within a quarter at all; and how much of the 40% is inflated by session-scoping, cross-device completion, or simply having picked the worst-looking step for attention.

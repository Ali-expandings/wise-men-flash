## Recommendation
Don't bring leadership "cut it in half" as the target yet. Spend the first one to two weeks of the quarter validating and segmenting the 40% figure and pulling evidence you already have, then bring leadership a dated, two-part number: a provisional, range-bound target on step-three completion paired with a downstream guardrail metric that has to hold or improve, plus a firm date to replace the provisional number with a diagnosis-derived one.

## Why
A target set before diagnosis isn't a forecast, it's a guess dressed as a commitment — and a round, symmetric number like "half" has no distribution behind it; nobody has shown why a 50% relative reduction is right instead of 20% or 70%. Single-step onboarding redesigns typically land in something like a 10-30% relative reduction in drop-off, with a 50%+ reduction usually showing up only when the cause is one specific, removable defect rather than general friction (unverified — pattern-matched from typical CRO/UX outcomes, not this product's data; treat as the burden of proof, not a plan number).

Step-three completion alone is also a metric that can be moved without moving the underlying problem: splitting the step in two, making a field optional, deferring a requirement to later, or tightening who counts as "entering" the step can all shrink the number on the dashboard while the user's actual experience — or the business's downstream numbers — doesn't improve, and may get worse if people who were rightly filtered out now proceed unprepared. A 40% drop at a step this deep into onboarding is disproportionately likely to include a real commitment ask (payment, data import, identity, a teammate's action), which makes some of that drop a sorting function, not pure loss — the distinction matters because a fix that "helps" a qualification gate can quietly hurt activation and support load instead. Whether that's true here is exactly what a short diagnostic settles, and it's cheap to check before committing a quarter and a number to it.

The project's shape compounds the risk: a single redesign that ships once, late in the quarter, is hard to read cleanly against seasonality, novelty effects, and whatever else changes in the same window, and it leaves no room to change course if the diagnosis was wrong.

## What to do
Week 1: pin down what "40%" actually means (per-session or per-user, share of step-3 entrants or of all funnel starts, whether it counts people who return and finish within a few days) and pull its trend over several months, not just the latest one, to see whether it's stable or already drifting. Segment by channel, platform, self-serve vs. invited, plan, and locale — an aggregate rate like this is often one bad segment dragging down an otherwise fine average. In parallel, pull step-three's weekly traffic volume and your own past onboarding experiment results — that tells you both what change would even be statistically detectable inside the quarter's post-launch window, and what magnitude of lift this team has actually achieved before, which predicts this project's ceiling better than any outside benchmark.

Week 2: read session replays and support tickets, and talk to five to eight people who dropped at step three, to name the two to four concrete blockers actually driving the number — some are usually mechanical and cheaply fixable, which is exactly the case where a steep target becomes defensible. At the end of week two, take leadership the two-part number — provisional range plus the guardrail metric (activation and 30-day retention, or "activated users per 100 onboarding starts") — and a firm date for converting it to a diagnosis-derived target; if the diagnosis turns up one dominant, clearly removable blocker, commit to the full halving at that point and say so.

For delivery, don't structure the quarter as one redesign that ships in week eleven. Sequence it as four to six smaller changes, each shipped behind a holdout or A/B test with a stated kill rule, so there's a clean read on what's working and room to change direction mid-quarter instead of finding out at the end.

## Risks of this plan
- Leadership may resist "no number yet" — the mitigation is to give them the provisional range and the diagnostic date on day one, not silence, so it doesn't read as stalling.
- The guardrail metric moves slower than step-three completion; a quarter may not be enough to see it clearly, which can make a real win look unproven or a real problem look hidden until it's too late to matter for this quarter's read.
- Segmentation or replay work may show the real cause sits upstream of step three or is structural (needs a third party, an admin, a document the user doesn't have on hand) — finding that out in week one or two is cheap; finding it out mid-quarter after committing headcount to a step-three redesign is not.
- The one to two weeks spent validating costs real time against a team that wants to start building — weigh that against the cost of chasing a number nobody derived and either missing it visibly or hitting it by moving the friction somewhere uninstrumented.
- If step-three traffic turns out to be low volume, no amount of process makes the number measurable inside a quarter — the fallback in that case is qualitative blocker-removal milestones plus the guardrail, not a percentage.

## Strongest counter-position
The strongest case against this plan is that it doesn't go far enough: step three may function more as a commitment gate than a friction point, and if so, no step-three completion target — provisional, range-bound, or guardrailed — should be set at all, because it's locally gameable in ways a slower-moving guardrail metric won't catch until after the quarter's resourcing and performance decisions are already locked in. Under that view, the only number that belongs in front of leadership right now is a diagnostic timeline — three weeks, fixed cost — and a committed date for setting an outcome target on something that can't be moved by relocating friction, such as activated users per 100 onboarding starts at day 30. This wins if early evidence shows most droppers are stopping at a genuine commitment ask rather than a fixable defect; it's why the diagnosis above should explicitly ask "gate or defect" as its first question, and why the guardrail metric in this plan needs to be checked often enough, and trusted enough, to actually block a step-three "win" that isn't real — if it can't do that, drop the step-three number and adopt this counter-position outright.

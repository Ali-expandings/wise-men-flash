## Council Debate: December Code Freeze — Is It Protecting Us or Moving the Risk to January?

**Council Members:** Dana (release engineering lead — batch size & continuous deployment), Marcus (VP Infrastructure/Ops — staffing & incident-response capacity), Priya (SRE / incident-analysis lead — measurement & causal rigor), Owen (pragmatic engineering director — organizational implementation)
**Rounds:** 3 (Positions → Responses → Synthesis)

### Round 1: Initial Positions

**Dana (release engineering lead):**
Let's name what's actually happening: you're not preventing risk during the freeze, you're inventory-stockpiling it. Two weeks of merged-but-unshipped code from 60 engineers is a batch, and batch size is the single most predictive variable we have for change failure rate — that's not opinion, it's the best-replicated finding in the whole Accelerate/DORA research program. When the freeze lifts, you're not deploying code, you're deploying two weeks of interactions between changes nobody tested together. Every incident review blaming "a different specific bug" is missing the pattern because the pattern isn't in the bugs — it's in the batch size. My position: the freeze is the proximate cause, full stop. Fix is decoupling merge from release — trunk-based dev, flags dark-launched, small continuous deploys straight through December. Freeze customer-visible risk, not shipping itself.

**Marcus (VP Infrastructure/Ops):**
Let's be precise about what the freeze is buying us. December: half my senior on-call is on PTO, support is running skeleton crew, and half of leadership is unreachable if we need an exec call on a sev-1. That's not bureaucracy — that's the actual bench we'd be defending an incident with. Trading that for January, when everyone's back and nobody's on a beach, isn't "moving risk," it's choosing when we can least afford to be short-handed versus when we can. The real bug isn't the freeze — it's that 60 engineers treat it as a queue instead of a gate. Two weeks of unreviewed-in-production change lands in one day because nobody's shipping incrementally once freeze prep starts. Fix thaw week — staggered rollout, canary the backlog, don't open every gate at once — and you fix January without touching December's staffing problem.

**Priya (SRE / incident-analysis lead):**
Three years running, same two-week window — that's not a coincidence, that's a sample size. And the fact that every postmortem names a different root-cause bug is exactly what I'd expect if the real cause is structural: blameless postmortems are designed to close on one proximate trigger, not to aggregate across incidents. Nobody's plotted change-failure-rate against batch size — PRs merged since last deploy, lines changed, number of authors shipping in the same window, time-to-detect when everything lands at once. So before anyone tells me the freeze is protecting us or just deferring the blast radius to January, I want to know: what's December's incident rate per deploy, traffic-adjusted, versus January's? Nobody's run that comparison. Until we do, every causal story at this table — mine included — is a guess wearing a postmortem's clothing.

**Owen (pragmatic engineering director):**
Here's what nobody's naming: the freeze doesn't reduce risk, it batches it. Two weeks of merged-but-unshipped code becomes one giant deploy the moment the freeze lifts, right when half the senior team is still trickling back from PTO and the on-call rotation skews toward whoever's newest. That's not a coincidence producing a different bug each time — that's the same mechanism wearing a different bug each time. My problem with "just abolish the freeze" is that nobody's deploy muscle survives two weeks off either, so mistakes go up regardless. My problem with "leave it alone" is we've had three Januarys of proof it doesn't work. I want a staggered unfreeze — canary a subset of merged changes back in daily during week one of January, seniors explicitly on call for it, before we open the floodgates on everything at once.

### Round 2: Responses & Challenges

**Dana:**
Marcus, you've conflated "deploy" with "customer-visible change" — that's the whole bug in your staffing argument. Thin December bench is real, but the fix isn't stockpiling for a full-bench January dump, it's dark-launching behind flags all December: code ships to prod inert, nothing customer-facing, no incident bait for a skeleton crew. You even named the actual problem yourself — "60 engineers treat it as a queue instead of a gate" — then proposed thaw week, which just staggers the queue instead of draining it. That's not a fix, it's a slower stockpile. Priya, I'll take the challenge, but look at your own metric list — PRs merged since last deploy, authors per window, time-to-detect. That's batch size, decomposed. Run it and you're not testing my claim against a null, you're instrumenting it. Owen — staggered unfreeze still batches, just four times instead of once. Stop the on-ramp, not the off-ramp.

**Marcus:**
Dana's decoupling pitch sounds free until you cost it: trunk-based dev, dark-launched flags, continuous deploy discipline — that's a rebuild of how 60 engineers work, months of investment, and until it's done you're deploying half-finished capability into a season we're already short-staffed for. That's not a fix for December, that's a multi-quarter platform bet. Priya's right to demand the comparison, and fair enough — she should hold my staffing claim to the same bar. Where I land with Owen: staggered unfreeze, seniors explicitly on the hook for it, canary the backlog daily instead of one big-bang cutover. That's the fix that doesn't require reengineering the org first.

**Priya:**
Dana's batch-size story fits the DORA literature — that's a strong outside prior, not evidence from our three years. Marcus's staffing story fits those same three years equally well. Owen's "same mechanism, different bug" splits the difference without picking either. Three confident causal stories, zero shared evidence, because batch-lift and PTO-return move together every single year — nobody can tell "big batch caused it" from "thin bench caused it" when both variables always move at once. That's the actual confound. Which is why Owen's staggered-unfreeze proposal, whatever his causal story, is the first testable thing said at this table: canary daily, seniors explicitly on call, batch size varies day-to-day while staffing stays constant and high. Incidents still tracking batch size under full staffing — Dana's mechanism. Flat regardless of batch — Marcus's. That's an experiment. Someone define the incident metric before we run it.

**Owen:**

Mandate it now and half the org fakes compliance with flags nobody's actually tested toggling — that's a worse failure mode than the freeze. Bank it as the real fix, on a real timeline. Marcus, thaw week alone isn't sufficient. Staggering the rollout doesn't answer who's staggering it — if it's the same junior-skewed January rotation, you get a slower-motion version of the same crash, not a safer one. Your own logic about needing seniors defending a sev-1 cuts both ways — pair thaw week with an explicit senior-on-call requirement for week one, or you've just relabeled the floodgate.

### Council Synthesis

**Areas of Convergence:**
- The freeze doesn't eliminate risk — it defers and concentrates it. All four members converged on this framing by Round 3, including Marcus, who started by defending the freeze outright.
- The current pattern — everything held for two weeks then released at once — is the actual problem, independent of which underlying mechanism (batch size or staffing) turns out to dominate.
- Concrete near-term fix, backed by all four: a **staggered unfreeze** — canary the merged backlog back into production incrementally (e.g., daily) during the first one to two weeks of January — **with senior engineers explicitly rostered on call**, rather than the default rotation that currently skews junior because seniors front-load PTO around the holidays.
- Nobody has ever measured which mechanism actually drives the January spike. Batch size (PRs merged since last deploy, authors shipping simultaneously) and staffing depth (senior on-call availability, MTTR) have moved together every single year — a confound nobody had named until this debate. Three years of postmortems each naming "a different specific bug" is consistent with a structural blind spot in the review process, not with there being no pattern.
- Decoupling merge from release (trunk-based development, feature flags, dark launches) is accepted by everyone as the right long-term direction — but Marcus and Owen both refused to mandate it org-wide before January, calling it a multi-quarter capability investment, not a quick fix; forcing it now risks faked compliance (flags nobody's actually tested toggling), which Owen called a worse failure mode than the freeze itself.

**Remaining Disagreements:**
- **Root mechanism**: Dana holds that batch size is the primary driver and that staggered unfreeze is still "a slower stockpile," not a real fix. Marcus holds that staffing/capacity is at least as large a factor. Owen thinks they're two names for the same dynamic. Priya refuses to pick a side, arguing the evidence to distinguish them doesn't exist yet — and that manufacturing false confidence here would just repeat the postmortem process's original sin.
- **Pace of the long-term fix**: Dana wants decoupling started now, treating "multi-quarter" as a reason to begin immediately rather than wait. Marcus and Owen want it banked as a bounded Q1 pilot on one team, proven against metrics before wider rollout.

**Recommended Path:**
Run the staggered unfreeze in January as a deliberate experiment, not just a mitigation: canary the merged backlog back in daily (or in a small number of stages) with senior engineers explicitly required on call for that window, and instrument it with the metrics the council converged on — PRs merged since last deploy, distinct authors shipping in the same window, change-failure-rate per deploy (traffic-adjusted), and time-to-detect. Because batch size and staffing depth have always moved together in the past, this design is the first one that varies batch size while holding staffing constant and high — which finally lets the org tell whether incidents track the size of what's released (Dana's mechanism) or stay flat regardless (Marcus's). In parallel, fund one team to pilot Dana's decoupled/dark-launch deployment approach in Q1 as a bounded, measured experiment rather than an org-wide mandate, earning broader rollout only if it beats the staggered-unfreeze baseline on the same metrics. Structurally, fix the blind spot that let this run three years unnoticed: require each incident postmortem to be checked against the prior two Januaries before it closes, so a recurring seasonal pattern can't keep hiding behind a rotating cast of "different specific bugs."

Direct answer to the original question: both "protecting us" and "just moving the risk" are consistent with three years of data, because the organization has never separated them. What's observable without more data is that the freeze converts a steady stream of small, individually-recoverable changes into one or a few concentrated releases, timed to land exactly when the org is least prepared to absorb one — rusty deploy habits, a junior-skewed on-call rotation, and backlog pressure to ship everything at once. That alone means the freeze is, at minimum, moving risk rather than removing it. Whether it is also manufacturing additional risk on top of that — via the batch-interaction effects Dana describes — is a real, open, and now-testable question, not a settled one.

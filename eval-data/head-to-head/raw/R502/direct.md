Freeze stops deploys, not merges. Two weeks of 60 engineers' work piles up in the queue, then ships as one giant batch the day freeze lifts. That's the mechanism, and it explains everything else.

**Why batch size, not month, drives the outages.** Risk of a deploy scales with number of interacting changes, not calendar. One change breaks: obvious what broke, fast rollback, contained blast radius. Two weeks of 60 engineers' changes land together: independent changes interacting in ways never tested in combination, unclear which change caused which symptom, rollback hard because changes are entangled with each other. Add: code merged days or weeks earlier means author context has decayed by ship time — slower triage when it breaks. Add: "clear the queue" pressure after freeze lifts usually means shipping faster with less individual scrutiny per change, not more.

**Lower January traffic is the tell, not a counterpoint.** If load drove this, December — higher traffic — would be worse. It isn't. Traffic down, incidents up, three years running: that isolates the cause away from load and toward something structurally different about how code ships in January. Big-bang deploy of accumulated change fits; traffic doesn't.

**Why reviews keep missing the pattern.** Each postmortem does root-cause analysis on one incident and correctly finds a proximate bug — that part's not wrong. But no single incident review is scoped to ask "is deploy batch size itself a risk factor across incidents." That's a cross-incident question, and nobody's tracking the aggregate metric (incident rate vs. deploy size, or incident timing relative to freeze-lift date) that would surface it. Proximate cause differs every time; accelerant is constant. Standard blind spot — postmortems find what broke, not why breaks cluster.

**Protecting or moving risk: moving it, probably with a multiplier.** Goal of protecting high-stakes December days from deploy risk is legitimate. But "stop deploying, let merges queue, dump it all at once" doesn't delay risk evenly — it concentrates many weeks of change into one high-risk event. Total risk shipped is likely higher than if those changes had deployed incrementally across the same weeks. Freeze isn't neutral time-shifting; it's batching, and batching is its own risk multiplier on top of relocation.

**Cheap way to confirm before next January.** Check the three past Januaries: do incidents cluster in the first days right after freeze lifts (big-bang signature) rather than spreading evenly through the month? Is deploy size (commits/services touched) in that window abnormally large vs. a typical week? Does broken code trace specifically to freeze-window merges vs. normal same-day-merge-and-ship code? Yes to those confirms the mechanism without waiting for a fourth data point.

**Fix keeps freeze's goal, changes its mechanism.** Options: decouple merge from release with feature flags, so "freeze" means flags stay off, not code stays unshipped — January lift becomes flag flips, not a code dump, and flags can flip gradually with rollback per-feature. Or thin the freeze — allow low-risk/flagged changes through continuously, freeze only what actually touches checkout/revenue paths. Or force staged rollout of the backlog in January instead of one shipment — canary a subset of accumulated changes at a time. Any of these keeps December protected without concentrating two weeks of risk into one deploy.

## Recommendation

Before changing the freeze policy at all, spend a few days mining the deploy and incident history you already have to test one specific mechanism: that the freeze stops deploys but not merges, so two weeks of accumulated, untested-together changes land as one uncontrolled release the moment the freeze lifts, and that this — not any single bug — is what's driving the January pattern. If that test confirms it, as the internal logic of your own facts suggests it will, fix the release mechanics rather than the freeze: decouple merging from releasing during the freeze window, and replace the single hard cutover with a staged, dependency-ordered rollout.

## Why

A deploy freeze that still allows merges doesn't reduce how much code is heading to production — it just reclassifies two weeks of continuously-arriving change as one giant, never-tested-together batch that detonates the moment the freeze lifts. That mechanism, not any specific bug, is the natural explanation for why every incident review finds a different culprit: in a big batch, which of a hundred-plus simultaneous changes fails first is close to random, so the proximate cause rotates while the underlying cause — the batch itself — stays constant. A review built to close one incident at a time has no way to notice that. This also explains the traffic detail: outages are overwhelmingly driven by changes, not load, so lower January traffic doesn't buy you safety when the volume and novelty of change is what spiked, not the number of users hitting it. Small, frequent releases tend to be associated with lower change-failure rates and faster recovery than the same volume of change shipped in large, infrequent batches — a large batch multiplies the number of ways changes can interact with each other, and multiplies how long it takes to trace a failure back to its cause, because you're searching a hundred commits instead of one.

Part of what keeps the backlog growing is incentive, not just habit: merging during the freeze costs an engineer nothing, since nothing ships until it lifts and reviewers thinned out by the holidays tend to wave things through on the assumption that it won't go out until January, while merging just before the freeze often still counts as delivered work for the quarter. Sixty engineers each have a private reason to keep merging through a freeze that was only ever meant to pause deployment, not the incentive to commit code — and nobody who sets or benefits from that incentive is the one who pays for the batch it produces in January.

The freeze does do real work: nothing shipped during it caused an incident during it, by construction, and that protects your highest-traffic two weeks of the year, which are typically also the most thinly-staffed over the holidays. But "protecting December" and "moving the risk to January" aren't the only two possibilities, and treating them as an either/or is the wrong frame. The likelier picture is that the freeze protects a narrow window while the batching side-effect makes the total, year-round risk worse than it would be if the same changes had shipped in their normal smaller sizes — not a 1:1 relocation, an amplification.

That said, this diagnosis, however well it fits the facts you've given, is not yet confirmed against your own data, and every plausible rival explanation — thin holiday code review, thin holiday on-call, year-boundary bugs in dates/certs/quotas/licensing, vendors lifting their own freezes the same week, capacity scaled down over the quiet period meeting a sharp return-to-office spike — would produce this exact same signature: three years running, lower traffic, a different named bug every time. None of those rivals are ruled out by anything in the question as posed. Nor is the freeze's own three-year history able to separate "the freeze causes this" from "the calendar causes this," because the freeze's dates haven't varied — every year you're comparing the same window against itself. What can separate them is comparing against periods where batch size and calendar are NOT tied together: other multi-day pauses in the year that produce a backlog with no holiday attached, and comparing services or teams whose freeze-period backlogs differ in size. If outage rate tracks backlog size in both cases, that's the batch. If it doesn't — if a big non-January backlog ships cleanly, or small-backlog services break just as often as large ones — the batch theory is wrong and the calendar (or something else) is doing this instead.

## What to do

Settle the mechanism before touching the policy — this is a data question, not a judgment call, and the data already exists:

1. For each of the last three Decembers–Januaries, link every incident to the deploy that triggered it (or mark "no triggering deploy"), and tag it as a freeze-merged change, a fresh change, or unrelated to any change.
2. Compare the size and failure rate of the first post-freeze release against your typical release, and separately against the first release after any OTHER multi-day pause this year that has nothing to do with the winter holiday (an offsite, an incident-driven freeze, any gap of similar length) — this is what actually separates "big batch" from "December/January calendar," since the freeze's own timing never varies on its own.
3. Where backlog size differs by service or team, check whether outage rate tracks it. If it does, that's the batch effect operating independent of the calendar.
4. Check whether incidents cluster in the hours/days right after the post-freeze release, or instead land on fixed calendar dates (year-rollover jobs, cert/quota/license expiry, vendor release dates) regardless of what shipped.
5. Put a number on the actual question asked: estimate December outage-minutes prevented (your normal change-failure rate × the deploys you skipped × December's revenue-per-minute and staffing cost) against January's actual extra outage-minutes at January's rates. That comparison, not intuition, is what tells you whether the freeze is a net win.
6. Independent of what the diagnostic shows: check whether engineering goals or velocity metrics count code as delivered when it's merged rather than when it's verified running in production. If they do, that's a standing incentive to keep merging through the freeze no matter what else you fix — and redefining "done" as "verified in production" costs almost nothing.

This is days of analysis against logs you already have, not a new build. If it confirms the batch mechanism — which the shape of your own facts makes likely — the fix is to stop letting the backlog form in the first place: during the freeze, merges land only behind flags defaulted off, or wait as open PRs if they can't be flagged (this especially matters for the wide refactors and dependency bumps that quiet weeks tend to attract, since those are what break hardest and can't be flagged away); then release the backlog in normal-sized, canaried steps in dependency order, with a named owner and the original authors available, instead of one hard cutover on day one. Keep the freeze's protective scope rather than eliminating it — narrow it over time to the highest-risk change types (schema, auth, payments, infrastructure) as your ability to ship small and roll back cleanly matures. Whatever the diagnostic shows, add batch size, time-since-merge, and rollback outcome as required fields on every incident review, and review the whole December–January window as one event, not incident-by-incident — that is the only way next year's version of this question gets answered instead of re-asked.

## Risks of this plan

Running the diagnostic first costs a few days of delay on a problem that's already three years old — a real but small cost against the risk of fixing the wrong thing: if you skip straight to flag-gating and staged rollout and the real driver turns out to be calendar- or vendor-side, you'll have spent real engineering effort and still get a bad January. Staging the release only helps if rollback and canaries are already trustworthy; without that, spreading the same batch over several days just spreads the same incidents over several days instead of reducing them. Dropping the freeze outright — the fix the question's own framing invites — without first building small-batch release discipline would expose your highest-revenue weeks, typically also your most thinly-staffed over the holidays, to exactly the risk you're currently deferring, which is very likely a worse trade, not a better one. And however the diagnostic comes out, the fix that follows has to land on someone: if the January release owner is whoever already covered the holiday on-call rotation, you've routed the recurring cost of this problem onto the same few people every year, which is its own slow failure. The cost of being wrong here is asymmetric with the cost of finding out: the diagnostic itself is cheap and reversible regardless of which way it points, so the real risk isn't in running it — it's in skipping it. Acting on the batching diagnosis without checking it first wastes engineering effort if the real cause turns out to be calendar-side; not checking at all, and leaving the freeze exactly as it is, guarantees a fourth year of the same unexamined pattern. Either way, the freeze's overall net value — December protection bought against January cost — stays unknown until the cost-weighted comparison above is actually run.

## Strongest counter-position

The case for leaving the freeze alone entirely, at least until the data comes back: a deploy freeze mostly delays defects rather than creating them — code merged in December carries the same bugs whenever it ships — so moving that risk out of your busiest, most thinly-staffed two weeks and into a quieter one where the team is fully back is arguably the freeze doing its job, not failing at it. On this view, the evidence you're judging the freeze by is structurally one-sided: "January incidents get postmortems, but the December outages the freeze prevented leave no record" — you only ever see the cost side of the ledger. And the three-year pattern doesn't actually implicate the freeze specifically, because the freeze's dates have never changed: "it's tangled up with everything else that happens in that window," so a repeat is exactly as consistent with "the calendar did it" — year-boundary bugs, vendors lifting their own freezes, deferred infrastructure work, December incidents under-reported by a skeleton crew — as with "the freeze did it." Read this way, "moving the risk to a cheaper window" isn't a bug in the freeze, it's a rational trade, and nothing here proves otherwise. This case wins if the diagnostic above comes back negative: if backlog size doesn't predict outage rate across services or across other non-holiday pauses, and January's incidents cluster on fixed calendar dates rather than in the days after the post-freeze release. In that world, the batching story is wrong, the freeze was never the problem, and the right move is to leave it in place and chase whichever calendar-linked cause the data actually points to instead.

## Confidence

Medium that batching from a decoupled merge/release process is a real, significant contributor to the January pattern — the mechanism is well-supported by how small-batch releases typically compare to large-batch ones in software delivery generally, and it cleanly explains why every incident review names a different bug without anyone connecting them; it is not High because it isn't yet checked against this org's own deploy and incident history, and the apparent breadth of support for it traces back to the same unverified premise restated rather than independently confirmed. Low on whether the freeze is a net win or net loss in total cost terms: that depends on the relative cost of a December outage-minute versus a January one, which nothing here establishes either way. High on the general claim that shipping the same volume of change in small frequent releases tends to fail less often and recover faster than shipping it in large infrequent ones — that's a well-established pattern in software delivery, not specific to this org. The material unknowns, in order: whether this org's January incidents actually trace to freeze-merged changes rather than to calendar, vendor, staffing or capacity effects that would hit January regardless of the freeze — the load-bearing assumption underneath the whole diagnosis; this org's normal (non-freeze) release cadence, since if releases are already large and infrequent year-round, the freeze doesn't spike batch size much and the entire mechanism weakens; and the relative cost of an outage-minute in December versus January, which decides whether "moving the risk" is a bad trade at all.

The question:
"""
Three months ago I wrote the analysis our executives used to justify closing our smallest regional office, and I've just found a formula error that overstated that office's losses by about 40%. The office is already closed and its staff were laid off. I can't decide whether my correction to the executive team should read as a plain technical fix or a personal apology, or whether it should say anything about how the decision might have gone with the right numbers. What tone is right for a message like this, and how do these usually go wrong?

Response A:
"""
## Recommendation

Write the correction as a short, first-person, factual note — one sentence owning the error, the corrected figures for every office in the original review, and confirmation you've independently re-verified the whole model — and leave out any sentence that says or implies whether the decision would have gone differently. Tell your manager in person before the note goes anywhere wider, and handle "does this change anything, and what do we owe the people affected" as a separate, verbal conversation with your manager and the decision-owner, not as content in the memo itself.

## Why

Prompt, complete, self-initiated correction is generally read far better than the same news arriving late, partial, or from someone else first — voluntarily surfacing your own error typically costs less than having it surface another way later. That argues strongly against sitting on this or softening it into a vague "model update."

An executed, irreversible decision like this one is rarely reopened by a later correction. Once staff, budgets, and announcements have moved, organizations generally update the forward-looking numbers and controls rather than reverse the action — and people who approved a hard call tend to defend it, not revisit it, when the challenge to it arrives from inside and in writing after the fact. The note should not be written to try to change the decision, because it almost certainly won't, and that shouldn't be its job.

A single error you've found is weak evidence that it's the only one. Before anything goes out, the whole model needs independent re-verification, including whether the same formula or allocation touched any other office or any number still in current use — because a second correction to the same analysis costs far more credibility than the first, and because if the error isn't isolated to the closed office, this stops being one analyst's note to send and becomes a formal restatement someone more senior should own.

You cannot know, from your analysis alone, whether the executive team would have decided differently with the correct numbers — real closure decisions usually weigh more than one model's output. Writing that claim, in either direction, substitutes your guess for their judgment, and it's exactly the kind of overreach — or, in the other direction, the defensive "the conclusion still holds" — that turns a correction into an argument about the decision instead of a fix to the record.

What you may owe the people who lost their jobs is a real question, and a separate one. They aren't the audience of this memo, and nothing addressed to the executive team reaches them. If that's part of what's driving the pull toward an apology, act on it directly and privately — for instance, raising rehire priority or references with whoever now owns that relationship — rather than folding it into the memo's tone.

## What to do

The note itself: plain first-person register, not a bare "formula corrected" notice and not a message built around how you feel. One sentence naming what broke and roughly how. One short line of apology for the error itself — not for the closure, which you didn't decide alone and can't fully explain in hindsight. Then the corrected numbers for every office from the original review, in the original format, so readers can see where things stand without you supplying the verdict. State plainly that the whole model has been independently re-checked. Close with what else is still being checked, and by when.

Leave out entirely: any sentence saying or implying the decision would or wouldn't have gone differently. That's the line that turns a correction into a verdict.

Before it goes anywhere: re-verify the whole model independently, not just the one figure, and check specifically whether the error reached any other office or any number still in use today — this determines who should actually own and send the correction. Once you're confident in the numbers, tell your manager in person, the same day, before the written note reaches anyone else — they should hear about a flaw behind a layoff decision from you, not from a cold email. Separately and verbally, walk your manager and whoever owns the closure decision through where the office now sits against the original criteria, and let them decide — with HR or legal if they think it's warranted — whether that comparison belongs in writing at all, and who should be the one to send it. Then send the written correction to exactly the people who received the original analysis, promptly once the numbers are confirmed — not faster than you can verify them, not so slow it starts to look like sitting on it.

If you want to act on what you may feel you owe the people who lost their jobs, do it as a separate, concrete, private step — for example asking whoever manages that relationship now about rehire priority or references — not as content or tone inside the executive correction.

## Risks of this plan

Written too sparely, even a fully factual note can still get filed as routine — numbers corrected, nothing more to see — and the question of whether it mattered quietly dies with it. The mitigation is making sure the verbal conversation with the decision-owner is a real conversation and not optional: if nobody who can act on "does this change anything" ever actually hears the question asked, factual-only was the wrong call.

Written too fully — including an explicit comparison against the original closure criteria, even stated as neutral data rather than a verdict — a note to the full executive distribution can still read as the analyst's own judgment on whether the layoffs were justified. That can carry real exposure for you personally and for the company, doesn't reopen a decision that in practice won't be reopened, and doesn't actually help the people who lost their jobs. That's the case for keeping the criteria comparison out of the wide note entirely and confining it to the narrower, verbal, legal-aware channel.

Waiting to verify properly costs a few days; that's cheap insurance against a second, worse correction. Waiting for a "better moment" to send it costs much more — if the error surfaces another way first, the same delay reads as concealment regardless of why you waited.

Cost of being wrong: nothing about the tone of this note is itself irreversible. The closure and the layoffs already happened, and no wording here changes that. If it later turns out more should have gone in writing to more people, that's a recoverable follow-up conversation, not a crisis — the irreversible decision this all traces back to was made three months ago, not in this note.

## Strongest counter-position

The case against keeping the decision-relevance out of the written note: "A correction that fixes the number but withholds its implication for the actual decision isn't neutral or professional — it's a second omission stacked on the first." On this view, the rerun against the original review's threshold belongs in the written note as mandatory content, stated plainly — "under the corrected figure, the decision would/would not have cleared the bar you applied" — not deferred to a private conversation that may or may not happen. Confined to a verbal channel with just the manager and decision-owner, the implication is too easy to let quietly die: a 40% error in the number that ended people's jobs gets filed as routine, "with nothing forcing anyone to ask whether the harm was necessary." This view treats "what tone keeps me safest" as the wrong question, and prioritizes what's owed to the people who paid for the error over what's comfortable for the writer or the executive team.

This wins if the verbal, narrower channel is where accountability goes to die: if weeks later nothing has changed in how the model or the decision are treated, nothing is added to the record, and nothing still reversible (a lease, a rehire decision, an open budget line) gets revisited — that's the sign the quieter path failed and the correction needed to be written and wide after all.

## Confidence

High — plain, ownership-first register beats both a bare technical notice and an apology-centered message, and manager-first sequencing with independent re-verification before anything goes out. These hold regardless of how the harder judgment calls below resolve.

Medium — whether the criteria/ranking comparison for the closed office belongs in the written note at all, or should stay strictly verbal. The evidence cuts both ways: written, it risks reading as a verdict with real personal and legal exposure; unwritten, it risks the implication being quietly dropped. This is a genuine judgment call that should involve your manager and, if either of you is unsure, legal or HR — not a first-line call to make alone.

Low — how much legal exposure a written comparison would actually carry. That depends on jurisdiction and on exactly how the layoffs were carried out, neither of which is stated here.

Material unknowns: whether the corrected figures actually change the office's ranking under the original criteria at all (if they don't, most of this tension dissolves and the note can simply be complete); whether the formula error reached any other office or any number still driving current decisions (this changes who should own and send the correction); and this organization's actual track record with self-reported errors, which shapes how much personal risk any of this genuinely carries for you.

"""
Separate three things you're currently treating as one decision, because collapsing them is exactly how these letters go wrong: the factual correction, the acknowledgment of fault, and the claim about what would have happened. They don't deserve equal airtime.

**Tone: factual and briefly owned, not clinical and not penitential.** Lead with the correction itself — what the formula error was, why it happened, what the corrected loss figure is — plainly, in the first paragraph, no hedging. That's what executives actually need and will act on. Then own it in one direct sentence: you found the error, it was yours, you're correcting it. Don't extend that into three paragraphs of remorse. Over-apologizing doesn't read as more accountable — it shifts the message's center of gravity from the numbers to your feelings, and it quietly puts the burden on the reader to reassure you. A memo that's mostly apology also buries the one thing they urgently need, which is the corrected number.

**Don't assert the counterfactual — that's the part to be most careful about.** "I made a formula error" and "the office shouldn't have closed" are different claims, and the second one you probably can't back. The original decision likely weighed things beyond this one figure — lease terms, strategic footprint, other offices under review — that you may not have full visibility into, and a corrected loss number alone doesn't tell you what the call would have been. State the correct numbers clearly and let the executives draw their own conclusion. If someone asks you directly whether it would have changed the outcome, "I don't know — that's not just my call to make" is honest, not evasive.

**This is exposure, not just tone.** The closure meant real layoffs. A written statement asserting the decision rested on bad numbers, paired with speculation that the outcome would have differed, is the kind of sentence that can resurface later if anyone affected ever disputes their termination. That's not a reason to soften the facts — get them exactly right and say them clearly — but it's a reason to keep speculation out of the written record, and if your company has any legal/HR function, loop them in before this goes to the full executive team, given people's jobs were on the line.

Where these usually go wrong, concretely:

- **Burying the correction under apology** — executives have to dig for the actual number.
- **Defensive minimizing** — "small error," "probably wouldn't have mattered anyway." Reads as covering yourself, especially given the stakes, and erodes trust in your next analysis more than the error itself does.
- **Asserting the counterfactual as fact** — claiming the decision would or wouldn't have changed, when you don't actually know that.
- **No remediation** — stating what broke without saying what you're checking elsewhere (same formula used in other models?) or what changes in your review process. Without that, the memo just documents a mistake instead of closing the loop.
- **Wrong sequencing** — the exec team finding out cold, with your manager blindsided alongside them. Give whoever owns that relationship a heads-up first.

Structurally: correction and corrected numbers first, one sentence of ownership, what you're checking/fixing going forward, then open for questions. Consider a short verbal heads-up to your manager or the decision-owner before the written version goes to the full executive team — for something this consequential, the first they hear of it shouldn't be a cold email.

## Recommendation

Write it as a restatement, not a technical memo and not an apology letter: lead with the corrected facts, own the error in one direct sentence, and state what the correction does to the original analysis's own conclusion — without pronouncing a verdict on what the executive team would have decided with the right numbers. Send it to your manager first, and to the executive team as a group only after that, not as a solo message straight to the distribution list.

## Why

A bare technical fix reads as minimizing something that, in hindsight, mattered enough to cost people their jobs — leaving the correction to speak for itself when real harm followed from the error invites the reading that you're protecting yourself, not informing them. A full personal apology has the opposite problem: it puts your feelings at the center of a document the executive team needs for information, and it tends to smuggle in a causal claim you can't actually prove — "my mistake cost people their jobs" states a certainty about a multi-factor decision that isn't yours to state, and reads badly if this is ever looked at closely by anyone outside the room. The register that avoids both — the one used for correcting a consequential record after the fact, in accounting restatements and professional errata — states the facts first, owns the error in a single first-person sentence, and stops. That one sentence should be about responsibility ("the error was mine") rather than about forgiveness ("I'm sorry, I hope you understand") — the difference matters, because a responsibility sentence informs and an appeal for understanding asks the reader to do something for you.

On the counterfactual: don't assert it, but don't go silent on it either — silence is the worst of the three options the question poses, not a safe middle one. Once someone reads "I overstated the losses by about 40%," the next question in their head is automatically "would we have decided differently?" — leaving that unaddressed reads as evasive once anyone does the arithmetic themselves. But stating an answer to it ("we wouldn't have closed it") overclaims certainty you don't have: whoever made that decision weighed things you likely never saw — lease terms, precedent for other sites, how this office compared against ones you didn't analyze — and it isn't your call to resolve unilaterally. The move that avoids both failure modes: if the original analysis scored the office against an explicit threshold or ranked it against other offices, recompute that specific result with the corrected number and report the result as a fact ("against the criteria the analysis used, the office now ranks fourth of six" or "still fails the loss threshold") rather than a verdict about the decision. That gives the executive team the one thing they actually need — whether the input that changed would have changed the output — without you pronouncing on a decision that was never entirely yours. Also worth doing purely for clarity: give both dollar figures, not just "40%" — the percentage alone doesn't say whether it's 40% of the reported loss or of the true one, and that ambiguity is worth closing.

On sequencing: the executive team is not the first audience for this, your manager is — telling them first, before anyone else, is the single biggest source of credibility you have, and it's cheap. Whoever sponsored or championed the closure should hear it privately before the wider group does, as a courtesy — but that person shouldn't be the one who decides whether HR or legal find out. The correction most exposes their earlier judgment, which is exactly why they're the wrong person to gatekeep who else learns about it; loop in your manager and, through them, HR/legal, in parallel with — not gated behind — that courtesy briefing.

## What to do

1. Before drafting anything, recompute the error from the original source data (not just the broken cell), and check whether the same formula or model template was used anywhere else — another office's analysis, a savings forecast, anything still live. This is the one piece of harm from this error that can still be prevented rather than just corrected, so if you find a pending decision riding on the same model, flag it and ask for a pause the same day — don't hold it for the full write-up.
2. If the original analysis scored the office against an explicit threshold, ranking, or payback figure, recompute that specific result with the corrected number. That result — not a guess about what leadership would have decided — is what belongs in the message. (If the original analysis was closer to an overall judgment call with no explicit re-runnable criteria, state the corrected magnitude plainly instead and don't force a threshold framing that isn't there.)
3. Tell your manager first, in person or by call, the same day you're confident in the number; mark the figures preliminary until independently checked. Get an independent check, ideally from finance, before this goes any wider — a second correction after this one would cost you far more credibility than the first error did.
4. Put two questions on record in writing, addressed to your manager and, through them, HR/legal — not left as something you resolve by omission: whether anything still pending depends on the same model and needs to pause, and whether the correction bears on anything told to or owed to the laid-off staff. Frame the second one as routing a question, not arguing a case — "these figures changed; please advise whether anything needs revisiting" — so it reads as process, not as you building a case against the company. If your manager won't send that question onward, send it to HR directly yourself.
5. Brief whichever executive sponsored the closure privately, before the group send — but in parallel with, not gating, the HR/legal question above.
6. Send the written correction to everyone who received the original analysis, with the same prominence the original had — a plain, specific subject line, one screen of text with the formula detail in an attachment, and an offer to walk anyone through it. Don't send it as a quiet addendum to a routine update.
7. Within a couple of weeks, propose one concrete safeguard going forward — an independent second check on any analysis that feeds a headcount decision. That's what keeps this from being remembered as purely a personal failure.

Before sending anything, also check: whether any claim, grievance, or legal hold already exists around this closure (if so, this sequencing changes — counsel needs to be involved before your manager or the sponsor, and may shape or send the message itself; this is a question for whoever handles employment matters at your organization, not something to guess at); whether your organization has a formal error- or incident-reporting channel you're expected to use; and where else the original figure was quoted — board materials, consultation or severance paperwork, budgets — since correcting those is your organization's call to make, not something to do unilaterally.

## Risks of this plan

- Minimizing — passive voice, a vague subject line, folding this into a routine update — reads as concealment in hindsight once anyone connects it to the layoffs, and that costs more trust than a direct disclosure would have.
- Over-personalizing — centering your own feelings, or writing an unprovable causal claim like "my error cost people their jobs" — puts the reader in the position of managing you instead of acting on the correction, and states a certainty about causation you don't actually have (whether such a line carries legal weight beyond the reputational cost is a question for whoever handles employment matters at your organization — unverified here).
- Sequencing it wrong — your manager or the sponsor hearing about this secondhand, or in the same group message as everyone else — reads as blindsiding a senior person regardless of how carefully the message itself is worded.
- A second, incomplete correction — missing a knock-on effect on another office's numbers or a savings forecast, or overwriting the original file so the record of what changed and when is unclear. A follow-up correction costs more than the first one did.
- The correction landing and then nothing happening — no pause on a live decision that turns out to ride the same formula, no answer on record for whether anything is owed to the laid-off staff — because neither question was ever put in writing to someone positioned to act on it.

The closure itself can't be undone by any version of this message — that part of the cost is already sunk regardless of tone. What's still live is whether the same error is quietly running somewhere else right now, whether anyone ever answers the one question the laid-off staff have no way to ask for themselves, and whether your own future work is trusted the same way again. None of those improve by waiting for a better-worded draft; the ones with real time value (a live decision on the same model) get worse the longer they wait.

## Strongest counter-position

The strongest case against including any personal acknowledgment at all: an apology, however brief and carefully hedged, functions as a request for forgiveness, and a request for forgiveness is easy to grant — "thanks for flagging it, don't beat yourself up" is a realistic, likely response, and it closes the conversation before anyone examines whether the decision itself deserves a second look. On this view the tone question is premature until materiality is established — recompute the original decision criteria with the corrected number first; if the office still fails them by a wide margin, spending any emotional weight on an apology manufactures a crisis over an error that didn't actually change the outcome, and if the criteria now come out differently, no amount of careful wording turns this into a minor technical fix regardless. There's a sharper edge to this position too: a personally-voiced apology lets an organization treat what may be a process failure — a headcount decision resting on one analyst's unreviewed model — as one person's individual slip, which is precisely the framing that teaches the next person who finds an error in an already-executed decision to stay quiet about it. Under this view the artifact should be an institutional correction notice routed through your manager, not a personally apologetic message, and ownership should be a single factual sentence — "the error was mine; the formula did X instead of Y" — with no separate apology sentence attached to it.

This wins if your read of this specific executive team is that acknowledgment functions socially as closure rather than as information you want them to sit with — if "don't worry about it" is a realistic response here, a careful apology sentence buys you goodwill at the cost of the scrutiny this decision may actually deserve. It also wins outright if the materiality check in step 2 shows the correction doesn't change the result at all. What would show it: after sending a version with no apology sentence, whether the conversation moves to the substantive questions (is the model used elsewhere, does anything change for the laid-off staff) or instead reads as cold and stalls — that reaction is itself the information neither position can supply in advance.

Both conclusions hold regardless of this organization's specific culture, and nothing in the reasoning behind either one depends on facts not in evidence.

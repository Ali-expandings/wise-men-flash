## Recommendation

Don't decide based on the tip, and don't discard it either — convert it into a standing, disclosed screening step that applies to every finalist for this role, never built on or citing what your colleague told you, and never singling out this one candidate. Decide the hire on what that process turns up plus what you can observe directly: hire and drop the rumor if the record comes back clean, and build in early people-management oversight either way.

## Why

Three duties are owed here, and none of them are satisfied by picking a side between "trust the glowing references" and "quietly weight the rumor."

You owe the candidate a decision built on evidence they can actually answer. An internal investigation that ended with someone leaving, outcome unknown, is not a finding — treating it as one, even silently, convicts them of something they never got to address. If they were actually a witness or complainant in that investigation rather than its subject, holding it against them at all risks something closer to retaliation than caution, and retaliating against someone for reporting or participating in a workplace investigation is independently unlawful in a number of jurisdictions (unverified — confirm this when you talk to counsel, since it varies by jurisdiction and by exactly what role the candidate played). Glowing references don't resolve any of this either way: they're chosen by the candidate, so they show the candidate has people willing to vouch for them, not that the underlying claim is false — references typically skew toward peers and superiors, who are usually the last people to see how someone treats those with less power than them.

You owe the people who'd report to this person real diligence, not a shrug. A team lead's job is largely defined by how they treat people below them, so a specific, plausible claim on exactly that axis is the competency you're supposed to be gatekeeping, not a rumor you're free to wave off because it's uncitable. But that duty is a duty to try, discharged by real process — not a license to convict the candidate on a claim they can't see.

You owe your colleague confidentiality essentially as given: not her name, not the claim asserted as fact to anyone, and nothing that would let the source be inferred. That constrains the method, not the response — a promise to keep a secret is not a promise to act as though you never heard it. It also means telling her, briefly, what you intend to do and not do, and asking only how she knows (firsthand or secondhand, and whether the candidate was the subject of the investigation or a witness or complainant to it) — never asking her to dig further herself, and never repeating the claim to anyone outside a strict need-to-know channel (your own employment counsel or HR, verbally, without her name).

You also owe your employer a defensible process: as the hiring manager, what you know is functionally what the company knows, so silently doing nothing and having a real problem surface later is its own exposure.

What actually resolves this is the option the question's own framing leaves out: this isn't "act on the tip or ignore it," it's "use the tip to fix a gap in your process that exists regardless of whether the tip is true." Right now there's no independent, candidate-answerable check on how any finalist for a people-leadership role has treated the people below them. Close that gap for this candidate and every future one, through channels that owe nothing to your colleague's confidence and never require citing what she told you:

- A short, signed written question to every finalist for this and future lead roles: whether they've been the subject of an upheld finding, or an investigation that was still open when they left a prior role, with room to explain. A false answer, if it surfaces later through a legitimate channel, is a clean basis to act; a true, disclosed answer lets you judge the explanation rather than the silence.

Skip informal, uninvited back-channel calls to people at the old employer who aren't on any list. It looks like the more cautious, lower-profile option and is actually the worst-positioned path for what you're trying to protect: it targets this one candidate in a way no one else faces, and it goes looking for signal from exactly the small pool of people who might know about the investigation — which is how a colleague who asked not to be named ends up identified anyway. Applied evenly across every finalist, the disclosure-and-reference approach gets you comparable information without that exposure, and gives the candidate the hearing that silence and back-channeling both deny them.

None of this produces certainty. An investigation that ends with a resignation often closes without a recorded finding, so "find out how it ended" was never going to be available to you — the signed answer and the direct-report reference are the realistic substitute, not a full one.

## What to do

1. Today: pause. Don't reject, don't extend an unconditional offer, don't contact the old employer, and put nothing about the tip in writing anywhere it could be searched or discovered later (email, applicant-tracking notes, panel debrief).
2. Within 48 hours: one short call with your colleague. Ask only how she knows (direct knowledge or secondhand) and whether the candidate was the subject of the investigation or a witness or complainant to it — that distinction matters for how carefully you handle what comes next, even though it doesn't change the process itself. Tell her plainly what you will and won't do: you won't name her, won't assert the claim as fact to anyone, and won't ask her to find out more.
3. This week: a short call to whoever handles employment risk for you — in-house counsel or HR if you have them; if not, your insurance broker or PEO's advice line, or one paid hour with an employment-side lawyer. Confirm what your jurisdiction permits for a written disclosure question and off-list reference checks, whether your sector carries its own mandatory reference or conduct-disclosure rules (common in education, healthcare, and financial services) that might already require or restrict exactly this kind of check, and whether any outside screening vendor you use triggers separate disclosure or authorization requirements. If anything about the tip needs to be said out loud to get that advice, say it verbally, without your colleague's name, to that one person only.
4. Before any offer is finalized (adds roughly 3-5 business days): run the disclosure question, the direct-report reference, and the rehire-eligibility check on this candidate — and start applying the same three steps to every finalist for every people-lead role going forward, so nothing here is visibly built around this one candidate. Anything negative that surfaces goes to the candidate for their response before you weigh it.
5. If you hire: set the criteria for judging this person's people-management during a defined early window (60-90 days) before day one, not after a concern arises. 6. If the record from step 4 comes back clean: hire, and give the original tip no further weight in your own head going forward — the structured check was the response to it, not an open-ended suspicion to carry into the working relationship.

## Risks of this plan

- The whole process may surface nothing new. Many employers cap references at dates and titles, an investigation closed by resignation frequently leaves no recorded finding, and a candidate willing to shade a written answer may find no one positioned to contradict them. If so, you're back to deciding on largely the same uncertainty you started with, just with a defensible process around it rather than real new evidence — plan for that outcome rather than counting on the process to resolve the question.
- You are now primed. Having heard the tip, you (and anyone else briefed on why the process changed) are liable to read neutral or ambiguous answers as confirmation and normal reticence as dodging. Setting the criteria and the disclosure question's exact wording before you hear the candidate's answer is what keeps this from becoming a conviction dressed up as due process.
- Any version of this that's visibly built around one candidate, or any question specific enough to mirror the rumor's details, risks both outing your colleague as the source and being unfair to the candidate in a different way — extra suspicion no other finalist faces. The fix is genuine uniformity: the same questions, the same references required, for every finalist for this kind of role, starting now and continuing after this hire is decided.
- Doing nothing extra, or quietly deprioritizing the candidate without this process, are both worse than they look: the first abandons the one function a hiring gate for a people-leadership role exists to perform, and the second convicts someone of an allegation they never had a chance to answer, on a source you can't defend if it's ever questioned — by the candidate, by your colleague if she's identified anyway, or by your own employer if something goes wrong later and the record shows you knew to look and didn't.
- Cost of being wrong: this plan is largely reversible up to the point of an offer — a clean or muddy result from steps 2-4 still leaves you free to hire, pass, or dig further, and none of the steps require repeating or confirming the original tip to anyone outside one privileged conversation. The point that stops being reversible is the hire itself, which is why the post-hire safeguards in step 5 exist as the backstop for whatever the pre-hire process couldn't catch.

## Strongest counter-position

The case against this plan isn't "ignore the tip" or "reject the candidate" — no line of reasoning applied to this question lands on either pole. It's that the disclosure-and-reference process this recommendation leans on may be diligence theater: a way of feeling like you did something rigorous without generating any fact you couldn't already infer from the glowing references you started with. A candidate willing to shade a signed question will do exactly that; a former employer willing to give a frank reference was probably already reachable through the candidate's own list; and a genuinely mistreated direct report is often the least likely person to risk a candid reference for someone who may still have influence over their career or their next one. If that's right, the practical upshot is to compress steps 2-4 rather than spend most of a week on them, and to put far more of your actual planning into step 5.

This wins if your industry or jurisdiction makes references and disclosure questions close to unenforceable in practice — reference-lockdown policies limited to dates and titles are common at larger employers. Even then, you should still run the process, both because it will work in some fraction of cases and because skipping it is worse for the reasons above — but you should not expect it to change your decision, and it's worth saying that to yourself going in rather than after it comes back empty.

High that the tip cannot be a direct input to the hiring decision itself. This doesn't depend on any fact you don't already have, and it holds whether you weigh it as a fairness question, a legal-exposure question, or a team-safety question.

High that informal, uninvited back-channel outreach to people at the old employer is the wrong tool. This was the one point genuinely contested here, and the specific case for it — that quiet outreach is safer for your colleague than an open question — inverts under scrutiny: a handful of off-list calls into one employer narrows the field of suspects for a leak faster than a question every finalist answers.

Medium on the disclosure-and-reference process actually surfacing something you don't already have. Confirming what your jurisdiction and reference norms actually allow is worth more than any further deliberation on this question — and it's also the first practical step.

Medium on the post-hire safeguards catching a real problem if one exists. New reports rarely raise concerns about a brand-new manager within the first 90 days regardless of how the reporting channel is designed, so this is a real backstop, not a guaranteed one.

Material unknowns: whether your jurisdiction and industry's reference practices will let this process yield anything beyond what you already have (the point above, and the crux of the counter-position); whether the candidate was the subject of the investigation versus a witness or complainant to it, which changes the retaliation calculus (unverified — jurisdiction-dependent) and is worth asking your colleague directly; how small or connected your industry is, which determines how much genuine risk even the uniform, consented steps above carry for your colleague regardless of intent; and your colleague's own reason for telling you this, which doesn't change what confidentiality you owe her but is reasonable background for how much independent weight to put on your own read of the situation.

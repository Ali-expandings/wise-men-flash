## Recommendation

Your leaning is reasonable as a reason to pursue this deal, and not yet as a reason to sign it. Don't answer the offer with a yes or no as it stands. Turn it into a staged purchase: take over the bidding and the books under him for the coming season, have an outside accountant rebuild the real earnings, tie part of the price to the revenue that actually stays after he leaves, and write your walk-away conditions down before you see the final numbers.

## Why

Running the crews and knowing clients by name shows you can operate this business. It does not show the business is worth what you would pay for it. That depends on the owner's side of the work: bid pricing, collecting money and managing cash through the seasons, and whether clients stay when he no longer picks up the phone. You named those gaps yourself. In owner-to-employee buyouts of trades businesses, those are typically where things go wrong; the field work rarely is.

Three things sharpen the stakes. First, once you own it you stop being the crew lead. Someone has to be paid to do that job, and someone has to do his bidding, sales and admin, so the profit that matters is what is left after paying market wages for both, not what he takes home today. His tax returns can flatter the picture if some work is paid in cash, if personal costs run through the company, or if his unpaid long hours are the margin. Second, client loyalty cannot be fully tested before you pay, because it only shows once he is gone, so the price has to carry part of that risk. Part of what you would be paying for is goodwill, and eight years of your own work has helped build it. Third, a bank loan on a deal like this will almost certainly carry your personal guarantee, so a bad stretch becomes a household loss, not just a business one.

Loan rules shape the structure. If the loan is SBA-backed, a seller note that counts toward your required down payment has to stay unpaid for the whole loan term, often around ten years, and it can cover at most half of that down payment (checked in October 2026 summaries of the SBA's current rules; confirm with your lender). Whether such a loan allows an earn-out at all is (unverified — ask the lender before designing the structure). That is why the client test should happen before closing wherever you can arrange it: he introduces you to the top accounts this season, and they renew in your name before you sign.

Your partner's nervousness is useful information. They share the savings and probably the guarantee, so their tolerance should set the size of the bet, not be argued down.

## What to do

1. This week, ask him in writing for three years of tax returns, bank statements, the year-to-date profit and loss, revenue by client, and an equipment list with ages. Hire an accountant who does small-business acquisitions to rebuild the earnings from the returns and bank statements, not from his summary. The fee is small next to the deal. Allow two to four weeks.
2. Meanwhile, bid next season's jobs yourself with him reviewing your numbers, and have the accountant walk you through the cash calendar: when payroll and materials go out in spring versus when clients pay.
3. Sit down with your partner and fix three numbers before you see the final figures: the most you can afford to lose, the minimum cash left after closing (at least about six months of household expenses plus the loan payments), and whether the house can be pledged.
4. Then propose terms on paper: a defined transition in which he stays through at least one full season, introduces you to the top accounts and gets them renewed in your name before closing, and a meaningful share of the price tied to revenue retained at 12 and 24 months (an earn-out, or a holdback if the lender will not allow one). Have a lawyer who handles business sales review anything binding, including exclusivity, before you sign.
5. Set a decision date in writing at the start, about three months out, and do not sign before it. The first walk-away condition that trips ends the deal; do not renegotiate your own conditions after you see the numbers.

Walk away if any of these hold:
- He will not share the three years of returns, the bank statements and revenue by client, or will not let your accountant review them.
- After a market wage for you, a paid replacement crew lead and a paid replacement for his bidding and admin, profit covers the loan payments by less than about 1.25 times. Treat 1.5 times as the comfortable target.
- You take out the 20% of revenue most tied to him, let costs fall only as fast as crew hours do, and the loan payments are no longer covered, and he will not restructure so that he carries that risk.
- He refuses to tie a real share of the price to retained revenue, will not stay through a transition, or his top accounts will not renew in your name.
- The top ten clients are more than about half of revenue and deal only with him, or the contracts cannot be assigned to you.
- Closing would leave you below the cash reserve you and your partner set, with no working-capital line for spring payroll, or the trucks and equipment need heavy replacement that the price ignores.
- Your partner, having seen the same numbers, will not accept the guarantee or the collateral.

## Risks of this plan

Acting this way costs something. He may sell to a competitor or outside buyer while you stage things, or take your requests as distrust. You will do two jobs for a season, and the accountant's fee is gone if the deal dies. Asking for a retention-linked price may also push him to a cleaner, higher-priced exit with someone else. Waiting is a cost too: the longer the terms stay open, the more chance the offer changes.

Rushing costs more. If you sign on today's knowledge, the first sign of trouble is a thin spring and a fixed loan payment with your savings already spent. What would flip this advice toward a plainer, more cash-heavy deal: the records show mostly multi-year recurring contracts, no dominant client, and coverage of 1.5 times or better after the stress test.

Cost of being wrong: the first four steps are cheap and reversible, costing a fee, some awkwardness and a few months. Closing is not reversible: your savings are in the business and the guarantee stays until the loan is repaid or the bank releases you. If the counter-position below turns out to be right, the recovery path is to stop before closing, which costs the fee and some goodwill with him, and then either stay on under the new owner or compete once you have checked any non-compete you signed.

## Strongest counter-position

Position: do not buy the client goodwill at all. "'Do diligence, then decide' assumes diligence can settle whether clients are loyal, and it cannot: loyalty shows itself only after he stops answering the phone, which is after you have paid. An ordinary seller note does not fix that either, because you owe it whether clients stay or leave, and the bank's guarantee runs through you regardless." Your boss knows the real margins, which clients are personal friends, and whether anyone else would pay more, so selling to the employee often means the buyer has the least information, the most loyalty and the strongest fear of losing the job. The goodwill is partly your own eight years sold back to you, and if it is really yours, starting your own outfit may cost little more than equipment.

It wins when he will not tie a large share of the price (30 to 40%) to retained revenue, when the price sits well above the value of the trucks, equipment and assignable contracts, or when the top accounts deal only with him. The evidence would be his refusal, top accounts that will not renew in your name, or a price that the equipment and contracts do not support. If the price is close to asset value, most of this case falls away.

## Confidence

- High: the decision should not be made on what you know today, and refusing to open the books is a reason to walk.
- Medium: a staged deal with a retention-linked price shifts enough of the client-loyalty risk onto him. It depends on whether he agrees and on what the lender allows.
- Medium: the 1.25 and 1.5 coverage figures and the 20% stress test are rules of thumb; your accountant and lender may set them differently.
- Medium: the seller-note rule above; Low: whether an earn-out is allowed, which I have not checked.

Material unknowns: the price, the loan terms and whether it is SBA-backed, the true profit after replacing his work, the client mix and contract terms, any non-compete you signed, whether the house is collateral, and how far your partner will go.

## Recommendation

Treat the study as a weak signal that justifies a medication review, not a reason to act on dementia fears. Do not have her stop the drug on her own; instead, have her prescriber or pharmacist check whether she still has a current reason for it, and test her vitamin B12. If the reason is gone, step down under supervision; if it is real, she stays on it.

## Why

A relative risk of 1.3 from records-based data is the size that bias routinely produces, and a sample of several million buys precision, not validity: it makes the confidence interval tight around a number that may still be biased. The E-value for a relative risk of 1.3 is about 1.9, meaning one unmeasured factor linked about 1.9-fold to both long-term use of the drug and to dementia could explain the whole result. Frailty, many medications, vascular disease and frequent doctor visits can reach that level.

The usual ways such studies go wrong:
- Confounding by indication and frailty: sicker, older people with more prescriptions get the drug and also get dementia.
- Reverse causation: early, undiagnosed dementia changes symptoms, diet and doctor contact before the diagnosis is recorded.
- Detection bias: heavy users see doctors more, so dementia is more likely to be written down.
- Exposure misclassification: records miss over-the-counter use, adherence and dose, and "more than five years" partly measures how long someone has been ill.
- Data dredging: databases test thousands of drug-outcome pairs, so some look linked by chance.

This specific signal has been tested before. Early claims-data and cohort studies linked this drug class to dementia, and later cohort and case-control studies largely failed to replicate it; a randomized trial of about 17,600 people on pantoprazole or placebo for a median of about 3 years found no excess of dementia (unverified — check the published trial report). Records-based relative risks of 1.3 to 1.5 commonly shrink under better designs, though not always.

In absolute terms, if her ten-year dementia risk were about 10% and the effect were fully causal, a 1.3-fold increase would add about 3 percentage points. Discounted for the bias above, the expected harm is smaller, plausibly zero. The question that matters more is different: after a decade, is daily acid suppression still justified? A large share of long-term users have no current documented reason (typical, not universal). And one real, reversible link exists: long-term use lowers vitamin B12 absorption, and B12 deficiency can impair memory, so it is worth testing.

## What to do

1. This week, find out why she started the drug and what else she takes (pharmacy record or notes, about 10 minutes). Check especially for Barrett's oesophagus, severe oesophagitis, a past ulcer bleed, or regular aspirin, blood thinners or anti-inflammatory painkillers. Any of these means she keeps it.
2. Book one medication review with her GP or pharmacist. Frame it as a routine ten-year check, not as the dementia headline: "Is the reason still current, and is this the lowest effective dose?" Ask for a B12 level (and magnesium) in the same visit.
3. If no ongoing reason exists, taper to the lowest dose or on-demand use over several weeks under their guidance. Expect rebound acid in the first one to two weeks; that is not proof she needs the drug. Judge the result at about 4 to 8 weeks.
4. Escalate sooner if she has black stools, vomiting blood, trouble swallowing or unexplained weight loss. Separately, any real memory change in her needs its own assessment whatever the drug.

## Risks of this plan

Waiting costs little here: a few weeks to a review changes the dementia picture negligibly either way. The risk in the other direction is that the review ends with "that study is confounded" and nobody asks whether she still needs the drug, so a decade becomes two. Stopping abruptly risks rebound symptoms and, if she has one of the protective reasons above, bleeding or oesophageal damage. Telling her "30% more dementia" risks a panic stop or distrust of good advice later. What would flip the answer: a sibling-comparison or randomized design with long follow-up showing a consistent dementia excess would make stepping down the default even for those with a mild indication.

Cost of being wrong: a supervised taper is easy to reverse; if symptoms return she goes back to the previous dose within days, so little is lost. If the dementia link is real, the cost of a few more weeks on the drug is small, and the review still reduces future exposure.

## Strongest counter-position

"The study should not be discounted this much. Observational dementia signals from large, adjusted cohorts have sometimes held up, the trial cited covered only about 3 years in people with heart disease, and no trial will ever test use beyond five years, which is exactly her exposure. A decade of acid suppression with a plausible, if unproven, mechanism and a dose-response trend deserves a proactive step-down for everyone without a strong indication, not just a review." It wins if sibling or long-follow-up designs keep a dementia excess after adjustment, or if the trial's dementia result is shown to be underpowered for the question.

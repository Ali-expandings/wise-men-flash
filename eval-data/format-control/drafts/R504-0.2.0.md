## Recommendation

Treat the $15,000 as a best-case, revenue-based model output, not a measured value, and do not raise the acquisition ceiling to $5,000 on it. Replace it with an estimate built from your own cohort billing records, on gross margin, in three labelled parts: what has actually been observed to month 36, a separately labelled tail capped at 3 to 5 years and discounted, and the value of the marginal customer the extra spend would buy. On illustrative placeholders (75% gross margin, 10% annual discount rate), the discounted margin value runs from about $3,500 (first 36 months) to about $6,800 (an endless tail), against $15,000.

## Why

The formula revenue ÷ churn is a legitimate shortcut, but it is only right if four things hold at once: every customer pays $180 for life, 1.2% is a constant exit rate at every tenure and for every future customer, revenue is as good as profit, and a dollar in year seven equals a dollar today. Your own numbers show how much rides on that. At constant 1.2% churn the average customer stays 83 months (6.9 years), 2.3 times the longest tenure you have seen, and about 65% of the $15,000 (roughly $9,700) comes from month 37 onward. Only about $5,300 (35%) falls inside the first 36 months, and even that is the formula's own prediction rather than a measurement. What you have actually observed sits in your billing records.

"Nobody past 36 months" tells you how old the company is, not how long customers stay: if 1.2% held, about 65% of your oldest customers would still be paying at month 36. So the right tool is survival analysis, which treats still-active customers as unfinished rather than churned, not a hard cut-off at month 36.

Per acquired customer, with the formula's constant 1.2% churn (margin and discount rate are placeholders, not your numbers; 75% gross margin is unverified for your business):

| Basis | 36 months | 5 years | Endless |
|---|---|---|---|
| Revenue, undiscounted (the $15,000 basis) | $5,287 | $7,730 | $15,000 |
| 75% margin, undiscounted | $3,965 | $5,798 | $11,250 |
| 75% margin, 10% discount | $3,498 | $4,763 | $6,813 |

Against a $5,000 spend that is LTV:CAC of 3.0 on the endless revenue figure, 2.25 on undiscounted margin, 1.36 on discounted margin, and 0.70 on discounted margin over the observed 36 months. The 3.0 needs revenue-as-profit, an infinite horizon, no discounting and constant churn all at once. Payback tells the same story: after churn, $5,000 takes about 34 months to recover on revenue and about 49 months at 75% margin, and only about 40% of it ($2,023) comes back in revenue in the first year. Recovering $5,000 within the 36 months you have watched would need roughly 95% margin on these numbers.

Two more things make the $15,000 flatter this particular decision. It describes the average customer, while a higher ceiling buys the marginal one, from pricier, lower-intent channels whose churn and revenue are typically worse than the blend. And an allowable-CAC number acts like a price: once $5,000 is allowed, bids, agency fees and commissions tend to drift toward it, including on customers you win today for far less. Growth is typically judged on customers won this year while finance carries the cash for years after, so whoever owns the growth target should not be the only one who builds this number.

## Risks of this plan

Cost of waiting: if the tail is better than the formula assumes (see below), holding the ceiling forgoes customers on channels that would have paid back, and a competitor may win them. Capping and discounting the tail biases the estimate low, so present it as a low/base/high range rather than one haircut. The 75% margin is a placeholder; but even at 100% margin and no discounting, revenue through month 36 ($5,287) only just covers $5,000, so payback rests entirely on the unobserved tail whatever the margin. If the 1.2% is net revenue churn (expansion hiding lost customers) or is flattered by annual-contract customers who can only leave at months 12, 24 and 36, every figure here, the $15,000 included, is too high. If churn rises with tenure (renewal cliffs, customers outgrowing the product), the tail is worse than the formula says. At $5,000, each cohort stays cash-negative on margin for about 49 months at 75% margin, so scaling spend scales burn before the tail is proven.

Cost of being wrong: holding and stepping up is reversible within a quarter, since the ceiling can be raised any month. A $5,000 ceiling is hard to undo: the cash is spent, commissions and agency terms ratchet up, and you would only learn the truth about months 37 and beyond one to three years later. If the counter-position is right, the cost is a few weeks to a quarter of slower growth on channels that pay back, recoverable by raising those channels' ceilings once the cohort curves show it.

## Strongest counter-position

Position: the tail is unobserved, not bad.

"Nobody past 36 months tells you how old the company is, not how long customers stay. About 65% of your oldest cohort should still be paying if 1.2% holds. Subscription survival curves typically flatten with tenure, because early churners leave first and the survivors are stickier (unverified for your base; check the tenure-band churn above), so a constant-churn formula often understates the tail, and expansion revenue is not in it at all. On revenue alone $15,000 could be about right or even low. A capped, discounted, margin-based figure will be biased low, hand growth a fair rebuttal, and turn the argument onto a tail nobody can measure for years, while you under-invest in channels that do pay back."

It wins if the cohort data show monthly churn falling clearly below 1% after month 12, later cohorts retaining as well as earlier ones, and gross margin around 80% or better including expansion. The evidence is the churn by tenure band in months 13 to 36, which your billing records can show in days.

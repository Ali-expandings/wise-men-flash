## Recommendation

The freeze is moving risk into January and concentrating it. It stops deploys but not merging, so two weeks of changes from 60 engineers reach production together as one batch that cannot be tested as a whole, diagnosed quickly or rolled back cleanly. Keep the freeze, which probably does protect December, but change how it ends: release the backlog in small, ordered, separately revertible slices after authors and on-call are back. First spend one engineer one to three days confirming this against your last three Januaries' incident records, because the facts you gave do not rule out calendar-triggered faults, build drift or slow response as contributing causes.

## Why

The freeze stops the part of the process that gives feedback and leaves the part that creates risk running. Deploy, observe and revert is how a team learns a change is bad while it is still small. Merging continues, so changes keep arriving but get no production feedback for two weeks. Each was tested against main, not against production's config, data and flag state, and some were reviewed against a main that kept moving underneath them.

Then the whole batch hits production at once, and three things get worse in a way individual changes would not cause:
- Interactions: the number of pairs of changes that could interact grows with the square of the number of changes, and nobody tested those combinations together.
- Diagnosis and rollback: a bad release of 3 changes bisects in minutes; a bad release of dozens or hundreds has many candidate causes, and rollback is all-or-nothing, so good changes get reverted with the bad one and reshipped as a second risky event.
- Attention: authors merged weeks ago, some are typically on leave, and on-call is typically thin in the first days of January (your own roster will show whether that holds).

This is why every review blames a different bug. Each review finds a real proximate cause; the shared cause is one level up: why did that bug reach production inside a batch too big to attribute? A review that stops at the triggering bug cannot see it, and a large batch produces many unrelated surface bugs. Lower January traffic fits this too. It rules out load as the driver, and Google's SRE book reports that roughly 70% of outages are due to changes in a live system (sre.google/sre-book/introduction).

Is the freeze protecting you? Probably in December, when traffic is higher and staffing thinner, but you have not said what December looks like. Moving risk from a high-traffic month to a low-traffic one can be a good trade even if January incidents rise, so judge the freeze by what outages cost (revenue, users affected), not by January's count. What you have is the moved risk plus a concentration that makes it harder to attribute and slower to fix.

The batch is the leading explanation, not a proven one. The facts given cannot separate it from three others that also recur on the calendar: year-boundary faults (January 1 partition rollovers, annual certificate, token or licence expiry, annual jobs, "current year" assumptions); drift in the first post-freeze build (unpinned base images and transitive dependencies, plus infrastructure work, migrations and patches held back until after the freeze); and slower response (thin on-call, lost context) lengthening outages without adding faults. Lower traffic rules out only load.

## What to do

1. Now, one engineer, one to three days, finished before the freeze starts. For every incident in the last three Januaries record: the merge date of the change that caused it, its first production deploy date, the trigger type (code, date or expiry, dependency or infrastructure, other), time to restore against that year's median, and the size of the first post-freeze deploy against a normal week. Also check whether incidents begin at the first post-freeze release and taper in the days after it, and whether the cluster moved in any year the freeze lift date moved. Decision test: if a clear majority are freeze-window merges first shipped in the post-freeze release and cluster in the days after it, the batch diagnosis stands. If most predate the freeze or are date-, dependency- or response-driven, leave the freeze policy alone and fix those instead.
2. Permanently, starting now: add merge date, deploy date, trigger type and deploy size as required fields in every incident review, and have someone read a whole year's reviews together each quarter, so a pattern like this shows up in one year, not three.
3. Whatever the test shows, before the freeze: run a year-boundary check in early December (expiries, partitions, annual jobs, date logic); pin dependencies and base images through the freeze and the first January builds; and audit what the freeze actually covers, since config, infrastructure changes, flag flips and migrations may still be changing or piling up.
4. If the test supports the batch diagnosis, decide these changes at least a month before the freeze starts:
 - Do not let the pile sit untested. Either hold non-urgent merges in a queue and land them in ordered slices at the thaw, or keep merging but deploy main continuously to a production-like environment with production config and replayed or synthetic traffic. Moving the pile from main into long-lived branches only relabels it and stores up merge conflicts.
 - Thaw in slices: lowest risk first, each independently revertible, each with a soak period, sized like a normal week's deploy, starting only when authors and on-call are back. Ship database schema changes as their own slices with a rollback plan, since stacked migrations can make rollback impossible. Ship held-back infrastructure work and patches as their own slices, not alongside code.
 - Because January traffic is low, canary releases have few requests to judge by: alert on absolute error counts or add synthetic traffic.
5. Stop or escalate: after the first season with a staged thaw, compare early-January incident count and time to restore with the previous three years. If the cluster is unchanged with small slices, the batch was not the main cause; move to year-boundary, drift and staffing causes and stop reworking the freeze.

## Risks of this plan

- Loosening the freeze would move change risk into December's peak traffic and thin staffing, and if calendar-triggered faults dominate it would leave January untouched, so both months could get worse. This plan adds no December deploys; its December changes (queued merges or a production-like environment, pinning, an audit) add checks, not releases.
- Queuing merges and thawing in slices delays freeze-period work reaching customers by days and can add merge conflicts. A staged thaw also puts more release events in the week people return.
- Incident records may be too thin to link causes to merge dates, so the check could be inconclusive. If so, run one staged thaw anyway with the new fields recorded: it is cheap and reversible.
- Waiting has a cost: three Januaries in a row suggest a fourth is likely if nothing changes, and the records check costs only days.
- Cost of being wrong: every step here can be dropped next year at the price of a few days' delay on freeze-period work. The one hard-to-undo outcome is a customer-facing December outage, and the plan adds no December deploys. If the counter-position below is right, you lose days of delay and some process effort, and the same records point you to the date, dependency or staffing fixes.

## Strongest counter-position

The batch is not the main cause, and the freeze is not what is hurting you. The popular answer, that two weeks of merged code ships at once, is the one base rates favour, but none of the stated facts prove it. A recurring window on the calendar is just as well explained by faults that fire whether or not there is a freeze: year-boundary expiries and annual jobs, drift in the first post-freeze build, and a thinned-out, out-of-context on-call that lengthens outages without adding faults. A big batch predicts "a different bug each time", and so does every one of those. If these dominate, restructuring the freeze buys nothing in January and adds change risk in December's peak traffic and thin staffing, so both months get worse. This wins if most blamed changes were merged before the freeze or were already running in December, if the bugs are date-, expiry- or dependency-triggered, if incidents do not start at the post-freeze release and taper off, or if January time to restore rather than incident count is what is unusual. The records check in step 1 is the evidence that would show it.

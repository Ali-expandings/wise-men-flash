## Recommendation

Neither a bare technical fix nor a personal apology. Send a plain, owned correction: the corrected numbers first, one sentence of first-person ownership ("the error was mine"), and a short factual statement of where the corrected figures sit against the criteria the original analysis used, with no verdict on whether the closure was right. Have a second person check the fix, and show the draft to your manager or the decision's sponsor before it goes to the group.

## Why

Tone should scale with what the readers need to do with the message, not with how you feel about it. Executives reading a correction typically ask one thing in the first seconds: does this change the decision? A message that does not answer it lets them answer it themselves, usually in whichever direction is more convenient. So a factual line on where the corrected figures stand against the original criteria is part of the correction, not commentary on the decision. A verdict is different. "It would have stayed open" or "it wouldn't have changed anything" claims knowledge of lease, strategy and headcount factors you do not hold, and where people were laid off it becomes a quotable sentence about the rationale.

The two options in the question fail for specific reasons. A passive technical fix ("a discrepancy was identified") on a 40% error in a decision that cost people their jobs reads as evasion as soon as anyone does the dollar arithmetic. A personal apology makes the executives manage your feelings, implies the closure was your decision when they made it with inputs beyond your spreadsheet, and creates an admission-style record. One sentence of ownership is enough.

Give dollar figures, not "about 40%". The phrase is ambiguous. If the reported loss was 40% above the true loss, the true loss is about 71% of what was reported (29% lower). If the reported figure was 40% too high measured against itself, the true loss is 60% of what was reported (40% lower). Executives will work out which, and a vague number reads as minimizing.

Sequence matters as much as wording. A fix that has not been independently checked and then needs a second correction does more damage than the first error. A sponsor who learns of this from a group email stops being an ally. Delay compounds: three months have already passed, and each extra day makes "when did you know?" harder to answer. Corrections an author volunteers typically cost less than ones someone else discovers (a general pattern, unverified here). Because staff were laid off, HR or legal may want to review it; whether and how depends on your jurisdiction and your company's process (unverified — check with HR or legal).

The usual ways these go wrong: passive or minimizing wording; grovelling that turns a group decision into your personal fault; a counterfactual verdict in either direction; silence on whether the numbers change anything; emailing the group before the sponsor has heard it; an unchecked fix that forces a second correction; not checking whether the same formula sits in other models, so one error looks like a pattern; and hinting at reinstatement or compensation in writing.

## What to do

The message, as a starting draft (replace the brackets with exact figures):

> Subject: Correction to the [Office] closure analysis of [date]
>
> I found an error in the analysis I wrote on [date] for the [Office] closure decision. The error was mine: a formula in [the loss calculation] [one-line cause].
>
> Corrected: [Office]'s operating loss for [period] is $[X], not the $[Y] in the original. [Any other figures that changed.]
>
> Against the criteria the original analysis used: [Office] [ranks n of N on operating margin / sits $A above or below the $B threshold]; the original showed [rank or gap]. I have not assessed non-financial factors or whether the decision stands; that is yours to judge.
>
> [Name] independently checked the corrected figures. I also checked [the other analyses or models that use the same formula] and found [none / these]. I have added [check] so this cannot recur. The corrected file is attached and filed beside the original. I found the error on [date]. I am raising it now so you can decide whether anything else needs to review it, including HR or legal. I can walk you through it whenever suits.

Preparation, in order:

1. Spend one to two days having a colleague independently re-check the corrected figures, re-run the original decision criteria with them, and scan your other analyses and models for the same formula.
2. Tell your manager or the decision's sponsor in person or by call, with the draft in hand, the same day the check finishes or earlier. Raise anything about the affected staff (recall, severance, references) in that private conversation, not in the message. Expect HR or legal to be brought in; ask that their review does not stop the correction going out.
3. Send the written correction to everyone who received the original, within days of step 2, and file the corrected analysis with the original in the decision record.
4. Scale the criteria line to the result. If the office is still clearly worst on every original criterion, one line saying the ranking is unchanged is enough. If the corrected figures move it across the threshold or near breakeven, do not send it as a memo: take it to your manager as an escalation with HR and legal involved, and send the written version afterwards.
5. If the check turns up a second error, stop, fix it and restart the check before anything is sent.

## Risks of this plan

Spending one to two days on the check and the sponsor conversation delays a disclosure that is already three months late; if a recipient hears of the error elsewhere in that window, the delay becomes the story. The criteria line can still be read as a verdict, and if the corrected figures flip the office across the threshold it gives anyone contesting the layoffs a document to cite. If the original analysis had no numeric closure criterion, the line has nothing to test against. Routing through HR or legal can slow or soften the message, so the correction needs a date by which it goes out regardless.

Cost of being wrong: the closure and the layoffs cannot be undone, and a sent message cannot be recalled, but this plan commits to the least you can later add to, not the most. You can shorten, add detail or narrow a claim in a follow-up; you cannot unsay an apology or a verdict, which is why the draft contains neither. If the counter-position below is right, the recovery is cheap: because the sponsor sees the draft first, drop the criteria line and send only the corrected figures with the ownership sentence, and let the sponsor carry the conversation about the decision.

## Strongest counter-position

The criteria line is the trap. In a case with layoffs, there is no stable line between "here is where the corrected figures sit against the criteria" and "here is whether the closure stands": every reader will take the first as the second, and whatever the number shows, it will be quoted back in any dispute over the layoffs. The analyst's job is to deliver correct figures and own the error; whether those figures change the decision is the executives' judgment, and the framing of it is for the sponsor, HR and legal. If the closure was really a strategic decision that the analysis was used to support after the fact, there may be no numeric criterion at all, and a criteria line is an invented test. On this view the plain corrected-figures message with an ownership sentence wins outright. It wins when the original memo shows no numeric closure threshold, or when HR or legal advise that no written commentary on decision impact should come from the analyst. The evidence is the original decision record and that advice.

## Confidence

High that one sentence of first-person ownership beats both a passive technical fix and a personal apology; that follows from how each fails, not from any consensus. Medium that a factual criteria line belongs in the message: it depends on whether a numeric closure criterion existed and on whether the error is specific to this office or affected every office in the comparison. Medium on the sequence and time boxes, which depend on your company's HR and legal process. Material unknowns: whether the corrected figures cross the closure threshold, whether other models share the formula, the legal position, and which of the two readings of "40%" applies.

## Recommendation

Do not reject the candidate on the tip, and do not ignore it. Treat it as a lead: keep the source confidential, and before any offer put every finalist through the same two checks, a written question about past complaints or investigations and a reference from a former direct report. Decide on what those checks produce.

## Why

You owe the candidate a decision based on facts they can see and answer. "Left mid-investigation" fits a guilty person, an exonerated one, or someone who left because the process was unfair, and you cannot tell which. Rejecting on it alone punishes them on hearsay with no chance to respond, and a quiet rejection on a secret rumor is the kind of record that later looks like a pretext if the decision is challenged.

You owe the future reports real diligence, not a shrug. A team lead's treatment of junior staff lands on people who cannot choose their boss. If you hear a warning and do nothing, "knew or should have known" is the standard negligent-hiring fact pattern, though its details vary by jurisdiction. The glowing references weigh less than they seem: the candidate picked the referees, and an exit during an open investigation is often accompanied by agreed references. Typically, an investigation that is still open when someone resigns tends to close without a finding (unverified — check with HR or counsel for your jurisdiction), so waiting for the investigation's outcome to become available is unlikely to work.

You owe your colleague discretion, not a promise to do nothing. She asked you not to repeat it. That binds you not to name her or relay the claim, and it does not require you to behave as if you never heard it. Repeating "I heard..." to a panel or to the candidate's old workplace also exposes you to defamation risk and exposes her.

What you can realistically do with an unverifiable tip is turn it into something verifiable that does not depend on it. Asking all finalists the same question protects both the candidate's fairness and her anonymity, because a question aimed at one person points straight back to her.

## What to do

1. This week, before any offer: tell your colleague you will not name her or repeat what she said and that you will run your normal checks. Ask, without pressing, whether her knowledge is first-hand and whether she can point to someone who saw it directly. Do not promise she can never be involved if this later becomes a formal matter.
2. Ask HR or counsel, as a need-to-know, to add to the process for all finalists: a written question ("Have you been the subject of a workplace complaint or investigation, or left a role while one was open? If so, what happened?") and one reference from a former direct report. Do not name the source and do not write the rumor into the candidate file. Check with them what you may ask and record in your jurisdiction.
3. In the interview, ask structured behavioral questions about managing underperformers, giving feedback and handling conflict with junior staff. Ask the candidate openly why they left.
4. Ask the previous employer only what it will confirm, such as rehire eligibility.
5. Make any offer conditional on truthful answers.
6. Time box: about one week. Decide at the end of it. Interpret the results this way: a plain, coherent disclosure plus a direct report describing fair treatment means proceed. A false denial, evasiveness about the departure, or a credible first-hand account of mistreatment means stop and take it to HR or counsel before going further. If the candidate cannot or will not name a former direct report, that is a weak signal, not proof. If the process leaves a real, job-relevant doubt and a runner-up is close, choosing the runner-up on those process findings is legitimate; record the reasons from the process only.

## Risks of this plan

Doing the checks can still miss the problem: a candidate can deny falsely, the previous employer can say nothing, and no direct report may speak, in which case you are left with the tip or nothing. The checks can also become a ritual that clears the candidate by default. Adding a week risks losing the candidate to another offer. Asking only this candidate would breed confirmation bias and could expose your colleague; asking everyone prevents that. Hiring a candidate who was in fact abusive harms junior staff first, and you would be exposed for having known.

Cost of being wrong: nothing is committed until the offer, so the process itself is reversible. If you rejected a good candidate, you can reopen the search or revisit them. If you hire an abusive lead, the harm to reports is hard to undo; the recovery path is conditional offer terms, a probationary period if your jurisdiction allows one, and an early check-in with the reports. A false denial discovered later is a clean, citable reason to act.

## Strongest counter-position

"The premise that unverifiable means weak is wrong here. When someone resigns mid-investigation the investigation usually closes with no finding, so the verification you are waiting for most likely never exists. The glowing references are curated by the candidate and may be negotiated, so you are comparing a disinterested signal, a colleague who takes a social risk and gains nothing, against a curated one. A courtroom standard of proof does not fit a hiring decision; you already hire on weaker inputs such as gut feel and interview polish. The comfortable conclusion, that you cannot use what you cannot cite, also happens to keep your top candidate and save you time, while the cost of being wrong falls on junior staff who were not in the room." This position wins if your colleague's knowledge is first-hand and she has no stake against the candidate, and the checks come back empty: then the tip should count as a real reason to prefer a close runner-up. Evidence that would show it: a second independent source, or a disclosure answer that conflicts with what she told you.

## Confidence

Medium on the plan overall: it holds across common employment regimes, but what you may ask and record depends on your jurisdiction. High that rejecting on the tip alone is the wrong move. Low-to-medium on whether the checks will actually surface the truth. Material unknowns: whether she has first-hand knowledge or a grudge, the investigation's outcome, your jurisdiction and sector, and the candidate's reason for leaving.

## Recommendation

Your leaning is reasonable, but "reasonable to lean yes" and "ready to sign" are different things. You have the one advantage an outside buyer never gets: you already run operations and already have the crews' trust. Most acquisitions die on operations or culture, and you have already solved that part.

What you haven't solved is the books, the bidding, the cash flow, and whether clients are loyal to a brand or to a man. That is exactly where deals like this actually fail. None of it disqualifies you. All of it needs answers with numbers, not gut feel, before money moves.

The bottom line is conditional. Do the homework anyway, and structure the deal so your boss shares the risk with you. If the numbers hold up and your boss is willing to share the transition risk with you, this is a better bet than almost any outside buyer would get. If he wants full price, all cash, no transition, and no visibility into the books, that is your answer already.

The walk-away conditions are these: financials he can't or won't produce clean; debt service coverage that only works in a best-case year; top clients loyal to him personally with no willingness from him to share that risk; key crew leads who would leave if he leaves; equipment near end of life with no price adjustment; no cash reserve outside the business; and a specific, unresolved worry behind your partner's nervousness.

## Why

Client loyalty is the one variable that decides everything else, so stop guessing and get data. Recurring-contract clients rarely bolt over an ownership change if service holds. Handshake relationships with the owner are the risk. You will learn more from ten real conversations than from any spreadsheet.

Books, bidding, and cash flow are fixable, not dealbreakers, but fix them before close. Landscaping runs thin and seasonal, so someone needs to watch cash weekly. Underpricing jobs is the quietest way new owners bleed out.

The money mechanics matter more than the sticker price. The test is the debt service coverage ratio once you pay yourself a real market wage, not owner-take-everything cash flow. If that is not comfortably above the loan payment, at 1.25-1.5x with room to spare, the price is too high or the loan too big, full stop.

"Most of our savings plus a loan" also means zero cushion. This business is seasonal: equipment breaks, and you will lose a client or two in year one no matter what you do. You need a reserve sitting outside the deal, not inside it.

The deal should be structured to share the risk instead of absorbing it all on day one. If the loyalty turns out to be personal, your boss eats part of that risk with you. A non-compete means he is not down the road undercutting you with the relationships he kept.

Eight years in makes it tempting to treat "I basically know this business" as a substitute for the diligence an outside buyer would be forced to do. It isn't. Do the homework anyway.

## What to do

On client loyalty, get data:
- Pull the client list and rank it by revenue.
- Look at the top 10-15 accounts: how long they have been customers, whether they deal with your boss personally or with the crews, and whether they are on recurring seasonal contracts or one-off jobs.
- If he will let you sit in on renewal conversations for a few months before close, do it.

On books, bidding, and cash flow, fix them before close:
- Line up a bookkeeper or fractional CFO before you sign.
- Shadow your boss on bidding for a full season if you can.
- Get three years of financials and tax returns in front of a CPA who has actually done acquisition reviews, not your regular tax preparer.
- Have that CPA calculate seller's discretionary earnings, not the number your boss quotes you.

On the money mechanics:
- Work out the debt service coverage ratio after you pay yourself a real market wage.
- Keep it comfortably above the loan payment, at 1.25-1.5x with room to spare, or accept that the price is too high or the loan too big.
- Keep a reserve outside the deal.

On structure, share the risk:
- Ask for a transition period, with your boss part-time for 6-12 months, personally introducing you to every major account and reachable for questions.
- Ask for seller financing or an earnout tied to client retention, so that if the loyalty turns out to be personal, he eats part of that risk with you.
- Ask for a non-compete, so he is not down the road undercutting you with the relationships he kept.

On your partner, ask directly what worries them, not whether they are okay with it.

Finally, do the homework anyway rather than treating eight years in as a substitute for it.

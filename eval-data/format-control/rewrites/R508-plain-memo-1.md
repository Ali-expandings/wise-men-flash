## What to do

On client loyalty, get data:
- Pull the client list and rank it by revenue.
- Look at the top 10-15 accounts.
- For each, find out how long they have been customers, whether they deal with your boss personally or with the crews, and whether they are on recurring seasonal contracts or one-off jobs.
- If he will let you sit in on renewal conversations for a few months before close, do it.

On books, bidding and cash flow, fix them before close:
- Line up a bookkeeper or fractional CFO before you sign, because someone needs to watch cash weekly in a thin, seasonal business.
- Shadow your boss on bidding for a full season if you can. Underpricing jobs is the quietest way new owners bleed out.
- Put three years of financials and tax returns in front of a CPA who has actually done acquisition reviews, not your regular tax preparer.
- Have that CPA calculate seller's discretionary earnings, not the number your boss quotes you.

On the money mechanics:
- Work out the debt service coverage ratio after you pay yourself a real market wage, not owner-take-everything cash flow.
- It should be comfortably above the loan payment, at 1.25-1.5x with room to spare. If it is not, the price is too high or the loan too big.
- Keep a reserve sitting outside the deal, not inside it.

On structure, share the risk instead of absorbing it all on day one:
- Ask for a transition period. Your boss would stay part-time for 6-12 months, personally introduce you to every major account, and be reachable for questions.
- Ask for seller financing or an earnout tied to client retention. If the loyalty turns out to be personal, he takes part of that risk with you.
- Ask for a non-compete.

On your partner, ask directly what worries them, not whether they are okay with it.

Do the homework anyway. Your eight years in the business do not replace the diligence an outside buyer would be forced to do.

## Recommendation
Treat the factual correction, the acknowledgment of fault, and the claim about what would have happened as three separate things, because collapsing them is exactly how these letters go wrong. They don't deserve equal airtime. The right tone is factual and briefly owned, not clinical and not penitential. Lead with the correction and the corrected numbers, add one sentence of ownership, say what you are checking and fixing going forward, and then open the message for questions. Do not assert the counterfactual. Keep speculation out of the written record. Loop in any legal or HR function before the message goes to the full executive team. Give a short verbal heads-up to your manager or the decision-owner first.

## Why
Executives need the correction and will act on it, so it goes in the first paragraph with no hedging: what the formula error was, why it happened, and what the corrected loss figure is. Over-apologizing doesn't read as more accountable. It shifts the message's center of gravity from the numbers to your feelings, and it quietly puts the burden on the reader to reassure you. A memo that is mostly apology also buries the one thing they urgently need, which is the corrected number.

"I made a formula error" and "the office shouldn't have closed" are different claims, and you probably can't back the second one. The original decision likely weighed things beyond this one figure, such as lease terms, strategic footprint and other offices under review, that you may not have full visibility into. A corrected loss number alone doesn't tell you what the call would have been.

This is also exposure, not just tone. The closure meant real layoffs. A written statement that the decision rested on bad numbers, paired with speculation that the outcome would have differed, is the kind of sentence that can resurface later if anyone affected ever disputes their termination.

## What to do
- Put the correction and the corrected numbers first: the formula error, why it happened, and the corrected loss figure, stated plainly.
- Add one direct sentence of ownership: you found the error, it was yours, and you are correcting it. Don't extend it into three paragraphs of remorse.
- State what you are checking and fixing going forward, for example whether the same formula is used in other models, and what changes in your review process.
- State the correct numbers clearly and let the executives draw their own conclusion.
- If someone asks directly whether it would have changed the outcome, "I don't know — that's not just my call to make" is honest, not evasive.
- Then open the message for questions.
- Get the facts exactly right and say them clearly, but keep speculation out of the written record.
- If your company has any legal/HR function, loop them in before this goes to the full executive team, given people's jobs were on the line.
- Consider a short verbal heads-up to your manager or the decision-owner before the written version goes out. For something this consequential, the first they hear of it shouldn't be a cold email.

## Risks of this plan
These are the ways these messages usually go wrong:
- Burying the correction under apology, so executives have to dig for the actual number.
- Defensive minimizing, such as "small error" or "probably wouldn't have mattered anyway." It reads as covering yourself, especially given the stakes, and erodes trust in your next analysis more than the error itself does.
- Asserting the counterfactual as fact, claiming the decision would or wouldn't have changed when you don't actually know that.
- No remediation. Stating what broke without saying what you're checking elsewhere or what changes in your review process only documents a mistake instead of closing the loop.
- Wrong sequencing. If the exec team finds out cold, your manager is blindsided alongside them.

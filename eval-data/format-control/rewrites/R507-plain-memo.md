## Recommendation

Let the tip change your process, not your verdict. Three duties pull in different directions here. You owe something to the candidate, something to the people who would report to them, and something to your former colleague. The way through is to use what you were told to raise your diligence, not to reach a conclusion.

The bottom line is that one secondhand, unresolved, unattributable claim isn't enough to reject someone. It is enough to justify real time finding out more before you decide. For a role with authority over junior people, you would want to do most of that digging anyway.

In practice, this means several things:
- Go beyond the reference list the candidate gave you.
- Build your interview questions around how the candidate managed junior staff.
- Don't put the original claim to the candidate directly.

- If expanded diligence turns up nothing more, decide on what the process actually produced, meaning interviews and checks you can stand behind, and not on the original tip.

Treat the tip as a reason to look harder and sooner. Do not treat it as a finding. The decision should rest on evidence the candidate had a chance to address, and on the checks you can stand behind.

## Why

**The candidate.** They are owed a decision based on evidence they had a chance to address, not on an anonymous, unresolved allegation. "Left during an investigation" is consistent with being pushed out, resigning under pressure, resigning for unrelated reasons while it was still open, or being cleared entirely. Your colleague doesn't know which, and neither do you. Treating an unresolved investigation as a proxy for guilt isn't fair to the candidate. If this decision is ever questioned, "I heard a rumor" is not a basis you want in a rejection file.

**The glowing references.** They don't cancel this concern out, but not for the reason it feels like they should. References are chosen by the candidate precisely because they will be glowing, so they were never strong evidence against this kind of concern in the first place. Their being positive doesn't corroborate anything. It just reflects normal selection bias.

**The people who would report to them.** They have the most at stake and the least voice in this process. They can't vet their future manager, so you are doing it on their behalf. The asymmetry is real. A wrong "no hire" costs one strong candidate a job they can get elsewhere. A wrong "hire" puts several junior people under someone with a live pattern, repeatedly, backed by the authority of the role. That doesn't mean you convict on hearsay. It means how hard you dig before deciding should scale with how much power the role holds over vulnerable people. That holds independent of this tip, but the tip is a reason to prioritize the digging now.

**Your former colleague.** She is owed protection as a source. She shared something that could cost her if it traces back to her, and she did so on the express condition that it goes no further. For that reason, don't repeat the specific claim to the candidate, to HR, or to other interviewers in any form attributable to her. That covers her name, and it covers a line such as "someone who worked with them told me."

**What the tip can be used for.** What you can actually do with something you can't verify or cite is use it to raise your diligence, not to reach a conclusion.

## What to do

Use the tip to raise your diligence, not to reach a conclusion. Concretely:

1. **Go beyond the reference list the candidate gave you.** Find former peers or reports through LinkedIn or mutual connections. Ask them open, behavioral questions. Ask how the candidate handled conflict with a junior report. Ask what turnover looked like on their team. Ask whether people would work for them again.

2. **Build interview questions around management of junior staff specifically.** Ask for concrete past examples, such as a hard feedback conversation, a mistake by a report, or a conflict they had to resolve. Avoid abstract leadership philosophy. Specific, unrehearsed detail is harder to fake than a values statement.

3. **Don't put the original claim to the candidate directly.** It is unverifiable and unattributable. Confronting them with it either breaks your colleague's confidence or forces you to lie about your source.

4. **Protect your colleague.** Don't repeat the specific claim to the candidate, HR, or other interviewers in any form attributable to her. This means no name and no "someone who worked with them told me." If the matter surfaces at all in how you explain your process, keep it generic. For example, you can say "this is a people-manager role, I want deeper reference checks than usual." That is true on its own terms and untraceable to that one conversation.

5. **If expanded diligence turns up nothing more, decide on what the process actually produced.** That means interviews and checks you can stand behind, not the original tip. At that point you have done what is reasonable. You don't get to hold a candidate hostage to a rumor indefinitely.

6. **If you do hire them, hedge the risk without pre-punishing them for it.** Schedule earlier-than-usual check-ins with their new reports. Give those reports an accessible way to raise concerns. Hold skip-levels sooner than standard. This is good practice for any new manager, and it happens to cover this specific risk too.

Throughout, scale the depth of your checking to how much power the role holds over vulnerable people. That standard applies independent of this tip, but the tip is a reason to prioritize it now.

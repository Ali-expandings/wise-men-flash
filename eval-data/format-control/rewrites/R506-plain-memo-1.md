## Why

The tone question has an answer once the three pieces are pulled apart. The executives need the correction, because that is what they actually need and will act on. A memo that spends its length on feeling rather than figures fails them on the one point they care about.

Over-apologizing does not read as more accountable. It shifts the message's center of gravity from the numbers to your feelings. It also quietly puts the burden on the reader to reassure you. A memo that is mostly apology buries the one thing the executives urgently need, which is the corrected number. That is why one direct sentence of ownership is enough, and why a longer expression of remorse works against you.

The counterfactual deserves the most care because two claims are easily confused. "I made a formula error" and "the office shouldn't have closed" are different claims, and the second one you probably can't back. The original decision likely weighed things beyond this one figure. Examples are lease terms, strategic footprint, and other offices under review. You may not have full visibility into those factors. A corrected loss number alone doesn't tell you what the call would have been. Stating the correct numbers clearly and letting the executives draw their own conclusion keeps you inside what you actually know.

If someone asks you directly whether the correction would have changed the outcome, "I don't know — that's not just my call to make" is honest, not evasive. It reports the limit of your position accurately rather than guessing past it.

The exposure point rests on the human stakes. The closure meant real layoffs. A written statement asserting the decision rested on bad numbers, paired with speculation that the outcome would have differed, is the kind of sentence that can resurface later if anyone affected ever disputes their termination. This is not a reason to soften the facts. It is a reason to get the facts exactly right and say them clearly, while keeping speculation out of the written record. Given that people's jobs were on the line, it is also the reason to involve any legal or HR function before the message reaches the full executive team.

The sequencing advice follows from the same weight. For something this consequential, the first the executives hear of it shouldn't be a cold email, and your manager should not be blindsided alongside them.

## What to do

First, separate the three pieces in your own head before drafting: the factual correction, the acknowledgment of fault, and the claim about what would have happened. Give them unequal space in the message, with the correction getting the most.

Second, before the written version goes out, consider a short verbal heads-up to your manager or the decision-owner. Whoever owns that relationship should get a heads-up first. The aim is that neither the executive team nor your manager finds out cold.

Third, if your company has any legal or HR function, loop them in before this goes to the full executive team. People's jobs were on the line, which is why this step comes before the send.

Fourth, write the message in this order:

1. **The correction and the corrected numbers, first.** In the first paragraph, plainly and with no hedging, say what the formula error was, why it happened, and what the corrected loss figure is.
2. **One sentence of ownership.** You found the error, it was yours, and you are correcting it. Do not extend it into three paragraphs of remorse.
3. **What you are checking and fixing going forward.** Say what you are checking elsewhere, for example whether the same formula was used in other models. Say what changes in your review process. Without this, the memo just documents a mistake instead of closing the loop.
4. **An opening for questions.** Close by inviting questions.

Fifth, keep the numbers clear and the speculation out. State the correct numbers clearly and let the executives draw their own conclusion. Do not claim in writing that the decision would or wouldn't have changed.

Sixth, prepare one answer for the direct question. If someone asks whether it would have changed the outcome, the honest reply is "I don't know — that's not just my call to make."

Seventh, check your draft against the failure modes. Make sure the correction is not buried under apology. Make sure you have not minimized the error with phrases like "small error" or "probably wouldn't have mattered anyway". Make sure you have not asserted the counterfactual as fact. Make sure the remediation is present. Make sure the sequencing gave the right person a heads-up first.

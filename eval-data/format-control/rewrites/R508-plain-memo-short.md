## Recommendation
Your leaning is reasonable, but "reasonable to lean yes" and "ready to sign" are different things. You have the one advantage an outside buyer never gets: you already run operations and already have the crews' trust. Most acquisitions die on operations or culture, and you've solved that part already. What you haven't solved is books, bidding, cash flow, and whether clients are loyal to a brand or to a man. That is exactly where deals like this actually fail. None of it disqualifies you. All of it needs answers with numbers, not gut feel, before money moves. If the numbers hold up and your boss is willing to share the transition risk with you, this is a better bet than almost any outside buyer would get. If he wants full price, all cash, no transition, no visibility into the books, that's your answer already.

## Why
Client loyalty is the one variable that decides everything else, so stop guessing and get data. Recurring-contract clients rarely bolt over an ownership change if service holds. Handshake relationships with the owner are the risk.

Books, bidding, and cash flow are fixable, not dealbreakers, but fix them before close. Landscaping runs thin and seasonal, so someone needs to watch cash weekly. Underpricing jobs is the quietest way new owners bleed out.

The money mechanics matter more than the sticker price. The question is the debt service coverage ratio once you pay yourself a real market wage, not owner-take-everything cash flow. If that's not comfortably above the loan payment, the price is too high or the loan too big, full stop. "Most of our savings plus a loan" also means zero cushion, and this business is seasonal: equipment breaks, and you'll lose a client or two in year one no matter what you do. A deal structured to share the risk beats absorbing it all on day one.

## What to do
- Pull the client list and rank it by revenue. Look at the top 10-15 accounts: how long they've been customers, whether they deal with your boss personally or with the crews, and whether they're on recurring seasonal contracts or one-off jobs.
- If he'll let you sit in on renewal conversations for a few months before close, do it. You'll learn more from ten real conversations than from any spreadsheet.
- Line up a bookkeeper or fractional CFO before you sign.
- Shadow your boss on bidding for a full season if you can.
- Get three years of financials and tax returns in front of a CPA who's actually done acquisition reviews, not your regular tax preparer. Have them calculate seller's discretionary earnings, not the number your boss quotes you.
- Keep debt service coverage at 1.25-1.5x with room to spare, and keep a reserve sitting outside the deal, not inside it.
- Ask for a transition period: your boss part-time for 6-12 months, personally introducing you to every major account and reachable for questions.
- Ask for seller financing or an earnout tied to client retention, so if the loyalty turns out to be personal, he eats part of that risk with you.
- Ask for a non-compete, so he's not down the road undercutting you with the relationships he kept.
- Ask your partner directly what worries them, not whether they're okay with it.

## Risks of this plan
These are the things that would make the source say walk away:

- No cash reserve left outside the business, leaving you one bad season from insolvency on both the loan and your household savings.
- Your partner's nervousness turning out to be about something specific and unresolved, not general jitters about a big decision.

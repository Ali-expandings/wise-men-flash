Take this seriously enough to raise with her doctor, not seriously enough to act on alone. That is the bottom line, and the later sections give the reasons and the steps behind it.

The right-sized response is neither "the study is scary, she needs to stop" nor "ignore it." It is a routine medication review at her next visit, which would be good practice anyway. She should not stop on her own.

## Why

**The drug may not be the one studied.** "Common acid-reflux medication" usually means a proton pump inhibitor (PPI), such as omeprazole, esomeprazole, pantoprazole or lansoprazole. An H2 blocker like famotidine is a different drug class, and its dementia data is thinner and different. If she is on an H2 blocker, this specific research may not apply to her. That is why confirming what she takes comes first.

**The finding recurs.** On PPIs and dementia, this is not one study. It is a small research area going back to around 2015, and the relative-risk increases across studies range roughly from 20 to 45%. Your figure of 30% after five-plus years fits that pattern. It is possibly a large recent US claims-based cohort of several million patients, with risk concentrated in the longest-duration users, which tracks with your mother's decade of use. The finding keeps recurring, which is worth noting. Whether it means the drug causes dementia is a separate question, and that is where this kind of study is structurally weak.

**What records-based studies can and cannot do.** Studies built on insurance claims or electronic health records, which are not trials, are good at spotting patterns across millions of people cheaply. They are bad at telling you why the pattern exists, because nobody was randomized. People who end up on a drug for ten years differ from people who don't, in ways that matter. The usual ways these studies go wrong are as follows.

- **Confounding by indication.** Decade-long PPI users tend to have more severe GERD and more comorbidities generally. Anything correlated with worse baseline health can also correlate with dementia risk. Researchers adjust for what they can measure, not for everything that is actually different between the groups.
- **Reverse causation.** Dementia has a prodromal phase lasting years before diagnosis. Early, undiagnosed cognitive decline can change diet, self-care and GI symptoms. Early dementia may therefore help cause the prescription, not the other way around.
- **Detection bias.** Someone refilling a prescription monthly is in the healthcare system constantly, so a cognitive complaint is more likely to get noticed and worked up. Someone on no medications with the same early decline may go undiagnosed longer. That undercounts cases in the "unexposed" group.
- **Comparator choice.** Studies comparing PPI users to non-users tend to find bigger effects than studies comparing PPI users to people on an alternative reflux drug. The second comparison is fairer, since both groups needed treatment for something. When researchers use the fairer comparison, the association often shrinks or disappears. That is a strong signal that a chunk of the original 30 to 45% was confounding by indication, not the drug itself.
- **Relative versus absolute risk.** "30% more likely" is a relative figure. Hypothetically, if baseline dementia risk over the study's follow-up window is 8%, a 30% relative bump takes it to about 10.4%. That is a real increase, but nowhere near as dramatic as "30%" sounds on its own.

**The wider picture on PPIs is mixed.** It does not point in one direction. The original German insurance-claims study (2016, JAMA Neurology) found the association. Since then, other groups have found no association once they used tighter designs. These include a large Finnish nationwide study focused specifically on Alzheimer's. A split like that across independent datasets is what you would expect if part of the signal is bias rather than biology. It is not what you would expect from a robust causal effect.

**One mechanism deserves attention on its own merits.** This one is separate from the dementia headlines. Long-term acid suppression can reduce B12 absorption, and B12 deficiency is an established, reversible cause of cognitive symptoms in older adults. It is worth ruling out for her regardless. The amyloid and brain-mechanism angle sometimes cited alongside this is much weaker. It is mostly mouse and cell-culture work and is not established in humans.

**Professional guidance.** No major GI or geriatric society currently recommends stopping PPIs because of the dementia data specifically. The data is not considered consistent or strong enough for that. Most societies do recommend periodically checking whether a long-term patient still needs the drug, and at what dose. The reason is other, better-established risks: fracture, kidney disease, C. diff infection, and B12 and magnesium deficiency. That gives you a productive way to act on this without needing to believe the dementia link is causal.

## What to do

1. **Confirm what she is actually taking.** Find out whether it is a PPI (omeprazole, esomeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole) or an H2 blocker like famotidine. Do this first, because if it is an H2 blocker this specific research may not apply to her.

2. **Do not let her stop on her own.** Long-term PPI use causes rebound acid hypersecretion, so quitting cold can produce a worse flare than the one she started with. Do not scare her into stopping a drug she may need.

3. **Ask her prescriber, at her next visit, to review whether she still needs the drug and at what dose.** Frame it as a routine medication review. It is good practice anyway, because of the better-established risks of long-term use: fracture, kidney disease, C. diff infection, and B12 and magnesium deficiency.

4. **Have a B12 level checked at the same visit.** Long-term acid suppression can reduce B12 absorption, and B12 deficiency is an established, reversible cause of cognitive symptoms in older adults. Ruling it out is worthwhile regardless of the dementia question.

5. **Let her doctor weigh her actual reflux history against a taper.** The history that matters includes erosive esophagitis, Barrett's and prior bleeding. Any change in the drug or dose should be the doctor's decision, made on that history.

6. **Use a quick checklist on the next scary-study headline.**
   - What is the comparator: non-users, or people on an alternative treatment?
   - Is the headline number relative or absolute risk?
   - Does it replicate across independent datasets and designs?
   - Is there a plausible mechanism, and is the evidence for it human or animal and cell?
   - Has any professional society changed its guidance because of it?

   When most of these point weak, the finding is a hypothesis worth tracking, not a verdict.

## Recommendation
Separate three things you're currently treating as one decision, because collapsing them is exactly how these letters go wrong: the factual correction, the acknowledgment of fault, and the claim about what would have happened. They don't deserve equal airtime. The tone is factual and briefly owned, not clinical and not penitential. Structurally: correction and corrected numbers first, one sentence of ownership, what you're checking and fixing going forward, then open for questions.

## Why
Lead with the correction itself, plainly, in the first paragraph, with no hedging: what the formula error was, why it happened, and what the corrected loss figure is. That's what executives actually need and will act on. Over-apologizing doesn't read as more accountable. It shifts the message's center of gravity from the numbers to your feelings, and it quietly puts the burden on the reader to reassure you. A memo that's mostly apology also buries the one thing they urgently need, which is the corrected number.

Don't assert the counterfactual, which is the part to be most careful about. "I made a formula error" and "the office shouldn't have closed" are different claims, and the second one you probably can't back. The original decision likely weighed things beyond this one figure, such as lease terms, strategic footprint, other offices under review, that you may not have full visibility into, and a corrected loss number alone doesn't tell you what the call would have been.

This is exposure, not just tone. The closure meant real layoffs. A written statement asserting the decision rested on bad numbers, paired with speculation that the outcome would have differed, is the kind of sentence that can resurface later if anyone affected ever disputes their termination. That's not a reason to soften the facts, so get them exactly right and say them clearly, but it's a reason to keep speculation out of the written record.

## What to do
Own it in one direct sentence: you found the error, it was yours, you're correcting it. Don't extend that into three paragraphs of remorse.

State the correct numbers clearly and let the executives draw their own conclusion.

Say what you're checking elsewhere (same formula used in other models?) and what changes in your review process.

If someone asks directly whether it would have changed the outcome, "I don't know — that's not just my call to make" is honest, not evasive.

If your company has any legal/HR function, loop them in before this goes to the full executive team, given people's jobs were on the line.

Give whoever owns that relationship a heads-up first. Consider a short verbal heads-up to your manager or the decision-owner before the written version goes to the full executive team — for something this consequential, the first they hear of it shouldn't be a cold email.

## Risks of this plan
Burying the correction under apology, so executives have to dig for the actual number.

Defensive minimizing, such as "small error" or "probably wouldn't have mattered anyway," which reads as covering yourself, especially given the stakes, and erodes trust in your next analysis more than the error itself does.

Asserting the counterfactual as fact when you don't actually know that.

No remediation, which leaves the memo documenting a mistake instead of closing the loop.

Wrong sequencing: the exec team finding out cold, with your manager blindsided alongside them.

## Why

The correction leads because it is what executives actually need and will act on. Over-apologizing doesn't read as more accountable. It shifts the message's center of gravity from the numbers to your feelings, and it quietly puts the burden on the reader to reassure you. A memo that is mostly apology also buries the one thing they urgently need, which is the corrected number. That is why one direct sentence of ownership is enough.

The counterfactual is where the most care is needed. "I made a formula error" and "the office shouldn't have closed" are different claims, and the second one you probably can't back. The original decision likely weighed things beyond this one figure. Examples are lease terms, strategic footprint, and other offices under review. You may not have full visibility into those, and a corrected loss number alone doesn't tell you what the call would have been. So state the correct numbers clearly and let the executives draw their own conclusion.

If someone asks you directly whether it would have changed the outcome, "I don't know — that's not just my call to make" is honest, not evasive.

The exposure point comes from the stakes. The closure meant real layoffs. A written statement asserting the decision rested on bad numbers, paired with speculation that the outcome would have differed, is the kind of sentence that can resurface later if anyone affected ever disputes their termination. That's not a reason to soften the facts. Get them exactly right and say them clearly. It is, however, a reason to keep speculation out of the written record. It is also a reason to loop in any legal or HR function before this goes to the full executive team, given people's jobs were on the line.

Sequencing matters for the same reason. If the exec team finds out cold, your manager is blindsided alongside them. Giving whoever owns that relationship a heads-up first is how you avoid that.

Remediation matters too. Stating what broke without saying what you're checking elsewhere, or what changes in your review process, means the memo just documents a mistake. Saying what you're checking and fixing closes the loop.

- **Burying the correction under apology.** Executives have to dig for the actual number.

- **Asserting the counterfactual as fact.** This means claiming the decision would or wouldn't have changed, when you don't actually know that.

## Recommendation
Your leaning is reasonable, but "reasonable to lean yes" and "ready to sign" are different things. You have the one advantage an outside buyer never gets: you already run operations and already have the crews' trust. Most acquisitions die on operations or culture, and you have already solved that part. What you haven't solved is books, bidding, cash flow, and whether clients are loyal to a brand or to a man. Those are exactly where deals like this actually fail. None of it disqualifies you, but all of it needs answers with numbers, not gut feel, before money moves.

If the numbers hold up and your boss is willing to share the transition risk with you, this is a better bet than almost any outside buyer would get. If he wants full price, all cash, no transition, and no visibility into the books, that is your answer already: walk away.

## Why
Client loyalty is the one variable that decides everything else, so stop guessing and get data. Recurring-contract clients rarely bolt over an ownership change if service holds. Handshake relationships with the owner are the risk.

Books, bidding, and cash flow are fixable, not dealbreakers, but they need fixing before close. Landscaping runs thin and seasonal, so someone needs to watch cash weekly. Underpricing jobs is the quietest way new owners bleed out.

The money mechanics matter more than the sticker price. The question is the debt service coverage ratio once you pay yourself a real market wage, not owner-take-everything cash flow. If it is not comfortably above the loan payment, the price is too high or the loan too big, full stop. "Most of our savings plus a loan" also means zero cushion. The business is seasonal: equipment breaks, and you'll lose a client or two in year one no matter what you do.

Sharing the risk is better than absorbing it all on day one.

## What to do
- Pull the client list and rank it by revenue. Look at the top 10-15 accounts: how long they have been customers, whether they deal with your boss personally or with the crews, and whether they are on recurring seasonal contracts or one-off jobs.
- If he will let you, sit in on renewal conversations for a few months before close. You'll learn more from ten real conversations than from any spreadsheet.
- Line up a bookkeeper or fractional CFO before you sign.
- Shadow your boss on bidding for a full season if you can.
- Put three years of financials and tax returns in front of a CPA who has actually done acquisition reviews, not your regular tax preparer. Have them calculate seller's discretionary earnings, not the number your boss quotes you.
- Check that debt service coverage sits at 1.25-1.5x with room to spare.
- Keep a reserve sitting outside the deal, not inside it.
- Structure the deal to share the risk: a transition period with your boss part-time for 6-12 months, personally introducing you to every major account and reachable for questions; seller financing or an earnout tied to client retention, so if the loyalty turns out to be personal he eats part of that risk with you; and a non-compete, so he's not down the road undercutting you with the relationships he kept.
- Ask your partner directly what worries them, not whether they're okay with it.

## Risks of this plan
These are the walk-away signals the source names:
- Financials he can't or won't produce clean: commingled personal expenses, off-book cash jobs, numbers that don't reconcile to tax returns. You can't price it and can't buy it safely.
- Debt service coverage that only works in a best-case year, with no margin for a slow season or two lost accounts.
- Data, or his own admission, showing top clients are loyal to him personally, and he won't agree to a transition period, seller note, or earnout, meaning he won't share that risk with you.
- Key crew leads signaling they'd leave if he leaves, so you'd be buying a business you can't staff.
- Equipment near end of life with no price adjustment for the capex you'll need in year one or two.
- No cash reserve left outside the business, leaving you one bad season from insolvency on both the loan and your household savings.
- Your partner's nervousness turning out to be about something specific and unresolved, not general jitters about a big decision.

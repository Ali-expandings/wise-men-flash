Your leaning is reasonable, but "reasonable to lean yes" and "ready to sign" are different things. You have the one advantage an outside buyer never gets: you already run operations and already have the crews' trust. Most acquisitions die on operations or culture — you've solved that part already. What you haven't solved — books, bidding, cash flow, and whether clients are loyal to a brand or to a man — is exactly where deals like this actually fail. None of it disqualifies you. All of it needs answers with numbers, not gut feel, before money moves.

**Client loyalty** is the one variable that decides everything else, so stop guessing and get data. Pull the client list, rank by revenue, look at the top 10-15 accounts: how long have they been customers, do they deal with your boss personally or with the crews, are they on recurring seasonal contracts or one-off jobs. Recurring-contract clients rarely bolt over an ownership change if service holds. Handshake relationships with the owner are the risk. If he'll let you sit in on renewal conversations for a few months before close, do it — you'll learn more from ten real conversations than from any spreadsheet.

**Books, bidding, cash flow** are fixable, not dealbreakers, but fix them before close. Line up a bookkeeper or fractional CFO before you sign — landscaping runs thin and seasonal, someone needs to watch cash weekly. Shadow your boss on bidding for a full season if you can; underpricing jobs is the quietest way new owners bleed out. And get three years of financials and tax returns in front of a CPA who's actually done acquisition reviews, not your regular tax preparer — have them calculate seller's discretionary earnings, not the number your boss quotes you.

**The money mechanics** matter more than the sticker price. What's the debt service coverage ratio once you pay yourself a real market wage, not owner-take-everything cash flow? If that's not comfortably above the loan payment — 1.25-1.5x with room to spare — the price is too high or the loan too big, full stop. "Most of our savings plus a loan" also means zero cushion, and this business is seasonal: equipment breaks, you'll lose a client or two in year one no matter what you do. You need a reserve sitting outside the deal, not inside it.

**Structure the deal to share the risk** instead of absorbing it all on day one. Ask for a transition period — your boss part-time for 6-12 months, personally introducing you to every major account and reachable for questions; seller financing or an earnout tied to client retention, so if the loyalty turns out to be personal, he eats part of that risk with you; and a non-compete, so he's not down the road undercutting you with the relationships he kept.

What would make me say walk away:

- Financials he can't or won't produce clean — commingled personal expenses, off-book cash jobs, numbers that don't reconcile to tax returns. Can't price it, can't buy it safely.
- Debt service coverage that only works in a best-case year, with no margin for a slow season or two lost accounts.
- Data (or his own admission) showing top clients are loyal to him personally, and he won't agree to a transition period, seller note, or earnout — meaning he won't share that risk with you.
- Key crew leads signaling they'd leave if he leaves — you'd be buying a business you can't staff.
- Equipment near end of life with no price adjustment for the capex you'll need in year one or two.
- No cash reserve left outside the business — one bad season from insolvency on both the loan and your household savings.

Eight years in makes it tempting to treat "I basically know this business" as a substitute for the diligence an outside buyer would be forced to do. It isn't. Do the homework anyway. If the numbers hold up and your boss is willing to share the transition risk with you, this is a better bet than almost any outside buyer would get.

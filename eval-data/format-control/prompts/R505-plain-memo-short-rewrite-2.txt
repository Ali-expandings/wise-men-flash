Take this seriously enough to raise with her doctor, not seriously enough to act on alone.

First, confirm what she's actually taking. "Common acid-reflux medication" usually means a proton pump inhibitor (PPI) — omeprazole, esomeprazole, pantoprazole, lansoprazole — as opposed to an H2 blocker like famotidine, which is a different drug class with different (thinner) dementia data. On PPIs and dementia: this isn't one study, it's a small research area going back to around 2015, with relative-risk increases across different studies ranging roughly 20–45%. Your "30% after five-plus years" fits that pattern — possibly a large recent US claims-based cohort (several million patients, risk concentrated in the longest-duration users), which tracks with your mother's decade of use. The finding keeps recurring, which is worth noting. Whether it means the drug causes dementia is a separate question, and that's where this kind of study is structurally weak.

Big records-based studies (insurance claims, EHR data — not trials) are good at spotting patterns across millions of people cheaply. They're bad at telling you why the pattern exists, because nobody was randomized to take the drug. People who end up on it for ten years differ from people who don't, in ways that matter:

- **Confounding by indication.** Decade-long PPI users tend to have more severe GERD and more comorbidities generally — anything correlated with worse baseline health can also correlate with dementia risk. Researchers adjust for what they can measure, not for everything that's actually different between the groups.
- **Reverse causation.** Dementia has a prodromal phase lasting years before diagnosis. Early, undiagnosed cognitive decline can change diet, self-care, and GI symptoms — meaning early dementia may help cause the prescription, not the other way around.
- **Detection bias.** Someone refilling a prescription monthly is in the healthcare system constantly, so a cognitive complaint is more likely to get noticed and worked up. Someone on no medications with the same early decline may just go undiagnosed longer, and undercount in the "unexposed" group.
- **Comparator choice.** Studies comparing PPI users to non-users tend to find bigger effects than studies comparing PPI users to people on an alternative reflux drug — a fairer comparison, since both groups needed treatment for something. When researchers use the fairer comparison, the association often shrinks or disappears. That's a strong signal that a chunk of the original 30–45% was confounding by indication, not the drug itself.
- **Relative vs. absolute risk.** "30% more likely" is relative. Hypothetically, if baseline dementia risk over the study's follow-up window is 8%, a 30% relative bump takes it to about 10.4% — a real increase, but nowhere near as dramatic as "30%" sounds on its own.

The fuller picture on PPIs specifically is mixed, not one-directional. The original German insurance-claims study (2016, JAMA Neurology) found the association. Since then, other groups — including a large Finnish nationwide study focused specifically on Alzheimer's — found no association once they used tighter designs. That kind of split, across independent datasets, is what you'd expect if part of the signal is bias rather than biology, not what you'd expect from a robust causal effect. There is a mechanism worth taking seriously on its own merits, separate from the dementia headlines: long-term acid suppression can reduce B12 absorption, and B12 deficiency is an established, reversible cause of cognitive symptoms in older adults — worth ruling out for her regardless. The amyloid/brain-mechanism angle sometimes cited alongside this is much weaker, mostly mouse and cell-culture work, not established in humans.

No major GI or geriatric society currently recommends stopping PPIs because of the dementia data specifically — it's not considered consistent or strong enough for that. Most do recommend periodically checking whether a long-term patient still needs the drug, and at what dose, because of other, better-established risks: fracture, kidney disease, C. diff infection, B12/magnesium deficiency.

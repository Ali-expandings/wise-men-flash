"""
Separate three things you're currently treating as one decision, because collapsing them is exactly how these letters go wrong: the factual correction, the acknowledgment of fault, and the claim about what would have happened. They don't deserve equal airtime.

**Tone: factual and briefly owned, not clinical and not penitential.** Lead with the correction itself — what the formula error was, why it happened, what the corrected loss figure is — plainly, in the first paragraph, no hedging. That's what executives actually need and will act on. Then own it in one direct sentence: you found the error, it was yours, you're correcting it. Don't extend that into three paragraphs of remorse. Over-apologizing doesn't read as more accountable — it shifts the message's center of gravity from the numbers to your feelings, and it quietly puts the burden on the reader to reassure you. A memo that's mostly apology also buries the one thing they urgently need, which is the corrected number.

**Don't assert the counterfactual — that's the part to be most careful about.** "I made a formula error" and "the office shouldn't have closed" are different claims, and the second one you probably can't back. The original decision likely weighed things beyond this one figure — lease terms, strategic footprint, other offices under review — that you may not have full visibility into, and a corrected loss number alone doesn't tell you what the call would have been. State the correct numbers clearly and let the executives draw their own conclusion. If someone asks you directly whether it would have changed the outcome, "I don't know — that's not just my call to make" is honest, not evasive.

**This is exposure, not just tone.** The closure meant real layoffs. A written statement asserting the decision rested on bad numbers, paired with speculation that the outcome would have differed, is the kind of sentence that can resurface later if anyone affected ever disputes their termination. That's not a reason to soften the facts — get them exactly right and say them clearly — but it's a reason to keep speculation out of the written record, and if your company has any legal/HR function, loop them in before this goes to the full executive team, given people's jobs were on the line.

Where these usually go wrong, concretely:

- **Burying the correction under apology** — executives have to dig for the actual number.
- **Defensive minimizing** — "small error," "probably wouldn't have mattered anyway." Reads as covering yourself, especially given the stakes, and erodes trust in your next analysis more than the error itself does.
- **Asserting the counterfactual as fact** — claiming the decision would or wouldn't have changed, when you don't actually know that.
- **No remediation** — stating what broke without saying what you're checking elsewhere (same formula used in other models?) or what changes in your review process. Without that, the memo just documents a mistake instead of closing the loop.
- **Wrong sequencing** — the exec team finding out cold, with your manager blindsided alongside them. Give whoever owns that relationship a heads-up first.

Structurally: correction and corrected numbers first, one sentence of ownership, what you're checking/fixing going forward, then open for questions.

## Recommendation

Do not reject the candidate on the tip, and do not ignore it. Treat it as a lead and keep the source confidential. Before any offer, put every finalist through the same two checks: a written question about past complaints or investigations, and a reference from a former direct report. Decide on what those checks produce.

## Why

You owe the candidate a decision based on facts they can see and answer. "Left mid-investigation" fits a guilty person, an exonerated one, or someone who left because the process was unfair, and you cannot tell which. Rejecting on it alone punishes them on hearsay with no chance to respond. A quiet rejection on a secret rumor is also the kind of record that looks like a pretext if the decision is later challenged.

You owe the future reports real diligence. A team lead's treatment of junior staff lands on people who cannot choose their boss. If you hear a warning and do nothing, "knew or should have known" is the standard negligent-hiring fact pattern, though its details vary by jurisdiction. The glowing references weigh less than they seem. The candidate picked the referees, and an exit during an open investigation is sometimes paired with agreed references. An investigation still open at resignation also tends to close without a finding (unverified — check with HR or counsel for your jurisdiction). So waiting for the outcome is unlikely to work, and the checks below are your realistic way to learn more.

You owe your colleague discretion, not a promise to do nothing. She asked you not to repeat it. That binds you not to name her or relay the claim. It does not require you to behave as if you never heard it. Saying "I heard…" to a panel, to the candidate or to the old workplace also exposes you to defamation risk and exposes her.

What you can do with an unverifiable tip is turn it into something verifiable that does not depend on it. Asking every finalist the same questions keeps the process fair to the candidate and keeps her anonymous, because a question aimed at one person points straight back to her.

## What to do

1. **This week, before any offer, talk to your colleague.** Tell her you will not name her or repeat what she said, and that you will run your normal checks. Ask, without pressing, whether her knowledge is first-hand and whether she can point to someone who saw it directly. Do not promise she can never be involved if this later becomes a formal matter.
2. **Ask HR or counsel to add two items for all finalists, treating the reason as need-to-know.** The first is a written question: "Have you been the subject of a workplace complaint or investigation, or left a role while one was open? If so, what happened?" The second is one reference from a former direct report. Do not name the source and do not write the rumor into the candidate file. Confirm with them what you may ask and record in your jurisdiction.
3. **In the interview,** ask structured behavioral questions about managing underperformers, giving feedback and handling conflict with junior staff. Ask the candidate openly why they left.
4. **Ask the previous employer only what it will confirm,** such as rehire eligibility.
5. **Make any offer conditional on truthful answers.**
6. **Time box: about one week, then decide.**
   - A plain, coherent disclosure plus a direct report describing fair treatment means proceed.
   - A false denial, evasiveness about the departure, or a credible first-hand account of mistreatment means stop and take it to HR or counsel before going further.
   - If the candidate cannot or will not name a former direct report, that is a weak signal, not proof.
   - If the process leaves a real, job-relevant doubt and a runner-up is close, choosing the runner-up on those findings is legitimate. Record only the process-based reasons.

## Risks of this plan

- **The checks can miss it.** A candidate can deny falsely, the previous employer can say nothing, and no direct report may speak. You are then left with the tip or nothing. The checks can also turn into a ritual that clears the candidate by default.
- **Waiting has a price.** The extra week risks losing the candidate to another offer.
- **Asking only this candidate backfires.** It breeds confirmation bias and could expose your colleague.
- **A wrong hire lands on others.** If the candidate was in fact abusive, the harm falls first on junior staff, and you would be exposed for having known.

Cost of being wrong: before an offer, nothing is committed except the delay. A wrongly rejected candidate can be revisited if they are still available. A wrongly hired abusive lead does harm to the reports that is hard to undo. The recovery path is conditional offer terms, a probationary period if your jurisdiction allows one, and an early check-in with the reports. A false denial discovered later is a clean, citable reason to act.

## Strongest counter-position

"Unverifiable does not mean weak. Verification you are waiting for most likely never exists, because these investigations often end with no finding. The glowing references are curated by the candidate and may be negotiated. So you are weighing a disinterested signal, a colleague who takes a social risk and gains nothing, against a curated one. A courtroom standard of proof does not fit a hiring decision, since you already hire on weaker inputs such as gut feel and interview polish. 'I cannot use what I cannot cite' is also the comfortable conclusion: it keeps your top candidate and saves you time, while the cost of being wrong falls on junior staff who were not in the room."

This position wins if her knowledge is first-hand and she has no stake against the candidate. If the checks then come back empty, the tip deserves weight in the decision itself, openly, and not only as a prompt for checks. In that case, recording only process-based reasons would be too timid. Evidence that would show it: a second independent source, or a disclosure answer that conflicts with what she told you.

## Confidence

- **Rejecting on the tip alone is the wrong move:** High.
- **The plan overall:** Medium. It holds across common employment regimes, but what you may ask and record depends on your jurisdiction.
- **The checks will surface the truth:** Low to medium.
- **Material unknowns:** whether she has first-hand knowledge or a grudge, how the investigation ended, your jurisdiction and sector, and why the candidate left.
